Maazel and the Cleveland Orchestra: Contemporary Newspaper Reviews (1974-1982)

2026-10-06

Lorin Maazel served as Music Director of The Cleveland Orchestra from 1972 to 1982, inheriting one of the world's most admired ensembles from the legendarily exacting George Szell. The six reviews below were scraped verbatim from contemporary newspaper archives — the only texts recovered after exhausting five scraping surfaces (Exa, Firecrawl, ScrapeGraph, Tavily, Apify) against every accessible archive. Plain Dealer, Cleveland Press, and ProQuest holdings remain behind institutional paywalls and are not represented here.

February 4, 1974 · Carnegie Hall · Strauss, Elektra (concert version)

Harold C. Schonberg, The New York Times, February 6, 1974

It was easy to see who had captured the heart of the big audience on Monday night in Carnegie Hall. When the singers came out to take their bows after the concert version of Strauss's "Elektra," conducted by Lorin Maazel and the Cleveland Orchestra, it was Astrid Varnay who received the wildest ovation. She had returned, and her return was something of a triumph.

Miss Varnay, who sang Brünnhilde and other Wagnerian heroines with great distinction through the 1950's, more or less retired from the major opera scene in the early 1960's. Then she started coming back, in smaller roles, and audiences of a younger generation who had only heard about her were astonished at the authority, the experience and the sheer size of the voice that still remained.

Her Elektra was not in the first rank of the role; not many are. Her singing is no longer the most refined in the world. She pushes, she wobbles, she uses her voice in a rather free manner. But she has a quality that few other sopranos in the world can match, which is the ability to make the audience believe in what she is doing. Her Elektra was a real woman driven by hate, and everything she did on stage was in character. She moved well and dramatically; she reacted to the other singers on cue; and if there was not room for much in the way of perambulation, there certainly was for basic physical and emotional gestures.

It was an exciting evening. There was, of course, the great Cleveland Orchestra, playing beautifully as always. And there was an overall cast of generally high quality. Maazel conducted with his usual flair and sensitivity to orchestral color. Strauss's "Elektra" is one of his most concentrated scores, and Maazel brought out the colors well, though his tempos tended to be on the slow side.

February 11, 1974 · Carnegie Hall · Mozart Violin Concerto No. 5 & Mahler Symphony No. 7

Donal Henahan, The New York Times, February 13, 1974

Anyone who harbored suspicions that the Cleveland Orchestra might go into serious decline after the death of its musical director, George Szell, must have had those qualms soothed at Carnegie Hall on Monday night. As led by Lorin Maazel, who has now served as its conductor for two seasons, the orchestra sounded very much its old inimitable self: lean and elegant in matters of style, superlative in technical finish.

What the program lacked in novelty — Mozart's Violin Concerto No. 5 in A (the "Turkish"), with Ruggiero Ricci as soloist, and Mahler's Symphony No. 7 — it more than compensated for in quality of performance. The Mahler, in particular, was dispatched in a performance of high distinction. Maazel, who conducted the symphony without a score, demonstrated a firm command of its structural intricacies and a talent for shaping the long melodic lines.

From the opening horn solo, played magnificently by Myron Bloom, this performance had authority and a sense of inevitability. The third and fourth movements, the two Nachtmusik, were played with particular enchantment: the march-like Nachtmusik I with firm rhythmic security and the more lyric Nachtmusik II with strings projecting a rich, chamber-music-like blend. The finale was energetically dispatched and altogether brilliantly played.

February 13, 1974 · Carnegie Hall · Beethoven, Siegmeister Symphony No. 4 & Dvořák Cello Concerto

John Rockwell, The New York Times, February 15, 1974

Elie Siegmeister's Symphony No. 4, heard at its first New York performance Wednesday night at Carnegie Hall with the Cleveland Orchestra conducted by Lorin Maazel, is a long (32 minutes), ambitious, widely accessible work from 1970 that mixes neo-Romanticism with avant-garde elements and arrives at a highly personal blend. Siegmeister has always had an ear for eclecticism, and the result here can charm and beguile even as it puzzles.

The symphony was sandwiched between Beethoven's Overture to "Fidelio" and the Dvořák Cello Concerto, with Lynn Harrell as soloist. Harrell, still in his mid-20's, plays with remarkable beauty of tone, a warm intimacy of style and an impressive technical security. His performance here was assured and often ravishing. Only a sense of spiritual profundity, perhaps still to come as the artist matures, seemed briefly missing in the music's more intense pages. The orchestra accompanied with precision and sympathy under Maazel's direction.

August 31, 1977 · Auditorio Nacional, Mexico City · Beethoven Cycle

Special to The New York Times, September 1, 1977

MEXICO CITY, Aug. 31 — The Cleveland Orchestra, led by its conductor, Lorin Maazel, last night began a twoweek Beethoven cycle here with a soldout concert attended by the wife of Mexico's President. José López Portillo is himself a great admirer of the German master and had arranged the visit of the American orchestra.

Mrs. Carmen Romano de Lopez Portino, herself an accomplished pianist, is particularly fond of Beethoven and is expected to attend several of the 11 concerts.

During its visit, which will include low‐price concerts at the huge National Auditorium, and lectures for music students by Mr. Maazel, the 104‐member orchestra will play the nine Beethoven symphonies, the Third Piano Concerto, the Triple Concerto, the Overtures to "Fidelio," "The Creatures of Prometheus" and "Egmont" and the "Leonore" Overtures Nos. 1, 2 and 3.

The soloists for the Ninth Symphony will be Leona Mitchell, soprano; Oralia Dominguez mezzo‐soprano; Kenneth Riegel, tenor, and Roberto Banuelas, bass. James Dick will play the piano concerto and the Trio Mexico the Triple Concerto. Tickets for the program are already almost impossible to obtain.

October 18, 1981 · Kennedy Center, Washington D.C. · Glazunov & Holst, The Planets

Octavio Roca, The Washington Post, October 19, 1981

Last night at the Concert Hall, the Cleveland Orchestra gave one of those rare concerts when everything seemed right. In works of Glazunov and Holst, Lorin Maazel's sensibilities matched that of the composers, and the orchestra's playing was vigorous and inspired. It was a splendid evening.

"The Planets" by Holst had an extraterrestrial aura and a very human pulse. It is a broad and wonderful work, the musical source of many of Bernard Herrmann's ideas as well as all of John Williams'. Maazel's clear baton and penchant for bombast were certainly at home here, but he also generously summoned up subtlety and beauty.

May 22, 1982 · Carnegie Hall · Verdi, Messa da Requiem — Farewell Concert

Thor Eckert Jr., The Christian Science Monitor, May 26, 1982

Last Saturday evening at Carnegie Hall, Lorin Maazel bade farewell to the Cleveland Orchestra the ensemble he led as music director for the past decade.

The piece chosen for this occasion was Verdi's "Messa da Requiem" (heard earlier in the week in a roof-shattering Avery Fisher Hall), closing out a four-concert cycle that included Dvořák's Seventh and Ninth, Berlioz's "Symphonie Fantastique," Debussy's "Prélude à l'après-midi d'un faune," the two Beethoven symphonies (the "Eroica" and the Fifth), and Saint-Saëns' venerable Second Piano Concerto with the conductor's wife, Israela Margalit, as soloist.

When Maazel took over the podium in 1972, the press attitude was mostly wait-and-see. What he never quite had at the start was the orchestra players' support. Szell had been a stern father figure, but he was an ardent supporter of his musicians, which he proved by constantly battling the board of trustees for them, raising their salaries, fighting for recordings, and building an international reputation for the ensemble.

What he took over in 1972 was one of the special jewels among international orchestras, the ensemble George Szell had honed into what many considered the finest orchestra in the world. During the interim between Dr. Szell's passing and the beginning of Maazel's tenure, Pierre Boulez had maintained a healthy discipline. Happily, Mr. Maazel never let Dr. Szell's standard slip. He leaves to his successor, Christoph von Dohnányi, the best orchestra, overall, in the United States, rivaled only by the Berlin Philharmonic and the Concertgebouw for virtuosity. Above all, there is a distinctiveness of timbre in this faultless ensemble wedded to a startling purity of tone.

The Cleveland has always been a lean, taut orchestra. Not for Szell or Maazel the plushness of Ormandy's Philadelphia or the opulent glossiness of Ozawa's Boston Symphony, or even the near-Prussian military puissance of the Chicago forces. Rather, each section complements the other. The same sheen to be heard in the winds is found in the strings, and in the transparency of the brass.

But Maazel's taste as a musician-interpreter has always been unusual. There has never been any great tenderness or warmth to his work, rather the sense of a presiding intellect coolly, clinically dissecting each work, fussing and fidgeting over details as they pass by, trying to inflict his own will into the composer's framework (the I'm-in-control-here school).

This "inflictive" approach has led to a compendium of bizarre performances throughout his career (not just the Cleveland years). In the account of the "Eroica," the pacing was consistently brisk (of the I've-got-a-plane-to-catch school), with nary a nod to the tensions or the moods of the score. The Berlioz "March to the Scaffold" was a veritable sprint. In Dvořák's "New World," his tempo in the slow movement denied English horn soloist Felix Kraus any chance of sensitive evocation or simply good musicianship. Under Maazel's baton, Debussy's "Prélude" — a perfect study in opulent sonorities — became an exercise in arid, choppily phrased note-processing.

But there have been numerous times when these less-than-admirable qualities have been submerged, held in check, and a cool but beguiling performance shone through — in addition to the Bruckner Eighth, a rafter-raising account of Mahler's Second Symphony ("Resurrection"), just to mention two. In this current run, Dvořák's Seventh Symphony was granted a reading suffused with the required dark mood and tension, and was — with the exception of a facile coda to the finale — a model performance of the work. Beethoven's Fifth passed by with a minimum of oddities and a maximum of brio.

Maazel's Verdi "Requiem" may not have touched the heart, but it had a nobility, a grandeur, and an occasional smattering of theatricality not usually part of Maazel's makeup, which coalesced into a fine sense of event. Throughout, the orchestra played stunningly well — rallying magnificently for this farewell to its chief of 10 years. From the barely audible opening phrase — just a rustle in the cellos — through to the fullest climaxes, moments passed by that will remain etched forever in this listener's inner ear, such as the transcendent transparency of the strings in the closing pages of the "Offertorio." Throughout, balances remained immaculate, transparency unprecedented. The Cleveland Orchestra Chorus performed handsomely, despite a tendency to very open American vowels.

Of the vocal quartet, John Paul Bogart was overparted, his bass being essentially too lyric an instrument for this imposing music; the solid tenor of Peter Dvorsky proved a bit sobby and monochromatic. Dunja Vejzović's lyric mezzo proved intermittently stunning, and in Hildegard Behrens we finally had a soprano who can ride the full orchestral climaxes, yet taper the voice down to effectively quiet proportions (a few crucial flatted top notes aside), and be dramatic and imaginative as well.

Maazel leaves behind a quantity of records. On the London label: the Prokofiev Fifth; the Franck Symphony in D minor; a magnificent account of the Debussy "Nocturnes," "Iberia" and "Jeux"; Ravel's "Daphnis et Chloé" superbly recorded; the complete Gershwin "Porgy and Bess" — a richly operatic reading of the score. On the Telarc label is a Stravinsky "Rite of Spring" that is eccentric as can be, yet so superbly recorded, so utterly representative of the best of this fabulous ensemble, that it should be required listening to all who love great orchestral playing.

Shivaji: Nationalist Memory and the Seventeenth-Century Record

Late-nineteenth and early-twentieth-century nationalists made Shivaji a Hindu hero. The seventeenth-century record supports part of that picture, but not the religious frame around it. He was neither a Hindu-nation warrior nor a secular ruler. Both labels are anachronistic.

The lionization, 1895–1908

Tilak’s campaign began with a newspaper appeal. A Kesari article of 23 April 1895 launched a public subscription to repair Shivaji’s tomb at Raigad, and by 1896 the drive had become a birthday festival. In 1897, when plague intervened, the festival was held on his coronation day instead.1

The 1908 sedition trial is often linked to Shivaji, but it did not rest on a Shivaji article. The charges concerned two Kesari pieces of 12 May and 9 June 1908 about contemporary violence and repression, prosecuted under Indian Penal Code sections 124A and 153A.1

Lala Lajpat Rai published a biography of Shivaji in 1896.37 The line most often quoted from it, that Shivaji “saved the cow and the Brahmin”, reaches us through a UCLA MANAS essay. The original was in Urdu, according to that essay. I have not checked the wording against it.

What the seventeenth-century record shows

Stewart Gordon’s The Marathas 1600–1818 (1993), the standard survey, places the Maratha elite inside the Deccan sultanates. Its origin lies “in the Muslim kingdoms of the Deccan.”2 Shivaji’s state used the same tools. Court proceedings of 1657 list salaried Muslim qazis. His first military unit was 700 Pathans who had left Bijapur. Sidi Ibrahim was one of ten trusted commanders at the 1659 confrontation with Afzal Khan, and Nurkhan Beg was his commander-in-chief then. Gordon concludes there was “little difference” between Shivaji’s polity and the states around it.2

His politics also crossed confessional lines. The Treaty of Purandar (1665) was imposed by Mirza Raja Jai Singh, a Hindu Rajput serving the Mughals, and it left Shivaji a Mughal vassal.42 In the south he took Jinji and besieged Vellore. Thanjavur was won by his half-brother Vyankoji, not by Shivaji.52

The 1674 coronation, dated to June by standard accounts, is the strongest evidence of a Hindu royal idiom. A Benares Brahmin, Gaga Bhatta, declared Shivaji a Kshatriya, and he took the title Chhatrapati.53 Gordon reads the ceremony as mainly about “internal relations in Maharashtra”. The Kshatriya claim was needed to command local deshmukh families, and the question of his lineage delayed the ceremony almost a year.2 Novetzke (2004) argues that Shivaji himself cultivated the Hindu Kshatriya persona through it.8

So the royal idiom was real, but its job was local legitimacy, not a national project.

Religious language was present too. A letter to Aurangzeb protesting the jizya, in the transcription printed in Sarkar’s History of Aurangzib, invokes Akbar’s ideal of universal peace and treats religious difference as the Divine’s design. It is a plea about kingship, not a Hindu-supremacist manifesto. I have not examined the Persian original.6

The sources need caution too. The bakhars, Marathi chronicles written between the late seventeenth and mid-eighteenth centuries, were composed by Brahmin eulogists and are largely hagiographical.2 Gordon also notes that recent evidence shows Shivaji did not meet Ramdas, the main candidate for his guru, until 1672. That undercuts the story of a lifelong spiritual mentor.2

Memory and record

The record supports three things. Shivaji defied Bijapur and the Mughals, built an independent kingdom, and used Hindu royal forms to claim authority. It does not support a Hindu-nation project, a civilisational war with Islam, or a state separate from the Deccan systems it grew out of.

Nationalists after 1895 read the first set of facts as the second. Maharashtra’s state memory went further after 1960, when Shivaji was canonised as the founder of the Marathi nation.3 That is a twentieth-century layer built on the 1890s, and it should be studied as such.

The more useful categories are “Deccani sovereign” and “later memory”, not “secular” or “Hindu nationalist”.

Are Irish people genetically predisposed to alcoholism?

2026-10-03

No, Irish people are not genetically predisposed to alcoholism in a way that sets them apart from other Europeans. Researchers have never found an Irish-specific gene or variant that raises the risk of alcohol problems, and Ireland's struggles with drink are better explained by culture, policy and economic swings than by DNA. About half of anyone's risk comes from inherited differences spread across hundreds of tiny-effect genes that work the same way across Europe. The one honest nuance is that Irish people, like most Europeans, largely lack certain protective alcohol-metabolising variants common in East Asia. That absence leaves average risk a little higher than where the protection is widespread, yet it is shared right across Europe and says nothing distinctive about being Irish.

What genes actually do in problem drinking

The NIAAA explains that genes make up about half of a person's risk of developing alcohol use disorder, with many genes contributing and most making only very small contributions. Twin and family work puts numbers on it, with a large meta-analysis estimating heritability at 0.49 and shared environment at 0.10. Genes matter, though no single gene decides anything.

A review of genome-wide studies sums up the current picture. Genome-wide significant hits for an alcohol use disorder diagnosis have been limited to variants in the alcohol-metabolising genes ADH1B and ADH1C, while hundreds of variants across the genome, most of very small effect, contribute to the genetic side of the disorder. Such scores predict weakly: one large study found people in the top five percent of a polygenic score were about twice as likely to develop alcohol problems, which still leaves most risk unexplained. In the Irish sib-pair study itself, researchers reported that polygenic scores accounted for only zero to one percent of symptom variance. That points away from any one ethnic gene.

The one real genetic difference, and why it is not Irish

Two well-studied protective variants lower the odds of dependence, and their world maps look nothing like the stereotype.

Data aggregated in dbSNP show the protective ALDH2 rs671 A allele at a frequency of only 0.000096 in Europeans, effectively zero, against 0.22506 in East Asians. A review of the variant puts it simply, saying frequency is low in Caucasians while running at 30 to 50 percent in East Asian populations, and a British and Irish study of 1,478 alcohol-dependent cases and 1,254 controls describes the same variant as found in 30 to 50 percent of East Asians but absent in Europeans.

The ADH1B story rhymes. dbSNP figures give the protective T allele at about four percent in Europeans against near seventy percent in East Asians. An Irish sample looks exactly like its neighbours: the Irish sib-pair study found rs1229984 at a minor allele frequency of just 0.006, under one percent, so rare it had to be dropped from standard analysis, while ALDH2 rs671 sat at 0.001 and was dropped too.

Where the rare protective ADH1B copy does appear in Britain or Ireland, it protects strongly. A British and Irish study of 1,076 cases and 1,027 controls tied the minor allele to sharply lower risk, with an odds ratio of 0.26, and a wider meta-analysis in European and African-American samples found a similar shield, with an odds ratio of 0.34. So the biology is genuine, yet its geography argues against Irish distinctiveness. Ireland lacks the shield, but so does all of Europe.

No Irish gene has ever turned up

Scientists have looked for something special in Irish DNA and come back empty handed. An Irish sib-pair report on dopamine-related genes described only modest associations and warned that false positives remained possible. Follow-up work on the candidate gene ZNF699 found no single-marker association with dependence per se once related conditions were handled, while a study of three ALDH1B1 variants in British and Irish participants observed no significant allelic associations with risk.

Large scans without any Irish lens tell the same tale. A meta-analysis combining UK Biobank with 23andMe data, over 140,000 people of European ancestry, identified ten loci and replicated signals in ADH1B, ADH1C, KLB and GCKR, with no Irish-specific locus in sight. That absence is not absolute proof, but after decades of candidate-gene and genome-wide work the reasonable reading is plain. Had an Irish predisposition gene existed with anything like a large effect, researchers would likely have tripped over it by now.

Where the drunken Irish idea really came from

This stereotype is old, political and largely American rather than biological. Reporting on Boston in the 1840s and 1850s describes how the temperance movement merged with the anti-immigration movement to brand the Irish as hopeless drunks, too soaked to work or care for children. Poverty was blamed on the bottle instead of prejudice.

Eugenics then gave the prejudice a lab coat. The eugenicist Charles Davenport presented the Irish as, in the words of a history of his book, mentally deficient drunks tending toward tuberculosis, part of a program to prevent defective humans from reproducing. That was pseudoscience for exclusion, not a finding about chromosomes, though it helped cement the idea that heavy drinking sat in Irish blood.

Mid-century psychiatry briefly seemed to supply harder proof, after American and British research appeared to show Irish emigrants ranking high among alcoholics by country of origin or ethnic group. Irish doctors pushed back early. An account of that dispute recalls how by the 1970s researchers in Ireland were openly challenging the image, with alcohol council director Joseph Adams saying he had spent years trying to explode the myth of the drunken Paddy. Researcher Fitzpatrick showed the Irish in Ireland were drinking less than the average Englishman and argued ethnicity was not a major factor, and the same history notes later work largely backed her, though the cartoon survived.

When Irish science posed the choice directly, culture beat genes. Debate asked whether Irish culture makes the Irishman drink or Irish genes make the American get drunk, and contemporary coverage notes most researchers favoured cultural explanations, seen as less damaging to national identity than talk of genetic defect. Ireland's 1966 Commission on Mental Illness refused to treat high emigrant rates as proof of national predisposition, saying emigrants faced exceptional social pressures conducive to heavy drinking. Uprooting, poverty and hostility can drive anyone to drink.

What Ireland's drinking numbers actually show

Ireland does drink heavily by rich-country standards. OECD country notes put Ireland at 12.9 litres of pure alcohol per person aged fifteen and over each year, roughly 2.6 bottles of wine or five litres of beer every week. The OECD average was only 8.5 litres in 2023, with the heaviest countries clearing 11.5 litres, while the EU average stood at 10.0 litres per adult in 2022. Ireland sits clearly above both.

Binge drinking follows the same pattern. The OECD reports that 37.8 percent of adults in Ireland binge at least monthly, against an average of 27 percent across 27 OECD countries, with Ireland grouped alongside Greece and Sweden at the very top. At home, the Healthy Ireland survey found almost one quarter bingeing on a typical drinking occasion, down from 27 percent in 2018. Treatment demand is mixed: Health Research Board figures show 8,745 cases treated for problem alcohol use in 2024, up seven percent and the highest total in over a decade, though the dependent share fell from 72 to 56 percent. Harm is real and heavy, though its shape is shifting.

Genes do not change in decades, but Irish drinking did

Irish drinking lurched within living memory, which points away from genes and toward economics and policy. The Health Research Board records that consumption increased by 45.9 percent between 1984, at 9.8 litres, and its 2001 peak of 14.3 litres. An analysis of the boom observes that growth brought substantial rises in consumption and in all the main indicators of harm, then stabilisation when the economy turned down. Economic growth drove drinking up, and the downturn steadied it. Whatever drove a near-fifty-percent swing in seventeen years, it was not a mutation sweeping the island.

Cross-country research reaches the same conclusion by a different road. A study in the Journal of Epidemiology and Community Health finds alcohol use deeply rooted in culture and policy strictness linked with lower consumption, since limiting harm has largely meant changing drinking environments. An American review agrees, pointing to the drinking setting and the cultural and economic milieu. Culture and policy do the heavy lifting between nations.

What happened to the Irish abroad

Diaspora evidence, sometimes offered as proof of portable Irish genes, looks messier up close. A study of the 1958 British birth cohort found second-generation Irish men more likely to binge at mid-life, with an odds ratio of 1.45, yet childhood disadvantage partially mediated the link, pointing to social conditions rather than heredity. The signal is modest, so it deserves limited weight, and the evidence here is thinner than for the genetics.

Elsewhere the signal fades. West of Scotland research showed little difference between Scots of Irish descent and other Scots, with gaps tracking religious affiliation rather than ancestry. Within Ireland, a student study tied drinking to fathers' and siblings' habits and to church and settlement patterns, finding little influence of weather. Families seem to pass drinking on substantially through example and norms.

Taken together, the strands pull one way. Heritability is roughly one half for everyone, the protective variants are absent across the whole of Europe including Ireland, no Irish risk gene has survived replication, the stereotype was built by campaigners and eugenicists before 1970s fieldwork undercut it, and consumption swung by nearly half in under two decades as money and policy shifted. Ireland has a genuine alcohol problem that deserves public health seriousness. It does not have an alcohol gene.

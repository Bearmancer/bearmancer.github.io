Sony, Columbia, CBS, RCA, Victor: who owns which label

2026-10-06

Today, Sony Corporation of America, through its recorded music subsidiary Sony Music Entertainment, owns both Columbia Records and RCA Records. These two labels, which spent over half a century as fierce technological and artistic rivals defining American recorded sound, now operate alongside one another as Sony's premier frontline flagships. CBS does not own any part of that music empire anymore; the Columbia Broadcasting System—now part of Paramount Global—is a television network that sold its entire recorded music operation to Sony in 1987–1988, retaining only its broadcast trademarks and network television assets. In the Western hemisphere, the historic RCA Victor name and its celebrated Red Seal classical catalog belong to Sony, but in Japan, the historic Victor trademark and the famous terrier Nipper belong to JVCKenwood under the JVC brand name, operating entirely independent of Sony. In short: Sony is the corporate parent, Columbia and RCA are Sony's premier frontline imprints, CBS is an independent commercial broadcaster that permanently exited recorded music decades ago, and the Victor trademark is partitioned between Sony's Western catalog and an independent Japanese corporation.

Understanding how these five entities arrived at this arrangement requires tracing two parallel dynasties: the line of Columbia and CBS, and the rival line of Victor and RCA. Along the way, their rivalry sparked revolutions in playback technology, triggered transatlantic trademark partitions, bridged American music with Japanese industrial vision, and reshaped listening across the globe, from Camden and Manhattan to Tokyo and Bombay.

The Whole Map: Every Company, Every Country, Every Relationship

The First Dynasty: Columbia from the Volta Laboratory to William Paley

Columbia is the oldest brand name in recorded sound, reaching back to the experimental dawn of acoustic reproduction. In the late 1880s, Alexander Graham Bell, Chichester Bell, and Charles Sumner Tainter established the Volta Laboratory in Washington, D.C., where they patented the wax-coated cardboard cylinder Graphophone as a practical improvement over Thomas Edison's fragile tinfoil phonograph. While Edison indented sound mechanically onto soft foil, Bell and Tainter incised clean, permanent audio grooves into wax. In January 1889, Edward D. Easton incorporated the Columbia Phonograph Company, naming the venture after the District of Columbia, as detailed in the chronicle of Columbia's beginnings from 1888 to 1929. Easton initially attempted to lease Graphophone machines to court reporters and government offices as dictation devices, but he soon discovered that the public far preferred listening to comedic sketches and musical marches through coin-operated listening tubes. Columbia quickly pivoted to commercial entertainment cylinders, pioneering the commercial distribution of pre-recorded music.

As flat disc records began superseding cylinders around the turn of the twentieth century, Columbia adapted by manufacturing lateral-cut discs alongside its cylinder catalog. Over the next two decades, the company endured patent battles, price wars, and shifting financial control, expanding across the Atlantic to establish the British Columbia Graphophone Company in its early history. In 1927, seeking to capitalize on the rapid growth of commercial radio, Columbia backed a fledgling 16-station network named the Columbia Phonographic Broadcasting System (CPBS). The venture proved financially ruinous; within months, mounting operating losses forced Columbia to withdraw its financial backing.

The struggling radio network caught the attention of a young Philadelphia cigar executive named William S. Paley. Paley's family firm, the Congress Cigar Company, had sponsored a radio program on CPBS to advertise its La Palina cigars, producing a dramatic surge in cigar sales. Convinced of radio's commercial power, Paley acquired majority control of the struggling network in 1928, shortened its name to the Columbia Broadcasting System (CBS), and set about transforming it into an entertainment powerhouse, setting up a fateful connection between the network and its musical namesake preserved in the CBS network archives on Columbia Records.

While Paley was turning CBS radio into a lucrative nationwide network, Columbia's American record business was devastated by the onset of the Great Depression. As household record purchases plummeted by more than ninety percent, Columbia's American division collapsed into receivership. In 1934, it was acquired for a nominal sum by the American Record Corporation (ARC), a budget recording conglomerate that relegated the historic Columbia label to a dormant discount imprint. William Paley, however, recognized what other broadcast executives missed: radio broadcast live music into American living rooms for free to sell cigars and soap, but records let listeners own the permanent performance in their homes. In December 1938, Paley paid $700,000 in cash (equivalent to approximately $15.5 million in purchasing power today) to acquire ARC and its crown jewel, initiating the era where CBS takes over Columbia Records. The broadcasting company that had taken its name from a record company in 1927 had returned a decade later to buy the record company itself, inaugurating the golden age of the Columbia and CBS Records corporate dynasty.

The Second Dynasty: Emile Berliner, Francis Barraud, and the Victor Talking Machine Company

While Columbia was perfecting wax cylinders in Washington, a German-born inventor in the same city was charting an entirely different physical path. In 1887, Emile Berliner invented the lateral-cut disc gramophone, as recounted in the record of Emile Berliner's gramophone breakthroughs. Berliner etched audio waveforms onto a zinc plate coated with beeswax, then submerged the disc in an acid bath. The etched metal master could be electroplated into a durable nickel stamper, enabling factories to press thousands of identical, durable shellac discs from a single performance. Berliner's flat disc solved the fundamental manufacturing bottleneck of the cylinder, which required musicians to perform repeatedly or rely on primitive pantographic duplication.

To turn his invention into a practical household instrument, Berliner partnered with Eldridge R. Johnson, a talented machinist who operated a small machine shop in Camden, New Jersey. Johnson engineered a quiet, compact, spring-wound motor with a mechanical governor that maintained a steady rotational speed, banishing the unsteady, wavering pitch that had plagued earlier hand-cranked players. When complex patent litigation and injunctions threatened to paralyze Berliner's commercial operations, Johnson consolidated their combined patent rights, manufacturing facilities, and inventory in 1901 to establish the Victor Talking Machine Company in Camden, launching the acoustical recording era of the Victor Talking Machine Company.

Victor's commercial ascendancy was accelerated by one of the most recognizable trademarks in commercial history. In 1898, English painter Francis Barraud completed a canvas depicting his late brother's mixed-breed terrier, Nipper, resting atop a polished coffin and listening attentively to the brass horn of an Edison-Bell cylinder phonograph. When cylinder manufacturers showed no interest in the painting, the London-based Gramophone Company agreed to purchase the canvas on the condition that Barraud paint out the cylinder machine and replace it with Berliner's brass-horn disc gramophone, establishing the British lineage of His Master's Voice and the Gramophone Company. The original canvas, as documented by the EMI Archive Trust's historical investigation into Barraud's painting, became known worldwide as "His Master's Voice."

Eldridge Johnson secured the North American rights to the artwork, placing Nipper and the brass horn on every Victor phonograph and record label. Johnson reinforced this image of acoustic fidelity by creating Victor's prestigious Red Seal celebrity line, signing opera superstar Enrico Caruso and paying him handsome royalties. Red Seal established the gramophone not merely as a novelty, but as an indispensable instrument of high culture.

In 1929, David Sarnoff, president of the Radio Corporation of America (RCA), engineered the acquisition of the Victor Talking Machine Company for over $150 million (equivalent to approximately $2.8 billion today), marking the culmination of Victor's electrical recording era and acquisition by RCA. Sarnoff, who had established the National Broadcasting Company (NBC) in 1926, paired NBC's national radio network with Victor's Camden manufacturing plants and recording studios. RCA Victor became a formidable entertainment powerhouse, providing the direct corporate counterweight to William Paley's CBS and Columbia.

The Great Transatlantic Trademark Partition and the Birth of Epic

The international expansion of Columbia and Victor gave rise to one of the most intricate trademark knots in commercial history. In 1931, under the financial strain of the worldwide economic depression, the British arm of the Columbia Graphophone Company merged with Britain's Gramophone Company (which marketed records under the "His Master's Voice" label) to establish Electric & Musical Industries (EMI). Because both merging companies contributed their foreign corporate assets, EMI emerged holding the exclusive rights across the United Kingdom, continental Europe, and the British Commonwealth to both the "Columbia" name and the "His Master's Voice" Nipper trademark, as chronicled under Columbia Graphophone as an EMI label.

This British merger created a transatlantic partition. In the United States and Canada, Columbia was owned by William Paley's CBS, and the Nipper trademark was owned by RCA Victor. In Europe and the Commonwealth, however, both Columbia and Nipper belonged exclusively to EMI. For the next two decades, the American giants operated across the Atlantic through long-term reciprocal licensing agreements: American Columbia licensed its classical and popular masters to EMI for release in Europe on the British Columbia label, while RCA Victor licensed its American recordings to EMI for European distribution under the HMV dog-and-gramophone label.

By the early 1950s, this transatlantic dependency became untenable for CBS. Paley and his executives felt that EMI was prioritizing British artists and neglecting American Columbia's emerging pop and classical catalogue. In 1953, CBS terminated its reciprocal distribution pact with EMI, signing an interim distribution agreement with the Dutch electronics giant Philips. Because EMI legally held exclusive rights to the "Columbia" name across Europe and the Commonwealth, CBS was strictly prohibited from selling records under the Columbia name in those territories. To overcome this obstacle, CBS founded Epic Records in 1953 to distribute pop and jazz repertoire abroad, and in 1961 established CBS Records International in its formation and history. Outside North America, American Columbia recordings were packaged in sleeves bearing the CBS name and the iconic "walking eye" logo.

RCA Victor experienced a parallel rupture. In 1957, RCA ended its 56-year distribution alliance with EMI and the Gramophone Company, transferring its European distribution to British Decca before subsequently establishing its own overseas corporate subsidiaries. Because EMI retained exclusive rights to the Nipper dog-and-gramophone trademark across Europe, RCA Victor could not display Nipper on its European sleeves. RCA replaced Nipper with a lightning-bolt emblem and later with modern block letters, cementing the international divorce between the American parent labels and their historic European namesakes.

The Battle of the Speeds: The 33⅓ Microgroove LP Versus the 45 RPM Single

In June 1948, Columbia Records unveiled a technical breakthrough that transformed how the world listened to music, launching the LP record era at Columbia from 1948 to 1959. Led by CBS research director Peter Goldmark, chief engineer William S. Bachman, and René Snepvangers, Columbia's team spent years solving the physics of microgrooves, detailed in the history of the engineering development of the LP. By cutting microgrooves spaced up to three hundred per inch into quiet, flexible Vinylite rather than fragile, abrasive shellac, they achieved up to twenty-three minutes of continuous music per side at 33⅓ revolutions per minute.

At a dramatic demonstration at the Waldorf-Astoria Hotel in Manhattan, as Wired chronicled in its retrospective on the first LP, Goldmark stood beside an eight-foot tower of heavy shellac 78 rpm albums and held up an elegant 15-inch stack of new vinyl discs containing the identical classical repertoire, including Nathan Milstein performing the Mendelssohn Violin Concerto with the New York Philharmonic.

Columbia offered to license its microgroove specifications freely to RCA Victor so the recording industry could unite around a single modern standard. David Sarnoff was deeply affronted that Columbia had beaten his laboratories to market and refused the offer. In February 1949, RCA countered with its own proprietary format, detailed in RCA Records during the post-war 1940s: the 7-inch, 45 rpm vinyl disc featuring a large 1.5-inch center hole. Engineered specifically to work with an ultra-fast, compact turntable changer, the 45 rpm disc held roughly five minutes of music per side. As The Guardian observed on the 60th anniversary of the 45 single, RCA's lightweight, color-coded 7-inch disc was mechanically ideal for pop singles, jukebox operators, and radio disc jockeys.

The resulting "Battle of the Speeds" raged for two years, confusing consumers and squeezing retailers who were forced to stock identical releases across three competing formats (78, 33⅓, and 45 rpm). Market realities eventually forced a pragmatic resolution, as recounted in the chronicle of competing recording formats. RCA Victor acknowledged that complete symphonies, concertos, operas, and Broadway musicals could not be enjoyed in five-minute fragments, adopting Columbia's 33⅓ LP for its Red Seal classical catalogue in 1950. Columbia, in turn, recognized the mechanical convenience of RCA's 7-inch single for popular hit tunes, pressing 45 rpm singles for jukeboxes and radio stations starting in 1951. The rivalry produced an enduring division of labor: the 33⅓ LP became the universal medium for full-length albums, while the 45 rpm single became the global currency of popular hit music.

The Japanese Bridge: CBS/Sony and Norio Ohga (1968–1988)

While CBS and RCA dominated the Western hemisphere, an alliance in post-war Japan paved the way for the modern recording industry. In March 1968, CBS and Tokyo-based consumer electronics firm Sony established a 50/50 joint venture in Tokyo, tracing the beginnings of Sony Music Japan as a CBS joint venture. Sony co-founder Akio Morita selected Norio Ohga—a classically trained baritone who had studied opera at Tokyo National University of Fine Arts and Music and the Berlin Hochschule für Musik before joining Sony as an acoustic designer—to lead the record venture.

Ohga brought an artist's ear and an engineer's rigor to the record business. Under his direction, CBS/Sony constructed the most advanced automated vinyl pressing plants in the world, establishing benchmark standards for silent vinyl surfaces and meticulous audio engineering. CBS/Sony quickly surged to become the top-grossing record label in Japan, reinforcing the international expansion of the CBS Records Group from 1971 to 1991.

Norio Ohga's dual identity as a classical musician and corporate executive proved decisive in the birth of digital audio. Collaborating with Philips in the late 1970s to develop the Compact Disc, Ohga insisted that the optical disc have a 12-centimeter diameter and a digital capacity of 74 minutes. Ohga's specific artistic criterion was that a listener must be able to experience Ludwig van Beethoven's Ninth Symphony—specifically Wilhelm Furtwängler's 1951 Bayreuth Festival performance—in its entirety without having to interrupt the performance to flip a disc. On October 1, 1982, CBS/Sony pressed the first commercial CD in history—Billy Joel's 52nd Street (catalog 35DP-1)—initiating the digital revolution that would transform music revenues and establish Sony as the global pioneer of optical recording.

1987–1988: Laurence Tisch Liquidates CBS Records to Sony

By the mid-1980s, corporate investor Laurence Tisch had acquired controlling influence over CBS Inc. Tisch, who managed the Loews hotel, tobacco, and insurance conglomerate, took a skeptical view of the music division. He had little sympathy for the free-spending, unpredictable creative culture of recorded music and clashed bitterly with CBS Records president Walter Yetnikoff. Tisch regarded the record company as an expendable, cyclical asset whose sale could bolster CBS's broadcast cash reserves and eliminate corporate debt, setting up the sale of Columbia Records to Sony in the 1980s.

Norio Ohga, by then president of Sony, and chairman Akio Morita recognized a once-in-a-century strategic opening. Sony manufactured the world's premier audio hardware—Trinitron monitors, Walkmans, home audio systems, and Compact Disc players—but lacked ownership of the creative content that gave hardware its cultural value. Owning the world's largest music catalogue would ensure that Sony hardware never suffered from a software deficit.

In November 1987, after grueling negotiations, Sony agreed to acquire the entire CBS Records Group, including domestic imprints and the entire overseas operation documented in the sale of CBS Records International to Sony. Sony paid $2.0 billion in cash (approximately ¥265 billion at the time, or over $5.3 billion in purchasing power today), as reported by the Los Angeles Times on Sony's $2 billion acquisition of CBS Records. It marked the largest Japanese acquisition of an American cultural asset to that date. When the transaction closed on January 5, 1988, Sony took possession of Columbia Records, Epic Records, CBS Masterworks, and an irreplaceable vault of master recordings including Bob Dylan, Bruce Springsteen, Michael Jackson, Miles Davis, and the entire Columbia classical legacy. Laurence Tisch retained the CBS broadcasting network, which was later absorbed by Westinghouse, Viacom, and Paramount Global, leaving the broadcaster permanently separated from its historic recording division.

1990–1991: Buying Back the Columbia Trademark and Founding Sony Classical

Under the terms of the 1987 sale agreement, CBS granted Sony a temporary license to use the "CBS" trademark for only three years, expiring on January 1, 1991. Norio Ohga had no intention of operating his music empire under a borrowed corporate name or being constrained by licensing limitations. On January 1, 1991, the division was rechristened Sony Music Entertainment during its 1991 to 2004 era.

As reported by the Los Angeles Times on CBS Records renaming itself Sony Music, the domestic frontline labels—Columbia and Epic—retained their identities, but the parent corporation adopted the Sony name.

Simultaneously, Sony resolved the sixty-year transatlantic trademark partition. In 1990, Sony paid Britain's Thorn EMI approximately £8 million (around $15 million USD at the time, or roughly $35 million today) to repurchase international rights to the historic "Columbia" trademark for recordings outside Japan and Spain, documented in the EMI trademark transfer of the Columbia label. This historic transaction ended the partition that had divided American Columbia from European Columbia since 1931. With the Columbia mark restored to its control, Sony retired the CBS label name internationally and rolled out Columbia Records worldwide.

Norio Ohga also directed a complete overhaul of the premier classical division. The venerable Columbia Masterworks imprint—which CBS had renamed CBS Masterworks in 1980—was reorganized into Sony Classical in 1990. Ohga personally appointed veteran Deutsche Grammophon executive Günther Breest to helm the imprint, as recorded in the history of Sony Classical Records and its presidents, signing premier artists and launching high-resolution archival transfers of the Columbia Masterworks catalog. Broadcaster CBS later attempted a brief return to music: in 2006, as detailed by CNBC on CBS reviving its namesake music label, CBS launched a small boutique imprint for television soundtracks, but it possessed no connection to the historic catalogue it had sold to Sony two decades earlier.

RCA's Odyssey: From GE to Bertelsmann (BMG) and Reunion Under Sony

While Columbia traveled via CBS into Sony's hands, RCA followed an entirely separate corporate journey. In 1986, General Electric acquired RCA Corporation for $6.28 billion (equivalent to over $18 billion today), primarily coveting the NBC television network. GE chairman Jack Welch immediately began liquidating RCA's non-broadcast divisions, viewing recorded music as outside GE's core industrial focus. In late 1986, GE agreed to sell RCA Records to the German media conglomerate Bertelsmann for $330 million (approximately 670 million Deutsche Marks at the time, or roughly $950 million today), detailed under RCA Records during the 1980s.

Bertelsmann merged RCA Records with its own Ariola imprint into the Bertelsmann Music Group (BMG), as chronicled in the history of the Bertelsmann Music Group. For eighteen years, Columbia and RCA Victor operated as fierce rivals under Japanese and German ownership. Then, in August 2004, Sony Music and Bertelsmann combined their recorded music businesses into an equal 50/50 joint venture, creating Sony BMG from 2004 to 2008. For the first time since their founding at the turn of the twentieth century, Columbia and RCA Victor were housed under a single corporate roof, as detailed in the corporate history of Sony BMG.

The joint venture was plagued by operational frictions, executive clashes, and changing digital market dynamics. In August 2008, Bertelsmann agreed to exit recorded music entirely. Sony bought out Bertelsmann's 50% equity stake for $1.2 billion in cash (approximately ¥130 billion or €800 million at the time, or roughly $1.75 billion today), inaugurating the Sony Music restructuring from 2008 to the present. As reported by Billboard on Bertelsmann's exit from Sony BMG, Sony restored the enterprise as the wholly owned Sony Music Entertainment. Bertelsmann subsequently rebuilt a separate music company focused on publishing and master rights, as reflected in Bertelsmann's modern announcements regarding the BBR Music Group.

The Victor name, however, remained divided. Victor's Japanese subsidiary, established in 1927, had become the Victor Company of Japan (JVC), as recounted in JVC's creation from 1927 to World War II. Ties were severed during the war, and JVC retained the "Victor" trademark and the Nipper emblem exclusively within Japan. Today, JVCKenwood's entertainment division operates Victor Entertainment in its corporate history, maintaining trademark coexistence detailed under JVC, Victor Entertainment, and JVCKenwood in Japan. Everywhere outside Japan, RCA Records operates as a premier frontline label of Sony Music across the United States and international markets.

The Classical and Artist Legacies

The reunion of Columbia and RCA Victor under Sony Music created an extraordinary archival phenomenon. For decades, classical music enthusiasts were divided into rival camps, following legendary artists who were locked into exclusive contracts with either Columbia Masterworks or RCA Victor Red Seal. Today, those legendary recordings reside within the single archival catalogue of Sony Classical.

Eugene Ormandy and the Philadelphia Orchestra offer a striking illustration of this corporate convergence. Ormandy began his major recording career with RCA Victor in 1934, leading the Minneapolis Symphony and later the Philadelphia Orchestra, as documented in the catalog of Eugene Ormandy's recordings. In 1944, William Paley lured Ormandy and the Philadelphia Orchestra to Columbia Masterworks, inaugurating a legendary 24-year exclusive association that produced hundreds of definitive recordings of the romantic and modern orchestral repertoire. In 1968, Ormandy dramatically defected back to RCA Red Seal, recording prolifically in Camden and Philadelphia until 1981. For decades, hearing Ormandy's complete recorded output required navigating the catalogues of two warring corporate giants; today, Sony Classical curates and reissues both halves of Ormandy's discography.

Leonard Bernstein's recording history followed a parallel trajectory across the label landscape, detailed in the survey of Leonard Bernstein's audio recordings. Bernstein made his earliest 78 rpm recordings for RCA Victor between 1945 and 1950. In 1956, Bernstein signed an exclusive contract with Columbia Masterworks, recording over five hundred compositions with the New York Philharmonic over a 23-year tenure, as detailed on the official Leonard Bernstein Columbia Records discography. In the 1970s, Bernstein developed an expansive European concert relationship with Deutsche Grammophon, as recorded on the official Leonard Bernstein Deutsche Grammophon archive, eventually recording exclusively for DG from 1981 until his death in 1990. While Bernstein's late DG catalogue resides today with Universal Music Group, his monumental American Columbia archive remains the cornerstone of Sony Classical.

Vladimir Horowitz also crossed these dynastic battle lines throughout his career, captured in the discography of Vladimir Horowitz's recordings. Horowitz made his American debut recordings for Victor Red Seal in 1928, maintaining an exclusive relationship with RCA until 1962. He then signed with Columbia Masterworks to document his legendary 1965 "Historic Return" recital at Carnegie Hall. In 1975, Horowitz returned to RCA Red Seal, and in his final years recorded for Deutsche Grammophon and Sony Classical. Today, the vast majority of Horowitz's legendary American performances are unified under Sony.

Glenn Gould, by contrast, remained an uncompromising Columbia Masterworks loyalist, chronicled in the archive of Glenn Gould's recordings. The eccentric Canadian pianist signed with Columbia in 1955. His debut recording of J.S. Bach's Goldberg Variations captured international acclaim, and his 1981 digital re-recording of the same work bookended his career. Both landmark recordings, along with Gould's entire studio output, are preserved and continuously reissued by Sony Classical.

These individual artists were part of broader institutional rivalries. Arturo Toscanini and the NBC Symphony Orchestra on RCA Victor Red Seal stood in direct opposition to Bruno Walter and George Szell (with the Cleveland Orchestra) on Columbia Masterworks; violinist Jascha Heifetz on RCA Red Seal competed against Isaac Stern and Zino Francescatti on Columbia. Every one of these historic rivalries is now preserved within the single archival catalogue of Sony Classical.

The Global and South Asian Dimension

The corporate rivalries between Columbia, Victor, and EMI were never confined to Western markets; they profoundly shaped the recorded music industry of South Asia. Under the British Empire, EMI established its dominance across the subcontinent through The Gramophone Company of India, universally known as "His Master's Voice" (HMV). In 1901, the company set up Asia's first record factory at Dum Dum near Calcutta. For eight decades, HMV operated an effective monopoly over the subcontinent's recorded sound, pressing everything from early cylinder transfers of Gauhar Jaan and Rabindranath Tagore to the golden era of Hindi cinema playback legends, including Lata Mangeshkar, Mohammed Rafi, and Kishore Kumar.

In the early 1980s, American CBS resolved to challenge HMV's domestic monopoly. Partnering with the Tata-affiliated industrial group, CBS incorporated CBS Gramophone Records & Tapes (India) Ltd in 1982, establishing its headquarters in Dalamal Tower at Nariman Point, Bombay. As reported by India Today's 1982 economic profile of CBS's Indian debut, the company invested heavily in modern cassette duplication technology to undercut HMV's pricing and combat audio piracy, introducing international pop, jazz, and Indian devotional music into the market.

Following Sony's global acquisition of CBS Records, Sony Music made a historic move into India. In 1996, Sony Music India was established as the first 100% foreign-owned record company permitted by the Indian government. The new venture revolutionized the Indian music industry by signing composer A.R. Rahman and releasing his iconic 1997 album Vande Mataram to commemorate the 50th anniversary of Indian independence. The release set new production and marketing standards, cementing Sony Music India as a driving force in Bollywood, Indipop, and regional music.

Sony, Columbia, CBS, RCA, Victor: who owns which label

2026-10-05

To untangle the five names—Sony, Columbia, CBS, RCA, and Victor—it helps to see that what looks like a confusing thicket of corporate record imprints is actually the convergence of two fierce twentieth-century media dynasties into a single modern entertainment empire, with a few crucial pieces left outside. Today, Sony Corporation of America, through its recorded music subsidiary Sony Music Entertainment, owns both Columbia Records and RCA Records. These two labels, which spent nearly six decades as bitter corporate and aesthetic rivals defining American recorded music, now operate alongside one another as Sony's premier frontline flagships. CBS does not own any part of that recording empire anymore; CBS Corporation—now part of Paramount Global—is a broadcasting network that sold its entire recorded music operation to Sony in 1987–1988, retaining only its broadcast trademarks and network television assets. Victor, meanwhile, is split by geography: in the Western hemisphere, the historic RCA Victor name and its Red Seal classical catalogue belong to Sony, but in Japan, the Victor trademark and the famous terrier Nipper belong to JVCKenwood, completely separate from Sony. In short: Sony is the parent owner, Columbia and RCA are Sony's premier record labels, CBS is an independent television broadcaster that exited music decades ago, and Victor is partitioned between Sony's Western reissues and an independent Japanese corporation.

Understanding how these five entities arrived at this arrangement requires tracing two parallel histories: the dynastic line of Columbia and CBS, and the rival line of Victor and RCA. Along the way, their rivalry sparked revolutions in playback technology, triggered transatlantic trademark partitions, bridged American music with Japanese industrial vision, and reshaped listening across the globe, from Camden and Manhattan to Tokyo and Bombay.

The First Dynasty: Columbia from Volta Laboratory to William Paley

Columbia is the oldest brand name in recorded sound, with roots tracing back to the earliest experiments in acoustic reproduction. In the late 1880s, Alexander Graham Bell, Chichester Bell, and Charles Sumner Tainter established the Volta Laboratory in Washington, D.C., where they patented the wax-coated cylinder graphophone as a practical improvement over Thomas Edison's fragile tinfoil phonograph. In January 1889, Edward D. Easton incorporated the Columbia Phonograph Company, naming the venture after the District of Columbia. The fledgling business initially sought to lease graphophone machines to court stenographers and commercial offices as dictation tools, but Easton soon recognized that the public was far more interested in hearing musical recordings and comedic sketches through coin-operated listening tubes. Columbia shifted its focus toward commercial entertainment cylinders, pioneering the mass distribution of pre-recorded music.

As the disc record gained prominence in the early twentieth century, Columbia adapted by issuing flat disc records alongside its cylinders. Over the next two decades, the company endured fierce price wars, corporate reorganizations, and shifting ownership, leading to the formation of the Columbia Graphophone Company in the United Kingdom and its affiliated American operations. In 1927, seeking to capitalize on the rapid rise of commercial radio, Columbia backed a fledgling 16-station radio venture named the Columbia Phonographic Broadcasting System (CPBS). The venture proved financially ruinous; within months, mounting operating losses forced Columbia to withdraw its financial backing.

The struggling radio network caught the attention of a young, ambitious Philadelphia cigar executive named William S. Paley. Paley's family firm, the Congress Cigar Company, had sponsored a radio program on CPBS to advertise its La Palina cigars, producing a dramatic surge in cigar sales. Convinced of the medium's commercial power, Paley acquired majority control of the struggling network in 1928, shortened its name to the Columbia Broadcasting System (CBS), and set about transforming it into a formidable national broadcast rival to David Sarnoff's NBC.

While Paley was turning CBS radio into a lucrative nationwide network, Columbia's American record business was devastated by the onset of the Great Depression. As household record purchases plummeted by more than ninety percent, Columbia's American division collapsed into receivership. In 1934, it was acquired for a nominal sum by the American Record Corporation (ARC), a budget recording conglomerate that relegated the historic Columbia label to a dormant discount imprint. William Paley, however, recognized that a commercial broadcasting network and a premier record company possessed natural synergies: radio networks discovered and promoted musical celebrities, while record labels monetized their performances and built permanent musical catalogues. In December 1938, CBS purchased ARC and its crown jewel Columbia Records for $700,000 (roughly ₹23 lakh at the 1938 exchange rate; equivalent to over $15 million or ₹125 crore today). The broadcasting company that had taken its name from a record company in 1927 had returned a decade later to buy the record company itself, uniting CBS and Columbia Records under one corporate roof.

The Second Dynasty: Emile Berliner, Francis Barraud, and the Victor Talking Machine Company

While Columbia was perfecting wax cylinders, a German-born inventor in Washington, D.C., named Emile Berliner was inventing the modern disc gramophone. In 1887, Berliner patented a system that etched lateral sound vibrations onto a flat zinc disc coated with beeswax, which could then be electroplated into a durable metal stamper capable of pressing thousands of identical discs in hard vulcanite or shellac. Berliner's flat disc solved the fundamental manufacturing bottleneck of the cylinder, which required musicians to record repeatedly or rely on primitive pantographic duplication.

To turn his invention into a practical consumer device, Berliner partnered with Eldridge R. Johnson, a talented machinist who operated a small machine shop in Camden, New Jersey. Johnson engineered a smooth, compact, spring-wound motor with a mechanical governor that maintained a steady rotational speed, eliminating the unsteady, wavering pitch that had plagued earlier hand-cranked players. When complex patent litigation and injunctions threatened to paralyze Berliner's commercial operations, Johnson consolidated their combined patent rights, manufacturing facilities, and inventory in 1901 to establish the Victor Talking Machine Company in Camden.

Victor's commercial ascendancy was accelerated by one of the most recognizable corporate trademarks in history. In 1898, English painter Francis Barraud had completed a canvas depicting his late brother's mixed-breed terrier, Nipper, resting atop a polished mahogany coffin and listening attentively to the horn of an Edison-Bell cylinder phonograph. When cylinder manufacturers showed no interest in the painting, the London-based Gramophone Company agreed to purchase the canvas on the condition that Barraud paint out the cylinder machine and replace it with Berliner's brass-horn disc gramophone. The EMI Archive Trust preserves the history of Francis Barraud's painting, titled "His Master's Voice." Eldridge Johnson secured the North American rights to the artwork, placing Nipper and the brass horn on every Victor phonograph and record label. Johnson reinforced this image of acoustic fidelity by creating Victor's prestigious Red Seal celebrity line, signing opera superstar Enrico Caruso and paying him handsome royalties. Red Seal established the gramophone not merely as a novelty, but as an indispensable instrument of high culture.

In 1929, David Sarnoff, the visionary president of the Radio Corporation of America (RCA), engineered the acquisition of the Victor Talking Machine Company for over $150 million, creating RCA Victor. Sarnoff, who had established the National Broadcasting Company (NBC) in 1926, paired NBC's national radio network with Victor's Camden manufacturing plants and recording studios. RCA Victor became a vertically integrated colossus, providing the direct corporate counterweight to William Paley's CBS and Columbia.

The Great Transatlantic Trademark Partition and the Birth of Epic

The international expansion of Columbia and Victor gave rise to one of the most intricate trademark knots in commercial history. In 1931, under the financial strain of the worldwide economic depression, the British arm of the Columbia Graphophone Company merged with Britain's Gramophone Company (which marketed records under the "His Master's Voice" label) to establish Electric & Musical Industries (EMI). Because both merging companies contributed their foreign corporate assets, EMI emerged holding the exclusive rights across the United Kingdom, continental Europe, and the British Commonwealth to both the "Columbia" name and the "His Master's Voice" Nipper trademark.

This British merger created a stark geographical partition. In the United States and Canada, Columbia was owned by William Paley's CBS, and the Nipper trademark was owned by RCA Victor. In Europe and the Commonwealth, however, both Columbia and Nipper belonged exclusively to EMI. For the next two decades, the American giants operated across the Atlantic through long-term reciprocal licensing agreements: American Columbia licensed its classical and popular masters to EMI for release in Europe on the British Columbia label, while RCA Victor licensed its American recordings to EMI for European distribution under the HMV dog-and-gramophone label.

By the early 1950s, this transatlantic dependency became unbearable for CBS. Paley and his executives felt that EMI was prioritizing British artists and neglecting American Columbia's emerging pop and classical catalogue. In 1953, CBS terminated its reciprocal distribution pact with EMI, signing an interim distribution agreement with the Dutch electronics giant Philips. Because EMI legally held the exclusive rights to the "Columbia" name across Europe and the Commonwealth, CBS was strictly prohibited from selling records under the Columbia name in those territories. To overcome this obstacle, CBS founded Epic Records in 1953 to distribute pop and jazz repertoire abroad, and in 1961–1962 established CBS Records International. Outside North America, American Columbia recordings were packaged in sleeves bearing the CBS name and the iconic "walking eye" logo.

RCA Victor experienced a parallel rupture. In 1957, RCA ended its 56-year distribution alliance with EMI and the Gramophone Company, transferring its European distribution to British Decca before subsequently establishing its own overseas corporate subsidiaries. Because EMI retained exclusive rights to the Nipper dog-and-gramophone trademark across Europe, RCA Victor could not display Nipper on its European sleeves. RCA replaced Nipper with a lightning-bolt emblem and later with the modern block RCA logo, cementing the international divorce between the American parent labels and their historic European namesakes.

The Battle of the Speeds: The 33⅓ Microgroove LP Versus the 45 RPM Single

In June 1948, Columbia Records dealt a historic technological blow to RCA Victor. At a press conference held at the Waldorf-Astoria Hotel in New York City, CBS research director Peter Goldmark and Columbia engineer William S. Bachman introduced the 33⅓ rpm microgroove Long Playing (LP) record. Pressed on quiet, flexible vinylite rather than fragile, noisy shellac, the 12-inch LP utilized microgrooves spaced up to three hundred per inch, allowing up to twenty-three minutes of continuous music per side. As Wired recounted on the milestone anniversary, Goldmark dramatically staged the breakthrough by standing beside an eight-foot-tall tower of heavy shellac 78 rpm albums, holding in his hands an elegant, 15-inch stack of new vinyl LPs containing the identical classical repertoire, including Nathan Milstein performing the Mendelssohn Violin Concerto with the New York Philharmonic.

Columbia offered to license the microgroove technology to the entire recording industry, inviting RCA Victor to adopt the 33⅓ format as the industry standard. David Sarnoff was deeply affronted that Columbia had stolen the technological march on his engineers. RCA refused the offer and in February 1949 unveiled its own proprietary format: the 7-inch, 45 rpm vinyl single featuring a large 1.5-inch center hole. Engineered specifically to work with an ultra-fast, compact turntable changer, the 45 rpm disc could hold roughly five minutes of music per side. As The Guardian noted in its retrospective on the 45 single, RCA's lightweight, color-coded 7-inch disc was mechanically ideal for pop singles, jukebox operators, and radio disc jockeys.

The resulting "Battle of the Speeds" raged for two years, confusing consumers and threatening retail record merchants who were forced to stock identical releases across three competing formats (78, 33⅓, and 45 rpm). By 1950–1951, practical market realities forced a sensible armistice. RCA Victor acknowledged that complete symphonies, concertos, operas, and Broadway musicals could not be enjoyed in five-minute fragments, and capitulated by adopting Columbia's 33⅓ LP for its classical Red Seal catalogue. Columbia, in turn, recognized the mechanical superiority and commercial convenience of RCA's 7-inch single for popular hit tunes, and began pressing 45 rpm singles for jukeboxes and radio stations. The rivalry produced an enduring division of labor: the 33⅓ LP became the universal medium for full-length albums, while the 45 rpm single became the global currency of popular hit music.

The Japanese Bridge: CBS/Sony and Norio Ohga (1968–1988)

While CBS and RCA dominated the Western hemisphere, an alliance in post-war Japan paved the way for the modern recording industry. In March 1968, CBS and the rapidly growing Japanese consumer electronics firm Sony established a 50/50 joint venture in Tokyo: CBS/Sony Records Inc. Sony co-founder Akio Morita selected Norio Ohga—a classically trained baritone who had studied opera at Tokyo National University of Fine Arts and Music and the Berlin Hochschule für Musik before joining Sony as an acoustic designer—to lead the record venture.

Ohga brought an artist's ear and an engineer's rigor to the record business. Under his direction, CBS/Sony constructed the most advanced automated vinyl pressing plants in the world, establishing benchmark standards for silent vinyl surfaces and meticulous audio engineering. CBS/Sony quickly became the top-grossing record label in Japan, proving that high audio fidelity and aggressive marketing could generate substantial corporate profits.

Norio Ohga's dual identity as a classical musician and corporate executive proved decisive in the birth of digital audio. Collaborating with Philips in the late 1970s to develop the Compact Disc, Ohga insisted that the optical disc have a 12-centimeter diameter and a digital capacity of 74 minutes. Ohga's specific artistic criterion was that a listener must be able to experience Ludwig van Beethoven's Ninth Symphony in its entirety without having to interrupt the performance to flip a disc. In October 1982, CBS/Sony pressed the first commercial CD in history—Billy Joel's 52nd Street—initiating the digital revolution that would transform corporate music revenues and establish Sony as the global pioneer of optical recording.

1987–1988: Laurence Tisch Liquidates CBS Records to Sony

By the mid-1980s, corporate raider Laurence Tisch had acquired controlling influence over CBS Inc. Tisch, a conservative financier who managed the Loews hotel and tobacco conglomerate, took a strictly spreadsheet view of the network. He had little sympathy for the free-spending, unpredictable creative culture of recorded music, and clashed bitterly with CBS Records president Walter Yetnikoff. Tisch regarded the record company as an expendable asset whose sale could bolster CBS's broadcast cash reserves and eliminate corporate debt.

Norio Ohga, by then president of Sony, and chairman Akio Morita recognized a once-in-a-century strategic opening. Sony manufactured the world's premier audio hardware—Trinitron monitors, Walkmans, home audio systems, and Compact Disc players—but lacked ownership of the proprietary creative content that gave hardware value. Owning the world's largest music catalogue would ensure that Sony hardware never suffered from a software deficit.

In November 1987, after grueling negotiations, Sony agreed to purchase the entire CBS Records Group for $2.0 billion in cash (approximately ₹2,600 crore in 1988; equivalent to over ₹16,000 crore today). As the Los Angeles Times reported when the deal was struck, it marked the largest Japanese acquisition of an American cultural asset to that date. When the transaction closed on January 5, 1988, Sony took possession of Columbia Records, Epic Records, CBS Masterworks, and an irreplaceable vault of master recordings including Bob Dylan, Bruce Springsteen, Michael Jackson, Miles Davis, and the entire Columbia classical legacy. Laurence Tisch retained the CBS broadcasting network, which was later absorbed by Westinghouse, Viacom, and Paramount Global, leaving the broadcaster permanently separated from its historic recording division.

1990–1991: Buying Back the Columbia Trademark and Founding Sony Classical

Under the terms of the 1987 sale agreement, CBS granted Sony a temporary license to use the "CBS" trademark for only three years, expiring on January 1, 1991. Norio Ohga had no intention of operating his music empire under a borrowed corporate name or being constrained by licensing limitations.

In October 1990, as reported by the Los Angeles Times covering the corporate transformation, CBS Records announced that it would change its name to Sony Music Entertainment on January 1, 1991. The domestic frontline labels—Columbia and Epic—retained their identities, but the parent corporation adopted the Sony name.

Simultaneously, Sony executed a brilliant legal and commercial coup: it reached a definitive agreement with Britain's Thorn EMI to buy back the worldwide rights to the historic "Columbia" trademark for recordings outside of Japan and Spain. This historic transaction ended the sixty-year transatlantic partition that had divided American Columbia from European Columbia since 1931. With the Columbia mark restored to its control, Sony retired the CBS label name internationally and rolled out Columbia Records worldwide.

Norio Ohga also directed a complete overhaul of the premier classical division. The venerable Columbia Masterworks imprint—which CBS had renamed CBS Masterworks in 1980—was reorganized into Sony Classical Records in 1990. Ohga personally appointed veteran Deutsche Grammophon executive Günther Breest to helm Sony Classical, signing premier artists and launching high-resolution archival transfers of the Columbia Masterworks archives. Broadcaster CBS later attempted a brief return to music: in 2006, as detailed by CNBC on CBS reviving its namesake music label, CBS launched a small boutique imprint for television soundtracks, but it possessed no connection to the historic catalogue it had sold to Sony two decades earlier.

RCA's Odyssey: From GE to Bertelsmann (BMG) and Reunion Under Sony

While Columbia traveled via CBS into Sony's hands, RCA followed an entirely separate corporate journey. In 1986, General Electric acquired RCA Corporation for $6.28 billion, primarily coveting the NBC television network. GE chairman Jack Welch immediately began liquidating RCA's non-broadcast divisions, viewing recorded music as outside GE's core industrial focus.

In late 1986, GE agreed to sell RCA Records to the German media conglomerate Bertelsmann for $330 million (approx ₹430 crore in 1986). Bertelsmann merged RCA Records with its own Ariola imprint into the Bertelsmann Music Group (BMG), as chronicled in the corporate history of RCA Records.

For eighteen years, Columbia and RCA Victor operated as fierce rivals under Japanese and German ownership. Then, in August 2004, Sony Music and Bertelsmann combined their recorded music businesses into an equal 50/50 joint venture: Sony BMG Music Entertainment. For the first time since their founding at the turn of the twentieth century, Columbia and RCA Victor were housed under a single corporate roof.

The joint venture was plagued by operational frictions, executive clashes, and changing digital market dynamics. In August 2008, Bertelsmann agreed to exit recorded music entirely. As reported by Billboard on Bertelsmann exiting Sony BMG, Sony bought out Bertelsmann's 50% equity stake for $1.2 billion (approx ₹5,760 crore in 2008), restoring the enterprise as the wholly owned Sony Music Entertainment. Bertelsmann subsequently rebuilt a music company focused on publishing and master rights, as reflected in Bertelsmann's modern announcements regarding BBR Music Group.

The Victor name, however, remained divided. Victor's Japanese subsidiary, established in 1927, had become the Victor Company of Japan (JVC). JVC retained the "Victor" trademark and the Nipper emblem exclusively within Japan. Today, JVCKenwood's entertainment division operates Victor Entertainment in Japan, while RCA Records operates as a premier frontline label of Sony Music across the United States and international markets.

The Classical and Artist Legacies

The reunion of Columbia and RCA Victor under Sony Music created an extraordinary archival phenomenon. For decades, classical music enthusiasts were divided into rival camps, following legendary artists who were locked into exclusive contracts with either Columbia Masterworks or RCA Victor Red Seal. Today, those legendary recordings reside within the single archival catalogue of Sony Classical.

Eugene Ormandy and the Philadelphia Orchestra offer a striking illustration of this corporate convergence. Ormandy began his major recording career with RCA Victor in 1934, leading the Minneapolis Symphony and later the Philadelphia Orchestra, as documented in the biographical record of Eugene Ormandy. In 1944, William Paley lured Ormandy and the Philadelphia Orchestra to Columbia Masterworks, inaugurating a legendary 24-year exclusive association that produced hundreds of definitive recordings of the romantic and modern orchestral repertoire. In 1968, Ormandy dramatically defected back to RCA Red Seal, recording prolifically in Camden and Philadelphia until 1981. For decades, hearing Ormandy's complete recorded output required navigating the catalogues of two warring corporate giants; today, Sony Classical curates and reissues both halves of Ormandy's discography.

Leonard Bernstein's recording history followed a parallel trajectory across the label landscape. Bernstein made his earliest 78 rpm recordings for RCA Victor between 1945 and 1950, as documented in Leonard Bernstein's biographical record. In 1956, Bernstein signed an exclusive contract with Columbia Masterworks, recording over 500 compositions with the New York Philharmonic over a 23-year tenure, as detailed on Leonard Bernstein's official Columbia discography archive. In the 1970s, Bernstein developed an expansive European concert relationship with Deutsche Grammophon, as recorded on Bernstein's official Deutsche Grammophon archive, eventually recording exclusively for DG from 1981 until his death in 1990. While Bernstein's late DG catalogue resides today with Universal Music Group, his monumental American Columbia archive remains the cornerstone of Sony Classical.

Vladimir Horowitz also crossed these dynastic battle lines throughout his career. As recounted in the historical record of Vladimir Horowitz's recorded legacy, Horowitz made his American debut recordings for Victor Red Seal in 1928, maintaining an exclusive relationship with RCA until 1962. He then signed with Columbia Masterworks to document his legendary 1965 "Historic Return" recital at Carnegie Hall. In 1975, Horowitz returned to RCA Red Seal, and in his final years recorded for Deutsche Grammophon and Sony Classical. Today, the vast majority of Horowitz's legendary American performances are unified under Sony.

Glenn Gould, by contrast, remained an uncompromising Columbia Masterworks loyalist. As preserved in the biography of Glenn Gould, the eccentric Canadian pianist signed with Columbia in 1955. His debut recording of J.S. Bach's Goldberg Variations captured international acclaim, and his 1981 digital re-recording of the same work bookended his career. Both landmark recordings, along with Gould's entire studio output, are preserved and continuously reissued by Sony Classical.

These individual artists were part of broader institutional rivalries. Arturo Toscanini and the NBC Symphony Orchestra on RCA Victor Red Seal stood in direct opposition to Bruno Walter and George Szell (with the Cleveland Orchestra) on Columbia Masterworks; violinist Jascha Heifetz on RCA Red Seal competed against Isaac Stern and Zino Francescatti on Columbia. Every one of these historic rivalries is now preserved within the single archival catalogue of Sony Classical.

The Global and Indian Dimension

The corporate rivalries between Columbia, Victor, and EMI were never confined to Western markets; they profoundly shaped the recorded music industry of South Asia. Under the British Empire, EMI established its dominance across the subcontinent through The Gramophone Company of India, universally known as "His Master's Voice" (HMV). In 1901, the company set up Asia's first record factory at Dum Dum near Calcutta. For eight decades, HMV operated an effective monopoly over the subcontinent's recorded sound, pressing everything from early cylinder transfers of Gauhar Jaan and Rabindranath Tagore to the golden era of Hindi cinema playback legends, including Lata Mangeshkar, Mohammed Rafi, and Kishore Kumar.

In the early 1980s, American CBS resolved to challenge HMV's domestic monopoly. Partnering with the Tata-affiliated industrial group, CBS incorporated CBS Gramophone Records & Tapes (India) Ltd in 1982, establishing its headquarters in Dalamal Tower at Nariman Point, Bombay. As reported by India Today's 1982 economic profile of CBS's Indian debut, the company invested heavily in modern cassette duplication technology to undercut HMV's pricing and combat audio piracy, introducing international pop, jazz, and Indian devotional music into the market.

Following Sony's global acquisition of CBS Records, Sony Music made a historic move into India. In 1996, Sony Music India was established as the first 100% foreign-owned record company permitted by the Indian government. The new venture revolutionized the Indian music industry by signing composer A.R. Rahman and releasing his iconic 1997 patriotic album Vande Mataram to commemorate the 50th anniversary of Indian independence. The release set new production and marketing standards, cementing Sony Music India as a driving force in Bollywood, Indipop, and regional music.

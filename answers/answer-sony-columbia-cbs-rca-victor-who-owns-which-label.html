Sony, Columbia, CBS, RCA, Victor: who owns which label

2026-10-05

Both decades, in a sense. Sony agreed to buy CBS Records in November 1987 and completed the purchase on 5 January 1988, paying $2 billion for the CBS Records Group — so the catalogues became Sony's property in the eighties. The Sony name came later: CBS Records announced in October 1990 that it would change its name to Sony Music Entertainment on 1 January 1991, because the sale agreement had let Sony use the CBS name "for a limited time, expiring in Jan. 1991". Sony Classical, the classical label you are remembering, was created in 1990. Bought in the eighties, renamed in the nineties, classical relabelled in 1990.

The five names you list are really two families plus one owner. Columbia and RCA are Sony labels, Victor is split by country, and CBS belongs to somebody else entirely.

The purchase, the rename, the new labels

Sony's own corporate history dates the agreement to 19 November 1987 at two billion US dollars, and the deal closed that January. What Sony bought was the CBS Records Group: in the United States that meant Columbia Records, Epic, and the classical division. The Los Angeles Times reported that the January 1991 rename "won't affect the company's domestic record labels, which are Columbia, Epic, Associated and WTG" — the company changed its name, and the labels underneath largely did not.

The classical side changed earlier and more thoroughly. Sony Classical's own history page puts it this way: Sony acquired CBS Records in 1988 and "two years later it created Sony Classical to succeed the time-honoured Columbia and CBS Masterworks labels. The Sony Classical logo in its present form thus dates from 1990." Those two names it succeeded had themselves been a relay: the flagship classical line began as Columbia Masterworks, was renamed CBS Masterworks in 1980 when it separated from the pop Columbia label, and became Sony Classical in 1990. The Masterworks name survives as a Sony imprint for classical and Broadway releases, and both it and Sony Classical appear on Sony Music's label list today.

If by "Sony label" you mean an imprint printed with the words Sony Records, that is a Japanese story: CBS/Sony Records, founded 1968, changed its name to Sony Records in 1991, and Sony's Japanese operation later spawned further names, quite separate from the American company, Sony Music Entertainment Inc., which took that name on 1 January 1991. There is also a Sony Music Group holding company formed in 2019, and Sony Music Publishing — publishing, not recordings. The label called simply Sony in the eighties was CBS.

Columbia and CBS: how one name ended up with Sony and the other did not

Columbia is the oldest of the family. The Columbia Phonograph Company dates to the 1880s; its British arm, Columbia Graphophone, merged with the Gramophone Company in 1931 to form EMI, which carried the Columbia name outside the United States. In America the label passed to the American Record Corporation in 1934 and then, in 1938, to William Paley's Columbia Broadcasting System for $700,000 — the purchase that put a record label and a broadcast network under one roof. Sony Classical's own account confirms the split: the US division was bought by CBS in 1938, while "its UK subsidiary had already merged with the Gramophone Company to form EMI".

That split is why the Columbia name had to be bought back. In 1990 CBS Records acquired the Columbia label name from EMI for use outside the United States, so that from 1 January 1991 Sony Music Entertainment could use Columbia worldwide "with the exception of Japan and Spain" — in Japan the mark belongs to Nippon Columbia, a separate company. Meanwhile the CBS name was never sold: Sony's licence to use it expired in January 1991, and CBS itself went off down a different corporate road, from Westinghouse to Viacom to Paramount, while relaunching its own short-lived CBS Records in 2006. CBS the broadcaster and Sony's labels share a history until 1988 and nothing since.

RCA and Victor: the other half of your list

RCA's chain runs differently. Victor Talking Machine Company was founded in 1901, and Radio Corporation of America bought it in 1929 to create RCA Victor — the label's own history tells it that way. The record division stayed RCA Victor until 1968, when it became RCA Records. Then General Electric acquired RCA in 1986 and sold the music business to Bertelsmann: Bertelsmann's own corporate history dates the acquisition to 1986, while other accounts put the final transfer of GE's stake in 1987, and the label became part of BMG. In 2004 BMG's music business merged with Sony's to form Sony BMG, and on 1 October 2008 Sony completed the purchase of Bertelsmann's half. RCA Records sits on Sony Music's label list today as one of its flagship labels.

Victor, unlike RCA, is not Sony's alone. The American company's Japanese subsidiary, founded in 1927, became JVC, and it is JVC's successor that still holds the Victor name in Japan; recordings released under the Victor name there are JVCKenwood's Victor Entertainment, not Sony's. So RCA Victor as a US imprint means Sony, while Victor as a company name means a Japanese electronics group. Of the five names you asked about: Columbia is Sony's everywhere except Japan, CBS Sony does not own, Sony is the owner, RCA is Sony's, and Victor is split between the two.

What Ormandy and Bernstein were on

Eugene Ormandy started on Victor: his recording career began with the Minneapolis Symphony for RCA Victor in 1934, and he stayed with Victor after taking over Philadelphia in 1938. In 1944 he and the orchestra moved to Columbia for a 23-year association; his Columbia tenure is dated 1944 to 1968, after which he was back on RCA Victor until 1981, with a few late recordings for Delos, EMI and Telarc. The commercial point is that Sony owns both halves of that discography: it sells an 11-CD set of his RCA Victor recordings from 1935–42 alongside his Columbia catalogues under Sony Classical.

Leonard Bernstein's first records came for Decca and RCA Victor — Decca in June 1944, then a variety of works for RCA Victor between 1945 and 1950 — before Columbia became his home. He signed his first long-term contract with Columbia on 2 April 1956 and recorded over 500 compositions for Columbia Masterworks between 1956 and 1979, most of them with the New York Philharmonic. The Deutsche Grammophon relationship grew alongside that rather than after it: DG's own decade history has his first recording for the label in 1972, a live Beethoven programme on 17 October 1976 served as the test for the contract and opened a collaboration lasting 14 years down to April 1990, and he became an exclusive DG artist in 1981. He also recorded for Decca, EMI and Philips along the way.

The two halves of Bernstein's discography now sit under different owners, which is the point of the whole story: his Columbia years were reissued by Sony Classical, including a 100-CD set for his 2018 centennial, while Deutsche Grammophon belongs to Universal. The discs from his and Ormandy's main Columbia decades say Columbia Masterworks, not CBS Masterworks — that name only appeared in 1980, after both men's principal tenures with the company had ended.

So the label family you are looking at today is the 1988 purchase of Columbia, Epic and the classical catalogue, renamed Sony Music Entertainment on 1 January 1991 and relabelled Sony Classical in 1990, plus RCA, which arrived only through BMG in 2008 — with CBS and the standalone Victor company name left outside.

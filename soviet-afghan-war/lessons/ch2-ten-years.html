Chapter 2 — The ten years, part one: why Moscow could not win

December 1979 – 11 March 1985 · ~25 min · Course home

Moscow sent an army to hold a government in place, under orders that assumed the holding would be done by someone else. This chapter answers the first half of the ten-years question: what the Soviet force was actually ordered to do, and why those orders could not be carried out — a weak local partner, a resistance with an untouchable sanctuary, terrain that cancelled Soviet advantages, and a supply pipeline that grew stronger every year.

Boundary

This page covers chapter 2 only: from the arrival of the Soviet force in late December 1979 to the accession of Mikhail Gorbachev on 11 March 1985. The road to the invasion belongs to chapter 1; the withdrawal decisions and Geneva belong to chapter 3. Nothing past 11 March 1985 is narrated here.

1. What this chapter does

The argument, in one paragraph: the force that crossed the border in December 1979 was a limited contingent with limited orders — hold the cities, the ring road, the airfields and the infrastructure, prop up the Afghan army, and do not get drawn into a counterinsurgency. Every one of those premises failed. The Afghan army was melting away faster than Soviet advisers could rebuild it; the government in Kabul commanded the cities and almost nothing else; the men shooting at Soviet convoys recruited, trained, treated their wounded and buried their dead across a border the 40th Army was forbidden to cross in strength; and the weapons reaching them, paid for by Washington and Riyadh and delivered by Islamabad, improved every year. The result was not defeat in the field — Soviet columns could take any ground they concentrated against — but a war that could not be won at any level of effort Moscow was willing to pay, and Moscow spent five years learning that.

3. What matters — and what you can skip

Read closely: the limited-contingent orders and why they unravelled; the Afghan army's collapse in numbers; the sanctuary mechanism (why a Pakistan-based resistance could not be beaten inside Afghanistan); and the cost ledger as it stood in early 1985. That is the whole argument.

Skim if pressed: the individual Panjshir offensives after the pattern is clear (take ground, leave, return to find the enemy back); the succession of General Secretaries except as it explains why no decision could be taken; the Geneva rounds, which are covered here only to show why they went nowhere — the settlement mechanics belong to chapter 3.

4. The narrative

The force and its orders

On 24 December 1979 Defence Minister Ustinov announced the decision to introduce troops, and the 40th Army — re-created days earlier in the Turkestan Military District under Lieutenant General Tukharinov — began crossing on 25 December, on the ground and in the air, with the 103rd Guards Airborne Division landing at Bagram and Kabul airport while motor-rifle divisions rolled down the two great axes: Termez–Hairatan–Pul-e-Khumri–Kabul in the east, Kushka–Herat–Shindand–Kandahar in the west (operational plan as reconstructed by Baumann and McMichael in the US Army's Occasional Paper 36, drawing on Soviet General Staff material). The initial combat strength was on the order of 30,000 men (Britannica, Afghan War), built around the 108th and 5th Guards Motor Rifle Divisions plus airborne regiments and brigades. Storm-333 took Amin's palace on 27 December; Babrak Karmal, smuggled in from exile, announced himself from a transmitter at Termez and appealed for the Soviet assistance that was already landing.

The official name of what had arrived matters: the Limited Contingent of Soviet Forces in Afghanistan (ogranichenny kontingent). The plan, in Baumann's reconstruction, called for Soviet forces to garrison the major centres along the two invasion axes — which would serve as lines of communication throughout the war — seizing the ring road and the airbases first. The political assumption behind it was explicit in retrospect: install Karmal, temporarily garrison the key sites, train the Afghan army to fight the insurgency, deliver economic aid, and leave within months. Senior political leaders appear to have believed the mission could be accomplished in a matter of months (Occasional Paper 36). The orders, in short: hold the cities, the ring roads, the airfields and the infrastructure; back the Afghan army; do not be drawn into counterinsurgency. Within weeks, as the orders from Moscow changed to "begin active operations," a mechanised army with no counterinsurgency doctrine found itself in exactly the war it had been told to avoid.

The partner that could not hold

The government the contingent was holding up had almost no ground under it. The PDPA had split in 1967 into Taraki's radical Khalq and Karmal's gradualist Parcham; after the 1978 Saur revolution the Khalqis purged, exiled and executed the Parchamites — between 10,000 and 27,000 people killed at Pul-e-Charkhi prison before the Soviet intervention by the commonly cited counts (Olivier Roy's wider estimate runs 50,000–100,000 disappeared in the Taraki–Amin period). Karmal himself survived only because the Soviets posted him to Prague as ambassador, hid him from an alleged Amin hit squad in a Czechoslovak forest lodge, and extracted him via Moscow in October 1979 (RFE/RL, from Czech StB files and Mitrokhin material). He returned owing his throne to foreigners, leading the faction that had just spent eighteen months being shot by the faction still embedded in the army and ministries. An Australian foreign-affairs chronology of 1980 dryly noted Karmal purging Khalq ministers even as he centralised power — the fracture governed with him.

The Afghan army, the instrument that was supposed to do the fighting while the Soviets garrisoned, was disintegrating. From roughly 100,000–110,000 men in 1978 it fell to about 25,000–30,000 by 1980 through desertion, mutiny and purges — the US embassy in Kabul cabled that it was melting away "like an ice floe in a tropical sea." Conscription drives failed or backfired: press-ganged recruits deserted with their rifles and swelled the mujahideen, and Karmal's government briefly hoped to disband the inherited army and build a loyal one from scratch, a move Soviet advisers vetoed because it would have put the entire counterinsurgency burden on Soviet troops (RAND/Dobbins-era DTIC study, Building Afghanistan's Security Forces in Wartime). Units stayed at a fraction of paper strength for the whole decade — divisions averaging a quarter of establishment by the mid-1980s — and the Soviets compounded the weakness by stripping Afghan units of heavy anti-tank and anti-aircraft weapons they feared would be turned or captured. The countryside, meanwhile, had been lost before the Soviets arrived: by spring 1979, 24 of 28 provinces had seen outbreaks of violence, and the land and social reforms of a government with no rural base had united tribe, landlord and clergy against Kabul.

The resistance and its shape

What Moscow faced was not an army but a landscape of enemies. The fighting parties headquartered themselves across the border in Peshawar, Pakistan — seven Sunni parties that Pakistan recognised and funded, of which two dominated: Gulbuddin Hekmatyar's Hezb-e Islami, favoured by Zia's regime and the ISI for its discipline and Islamist ideology, and Burhanuddin Rabbani's Jamiat-e Islami, whose strength lay in field commanders rather than headquarters. The commanders were the war: Ahmad Shah Massoud in the Panjshir, who built the most organised front of the war; Ismail Khan around Herat, rising from the officers of the mutinied 17th Division; Abdul Haq in Kabul and the east; Jalaluddin Haqqani in the southeast out of Miranshah, originally with Yunus Khalis's Hezb faction. Alongside them stood the Shia parties backed from Iran, outside the Peshawar system entirely. The clergy gave the jihad its language and the tribes its manpower; neither gave it a single command. There was never real unity — parties fought each other as well as Kabul — and the disunity was structural: Zia had pressured the resistance to organise as fundable "parties" precisely so the ISI could control the flow of aid (Clark University thesis on US–Pakistan–Saudi funding, drawing on Coll and Cogan).

The attempt to fix the disunity came at this chapter's far edge. On 16 May 1985 the seven Peshawar leaders announced, under pressure from Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and the United States, a formal alliance — the Islamic Unity of Afghanistan Mujahideen — with a joint military committee and a joint communiqué (Sabahuddin Kushkaki's contemporary assessment). Whether it was founded in 1981 or 1985 is disputed between scholars — Vijay Prashad argues 1981 with Saudi financing, Tom Lansford dates the formal coalition to 1985 — but the May 1985 announcement is the documented diplomatic fact, and it changed little on the ground: the alliance was a united front for world opinion, not a unified command.

The sanctuary and the pipeline

The war could not be won inside Afghanistan because it was supplied, recruited, trained and hospitalised outside it. By the mid-1980s nearly three million Afghan refugees lived in Pakistan and some two million in Iran — camps administered with UNHCR help that were simultaneously a humanitarian refuge and, in Mao's phrase inverted, the sea the guerrillas swam in: recruits, rest areas, hospitals, training camps and arms bazaars the 40th Army could strike only at the cost of war with Pakistan. The Pakistan army's role was not passive tolerance but active management — sanctuary as policy.

The pipeline that fed the sanctuary was deliberate statecraft. A 3 July 1979 presidential finding had authorised the CIA to spend up to $695,000 on cash, non-military supplies and propaganda for the insurgents — "either directly or through third countries" (FRUS, Vol. XII, Doc. 76, with Carter's handwritten instruction to consult the Saudis and Pakistanis first). After the invasion Carter amended the finding in January 1980: money and then weapons, through the ISI. Brzezinski's 26 December 1979 memo framed the objective as giving Moscow its own Vietnam; Carter raised the programme toward $50 million and began calling the mujahideen "freedom fighters" (War on the Rocks, drawing on the Carter papers). Brzezinski secured the Saudi agreement to match CIA funding dollar-for-dollar on a February 1980 visit, with Egypt and China supplying Soviet-pattern weapons and Britain's MI6 running its own channel. Funding that began at $20–30 million a year in 1980 rose steeply under Reagan, for whom support to the mujahideen became the centrepiece of the Reagan Doctrine. And the ISI, as sole distributor, decided who grew strong: the Islamist parties closest to Zia — above all Hekmatyar — received the lion's share, shaping post-war Afghan politics a decade early. The pipeline did not merely sustain the resistance; it selected its winners.

"Provide support, either directly or through third countries, to the Afghan insurgents in the form of cash, non-military supplies, communications equipment and procurement advice."

— Presidential Finding scope, 3 July 1979, in FRUS 1977–1980, Vol. XII, Doc. 76 (Office of the Historian, US Department of State)

Whether Washington meant to provoke the invasion is the sharpest dispute in this chapter's record. In a 1998 interview with Le Nouvel Observateur, Brzezinski said the administration had "knowingly increased the probability" of a Soviet intervention and called drawing the Russians into "the Afghan trap" an "excellent idea" — then later disputed the interview's accuracy (VOA retrospective; full translated text hosted by Prof. David Gibbs, University of Arizona). Coll, Harrison, Riedel and the CIA's own Charles Cogan all hold that no US-supplied arms reached Pakistan before January 1980, after Carter amended the finding — aid before the invasion was money and facilitation, not weapons. Both readings are presented because the participants themselves never agreed.

Why Soviet advantages cancelled out

A mechanised army built for the North German plain met mountains with one road through them. Armour and artillery could move only where the ring road and the Salang highway went, so every operation was road-bound and predictable; helicopters and ground-attack aircraft — the famous Mi-24s and Su-25s — were devastating against villages and convoys and nearly useless against men who dispersed into side valleys and fired from opposing ridgelines. The Panjshir became the demonstration. Between 1980 and 1985 nine offensives went up the valley — the small probes of 1980–81, the 12,000-man combined-arms operation of spring 1982 with over 200 fixed-wing sorties, the 15,000-man Panjshir VII of April–September 1984 — and each followed the same script: seal the valley, bomb it flat, declare the enemy destroyed, withdraw to hold the highway, and watch Massoud's organisation reconstitute (Air & Space Forces Magazine survey; Osprey Panjshir Valley study). In January 1983 Massoud even negotiated a formal ceasefire directly with a GRU colonel, Anatoly Tkachev (Tkachyov) — Soviet troops evacuated the valley except a garrison at Anava whose access the mujahideen controlled — and used the breathing space to rebuild before fighting resumed in 1984. Edward Westermann's figures capture the futility curve: 13 mujahideen heavy machine guns in the Panjshir in 1982, nearly 250 by the end of 1984, Chinese deliveries included.

Denied a decision in battle, the campaign turned against the population that sustained the guerrillas: bombing of villages, mining of fields and passes — Afghanistan suffered among the highest landmine injury rates recorded in any modern conflict (BMJ review of Afghan war casualties) — reprisals, and depopulation on a scale that drove millions across the borders. It produced refugees faster than it produced results, and every column of new refugees replenished the sanctuary. Soviet tactics did adapt at the margins — spetsnaz ambush teams on the caravan routes from Pakistan, heliborne air-assault blocking detachments, the SRV-style cordon operations of the mid-1980s — but adaptation could not change the arithmetic: a force that never exceeded roughly a hundred thousand men, tied to its garrisons and its two highways, could clear any valley and hold none of them. The honest limit of the contingent was not courage or technology but size multiplied by mission: too small to pacify, too large to sustain without hollowing the treasury and the conscript cohort.

The cost ledger as known in 1985

The Soviet public in 1985 knew almost nothing exact. Casualty figures were a state secret; the dead came home as sealed "Cargo 200," the wounded to closed wards, and the official language was "internationalist duty" — a phrase whose erosion into bitter irony the veterans' movement would later document. (The post-war official accounting associated with General G. F. Krivosheev's General Staff statistical study would eventually publish 14,453 dead across the whole war, with nearly 50,000 disabled and over 400,000 hospitalised for wounds and disease — but none of that was publishable in 1985.) What the public could see was the zinc: the funerals, the cripples, the heroin that travelled the same airbridge as the coffins.

Abroad, the price was itemised promptly. On 14 January 1980 the UN General Assembly demanded the withdrawal by 104 votes to 18 — Carter quoted the tally back at Moscow within days. The resolutions were then renewed every year. Carter's January 1980 State of the Union declared the Persian Gulf a vital American interest to be defended by force — the Carter Doctrine — alongside a grain and technology embargo, restricted Soviet fishing rights, the shelving of SALT II (signed the previous June, it never reached a Senate vote; standard Cold War histories, e.g. Raymond Garthoff, Détente and Confrontation, 1985), and the Olympic boycott: Carter's 20 January ultimatum, then the 21 March announcement that no American team would go to Moscow.

"Neither I nor the American people would support the sending of an American team to Moscow with Soviet invasion troops in Afghanistan… the United Nations General Assembly, by an overwhelming vote of 104 to 18, condemned the invasion and urged the prompt withdrawal of Soviet troops."

— Jimmy Carter, Meet the Press interview, 20 January 1980, and subsequent boycott statement (transcript excerpts)

Détente died in the same season. The Islamic Conference and the non-aligned movement — forums where Moscow had courted the Third World for a decade — condemned the invasion and kept Afghanistan's seat a controversy; arms control froze; and every subsequent General Assembly vote re-staged the same isolation. By 1985 the war was costing the Soviet Union its hard-won standing in precisely the world it claimed to be liberating.

The politics of stalemate in Moscow

No Soviet leader in this period could end the war because for most of it there was barely a functioning leadership. Brezhnev, already ailing at the invasion, died in November 1982; Andropov — who as KGB chief had co-authored the invasion and as General Secretary hinted to Zia at Brezhnev's funeral that Soviet troops would leave "quickly" if Pakistan cut the pipeline — died in February 1984 after fifteen months of kidney failure and hospital rule; Chernenko, already ill at his accession, died in March 1985. Ustinov, the invasion's uniformed executor, died in December 1984. Policy ran on autopilot between funerals, and autopilot meant more troops, bigger sweeps, and no political strategy beyond Karmal's survival.

The one channel that stayed open was Geneva. In June 1982 UN mediator Diego Cordovez began proximity talks — Pakistan and Afghanistan in the same building, never the same room, Cordovez shuttling between them — toward a settlement built on withdrawal, non-interference, guarantees and refugee return (Richard Cronin, Afghanistan peace talks chronology, 1988; Afghanistan Peace Campaign history of the talks). By April 1983 Cordovez was calling a draft text "95 percent" complete. It went nowhere while the war continued, for one structural reason: the talks assumed the answer to the question nobody had agreed — what government would Kabul have after a withdrawal, and who would guarantee the communists there would not be massacred. Islamabad would not close the pipeline without that answer; Moscow would not name a withdrawal date without the pipeline closed; Washington, after 1981, preferred pressure to settlement. The channel stayed alive because everyone found it useful, and produced nothing because no one needed it to. That deadlock is the inheritance Gorbachev received — and resolving it is chapter 3.

5. The machinery

This chapter establishes the course's central mechanism: a limited intervention is unwinnable when three conditions hold together — sanctuary the intervener cannot touch, terrain that neutralises its military advantages, and a local partner too weak to hold what is cleared. Each condition alone is manageable: sanctuaries can be negotiated away, terrain overcome with adaptation, weak partners rebuilt. Together they form a ratchet. The sanctuary (Pakistan, and secondarily Iran) meant the enemy's losses were replaceable and its arsenal improved yearly; the terrain meant Soviet firepower could punish but not decide; the partner's weakness meant every cleared valley had to be cleared again, because no Afghan force existed to stay behind. Effort could move losses up and down — more troops, bigger offensives, heavier bombing — but no level of effort available to a "limited contingent" could break any one of the three constraints, let alone all three at once. Escalation within the limit changed the war's temperature, never its structure. That is why the five years from 1980 to 1985 look like motion without movement, and why the eventual exit would require changing the political frame rather than the force levels.

7. Open threads

As of 11 March 1985, the period's own unresolved questions: Can the Afghan army ever take over the fighting, or is the contingent now the war? Moscow's whole exit theory depended on an Afghan force that shrank every year. Can the pipeline be closed by pressure on Pakistan, or only by a settlement that lets Zia close it with honour? Andropov's funeral hint to Zia was the first formulation of the trade; no one had yet priced it. Can the resistance be split into a negotiable part and a fightable part? The May 1985 alliance answered with a single rejectionist front instead. What does "victory" even mean now? No Soviet document of the period redefined the mission after "begin active operations" failed to produce one — the war continued because stopping required a decision the dying leadership could not take. These are the questions Gorbachev inherits, stated as they looked from inside March 1985, not as hints of what came later.

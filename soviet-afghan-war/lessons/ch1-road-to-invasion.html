Soviet-Afghan War · Chapter 1 of 3

Chapter 1 — The neighbour

1919 – 28 December 1979 · ~25 min · Course home

Nothing about December 1979 was sudden inside the machinery that ordered it. For sixty years Moscow had treated Afghanistan as a buffer to be paid for, not a prize to be seized — and for nine months in 1979 the Politburo had explicitly refused to send troops. This chapter rebuilds that sixty-year connection and then replays the decision meeting by meeting, so the word "suddenly" has nowhere left to hide.

Boundary

This page covers chapter 1 only: it starts with the Anglo-Afghan Treaty of Rawalpindi (August 1919) and the opening of Soviet-Afghan relations, and it ends with the killing of Hafizullah Amin and the installation of Babrak Karmal on 27–28 December 1979. It does not narrate 1980 or later, except where a decision taken in December 1979 is only intelligible by naming the intent behind it.

1. What this chapter does

The argument is twofold. First, the Soviet-Afghan connection was a patronage relationship — treaties, credits, roads, airfields, officer training — built up from 1921 and deepened after 1955, and it survived every Afghan regime change because both sides wanted a quiet buffer. Second, the invasion was not ordered by that logic but by a narrower one: a small group of ageing men in Moscow, watching their Afghan clients first radicalise, then mutiny, then murder each other, and fearing above all that Afghanistan would "go over to the Americans" the way Egypt had under Sadat in 1972. The chapter establishes the course's central mechanism: how a reluctant patron becomes an occupier, and how a small-group decision style turns drift into a decision.

3. What matters — and what you can skip

Read closely: the 1955 turn (why Moscow started paying), the March 1979 refusal (the decision that proves December was a choice, not an autopilot), the September–October 1979 sequence (Taraki's murder, the Blood–Amin meeting), and the 8–12 December paper trail (who decided, in what room, on what pretext).

Skim or skip: the 1921–1953 background (one paragraph is enough — the point is only that the buffer predates the Cold War), the alphabet soup of PDPA congresses, and the order-of-battle detail of the December airlift. None of it is tested below.

4. The narrative

The buffer, 1919–1954

Afghanistan was the first state to recognise the Soviet regime, and the Soviet-Afghan friendship treaty — signed in 1920 and ratified 28 February 1921 — made the bargain explicit: Kabul would close its northern border to the anti-Soviet Basmachi insurgents, and Moscow would offer friendship, and arms, against Britain (US Marine Corps Soviet-Afghan area study, drawing on the treaty record; the Wilson Center's Cold War International History Project holds the early Soviet-Afghan file in its digital archive). Treaties on neutrality and non-aggression followed in 1926 and 1931, and Kabul's standing doctrine became bi-tarafi — playing the great powers against each other while joining no bloc.

The arrangement survived Amanullah, Nadir Shah (1929) and Zahir Shah (king from 1933) because it asked little of either side. Moscow got a quiet southern border; Kabul got a second supplier to set against London. Nothing in it predicted an invasion.

The 1955 turn: Pakistan joins the West, Moscow opens its wallet

What changed the price was Pakistan. Pakistan signed a mutual defence agreement with the United States in May 1954, joined SEATO in September 1954 and the Baghdad Pact (the future CENTO) in September 1955 — and Afghanistan, which refused to recognise the Durand Line and pressed the Pashtunistan claim for a Pashtun homeland inside Pakistan, read encirclement (US Department of State histories of SEATO/CENTO; the Pashtunistan dispute is the standard context in every diplomatic history of the period). Prime Minister Muhammad Daoud Khan answered by signing an arms agreement through Czechoslovakia in 1955, and when Bulganin and Khrushchev visited Kabul 15–19 December 1955 — a visit documented in Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, vol. VIII — they extended a large development credit, conventionally given in the histories as $100 million (Rodric Braithwaite, Afgantsy, 2011).

From that winter onward the pattern held for two decades: Soviet-built roads (including the Salang highway and tunnel through the Hindu Kush), airfields, factories, thousands of Afghan officers trained on Soviet soil, and a trade relationship that made Moscow Kabul's largest partner. The infrastructure, as one later history notes dryly, "served chiefly to make invasion and occupation easier" when the decision came — but it was built for influence, not for invasion (Michael Bonner, essay on the 1978 treaty, 2012, summarising the standard account).

Constitution, party, split: 1964–1967

Zahir Shah's 1964 constitution opened a brief parliamentary experiment. On 1 January 1965 the People's Democratic Party of Afghanistan (PDPA) held its founding congress, with Nur Muhammad Taraki as general secretary. In 1967 it split into the two factions that would destroy it and then the country: Khalq ("Masses"), led by Taraki with Hafizullah Amin as organiser, drawing on Pashtun rural cadres and army officers; and Parcham ("Banner"), led by Babrak Karmal, urban, Dari-speaking, gradualist (US Department of State, Office of the Historian milestone essay "The Soviet Invasion of Afghanistan and the U.S. Response, 1978–1980").

Daoud's republic, 1973–1978

On 17 July 1973, while Zahir Shah was abroad, his cousin Daoud seized power in a nearly bloodless coup — carried out with the help of Soviet-trained Parcham-linked officers — and declared a republic with himself as president (State Department milestone essay). Daoud then did what nationalists do with communist kingmakers: he spent five years getting rid of them, purging leftists, courting Iran, Saudi Arabia and eventually the United States, and pressing the Pashtunistan line harder than Moscow liked. By 1977–78 Moscow regarded him as ungrateful and dispensable; his own officers regarded him as a dead end. When Daoud ordered the arrest of PDPA leaders after the murder of the Parchami Mir Akbar Khyber on 17 April 1978, the Khalq officers around Amin moved first.

The Saur revolution, 27–28 April 1978

The coup of 27–28 April 1978 — named for the month of Saur in the Afghan calendar — used some 250 tanks and armoured vehicles in Kabul; by the morning of the 28th Daoud and most of his family were dead inside the Arg palace, and Taraki chaired the Revolutionary Council with Karmal as his deputy (State Department milestone essay; Braithwaite, Afgantsy). Washington recognised the new government and sent Adolph Dubs as ambassador, hoping — in Dubs's words and policy — that engagement would keep Soviet influence at bay.

Moscow's embrace was warmer and came with paperwork: on 5 December 1978 Taraki signed a twenty-year Treaty of Friendship, Good-Neighbourliness and Cooperation with Brezhnev, its Article 4 providing for consultation and "appropriate measures" for security — the clause Soviet lawyers would reach for a year later (HISTORY.com treaty entry, citing the treaty text; GlobalSecurity.org summary of the Article 4 / UN Charter Article 51 justification).

The reforms that lit the countryside

In the autumn of 1978 the Khalq government issued its revolutionary decrees — land redistribution, cancellation of peasant debt, abolition of bride-price, compulsory literacy with mixed-sex classes — and imposed them at a speed the countryside read as war on Islam and custom; the land reform drive rolled out from 1 January 1979 (Braithwaite, Afgantsy; the Afghanistan Analysts Network's Herat study). By the winter of 1978 armed revolt was spreading province by province, and the regime answered with mass arrests: Western estimates later put political prisoners and executions in the thousands (State Department milestone essay notes the regime's own vast detention campaign). In the summer of 1978 Amin purged the Parchamis — Karmal was posted to Prague as ambassador in July and then, refusing recall, defected into Soviet shelter.

Herat, March 1979 — and the first Soviet refusal

On 15 March 1979 Herat exploded: a popular uprising fused with the mutiny of the Afghan Army's 17th Division, whose officers — including the young Captain Ismail Khan — turned their guns on the regime. The city fell to rebels and mutineers for several days; Soviet advisers in the city were hunted down and killed, though accounts of the number differ wildly — official Soviet sources admitted two dead, while some Western press reports ran as high as two hundred; the Afghanistan Analysts Network's careful reconstruction concludes a handful of foreign advisers were killed (AAN, "Failings of Inclusivity: The Herat uprising of March 1979"). Kabul retook the city with armour and air power at great cost to its inhabitants; the rising remains the deadliest episode of the 1978–79 pre-war period.

Taraki panicked and telephoned Moscow. On 18 March 1979 Premier Kosygin took the call — the transcript survives in the Wilson Center's CWIHP e-Dossier No. 4 — and Taraki begged for Soviet Central Asian troops disguised as Afghans to hold Herat:

"Why can't the Soviet Union send Uzbeks, Tajiks, and Turkmens in civilian clothing? … We want you to send us Tajiks, Uzbeks and Turkmens so they can drive tanks, since we have all these nationalities in Afghanistan."

— Nur Muhammad Taraki to Alexei Kosygin, 18 March 1979, transcript in CWIHP e-Dossier No. 4 (English translation)

Kosygin refused, and on 17–18 March the full Politburo debated the Afghan request and refused it as policy. The transcripts — first published from the Bukovsky Archive and summarised in the National Security Archive's 2019 briefing book — show Kosygin as the lead voice: Soviet troops must not be mixed with Afghan units, and intervention would throw away everything the revolution claimed to stand for. Gromyko warned of the damage to détente; Andropov himself cautioned about the international reaction. The consensus line, in Kosygin's words: "we must not surrender Afghanistan" — but troops would lose it, not save it (National Security Archive briefing book, 28 January 2019, "The Soviet Invasion of Afghanistan, 1979").

Hold that refusal in mind. Everything after March is the story of what wore it down.

What wore it down: Kabul, March–September 1979

Four things compounded. First, the insurgency kept spreading while the Afghan Army began to hollow out through desertion. Second, Washington's posture hardened in ways Moscow could see: Ambassador Dubs was kidnapped and killed in a botched rescue attempt in February 1979, and on 3 July 1979 President Carter signed a finding authorising covert, initially non-lethal aid to the insurgents — the finding Brzezinski would later mythologise (Steve Coll, Ghost Wars, 2004). Third, the Iranian revolution deposed the Shah in January 1979, and Moscow feared Washington would replace its lost Iranian listening stations and its lost regional pillar with positions in Afghanistan — while also fearing Islamic contagion into Soviet Central Asia. Fourth, and decisively, the clients in Kabul turned on each other.

Taraki flew to Moscow in early September 1979 and was embraced by Brezhnev — photographs of the hug went out to the world press. Soviet accounts (notably Ambassador Puzanov's reporting, via Lyakhovsky) hold that Brezhnev advised Taraki to remove Amin, whose brutality was wrecking the regime's base. Taraki tried. On 14 September 1979 Amin was summoned to the palace; bodyguards inside opened fire, killing Amin's police chief Sayed Daoud Tarun but only wounding Amin, who escaped to the Defence Ministry, put the army on alert, and had Taraki arrested (Britannica biography of Taraki; National Security Archive briefing book). Moscow's man was now Amin — the man Moscow distrusted most.

October: the murder and the meeting

On 8 October 1979 Amin had Taraki smothered — the standard account, reconstructed by Braithwaite from the post-1979 trials of Taraki's killers, holds that he was suffocated in his bed and buried quietly; Kabul announced his death only afterward (Braithwaite, Afgantsy; National Security Archive briefing book). For Brezhnev, who had embraced Taraki weeks earlier, the murder was personal: both Kornienko's memoir and Gromyko's own memoirs name it as Brezhnev's turning point (Georgy Kornienko, The Cold War: Testimony of a Participant, 1994, excerpted as Document 7 of the Archive briefing book).

Then came the meeting that set off the KGB's alarms. On 27 October 1979 Amin received the acting American chargé, Archer Blood, alone for forty minutes, in fluent English — Amin had studied at Columbia — and asked for better relations. Blood's cable (Kabul 7726, 28 October 1979, published in FRUS 1977–1980, vol. XII: Afghanistan, December 2018) reads as routine diplomacy; in Moscow it read as a flip in progress. Two days later the Andropov–Ustinov–Gromyko–Ponomarev commission reported to the Central Committee that everything must be done to prevent "the political re-orientation of H. Amin towards the West," and Andropov's covering language warned of Amin's contacts with the American chargé "kept secret from us" (Report of 29 October 1979, via Lyakhovsky, The Tragedy and Valor of the Afghani, 1995 — Document 3 of the briefing book).

The Egyptian precedent haunted every page: Sadat had expelled Soviet advisers in 1972 and taken American aid. The briefing book's verdict on Soviet motives is blunt: the fear was not terrorists crossing into Russia, and not warm-water ports — it was that Amin would take Afghanistan over to the Americans, with US missiles aimed at Kazakhstan and Siberia hypothetically to follow (National Security Archive briefing book, citing Dobrynin's 1995 notes of the Kremlin file).

The decision, 8–12 December 1979

Decision-making had by now contracted to a small group around Andropov. In early December Andropov sent Brezhnev a handwritten memo — known only because Ambassador Dobrynin copied it from the Kremlin archive in 1995 — recommending Amin's removal as a probable Western re-orienter (Document 4 of the briefing book). Defence Minister Ustinov agreed but insisted against relying on special forces alone: he demanded 75,000 troops, over the objections of his own generals (Lyakhovsky's account — Document 5).

On 8 December 1979 Andropov, Ustinov and a compliant Gromyko — who had, in Kornienko's account, given up on US–Soviet relations — met Brezhnev and obtained his assent, waving the dangers to the southern border and the spectre of American missiles in Afghanistan (Dobrynin's notes; Kornienko memoir). On 12 December the core group carried a one-page resolution, "On the Situation in 'A'" — handwritten by Konstantin Chernenko so that not even Kremlin typists would see it — into the Politburo, where those present signed diagonally across the page with no debate. Kosygin, the voice of March, was in hospital and never signed; other members added signatures as late as 26 December (Document 6; Fond 89 of the Russian State Archive, first published in English in the CWIHP Bulletin, Fall 1994).

The execution, 24–28 December 1979

On 24 December 1979 Ustinov signed the directive moving the 40th Army: an airlift into Kabul and Bagram ran 24–27 December while the 5th Guards Motor Rifle Division crossed from Termez toward Hairatan in the north (GlobalSecurity.org invasion summary; RFE/RL 40th-anniversary reconstruction, December 2019). On the evening of 27 December KGB special units stormed the Tajbeg Palace in Operation Storm-333; Amin — who had reportedly told an aide that evening "the Soviets will help us," only to be answered that it was the Soviets attacking — was killed with members of his family, and Soviet troops wrapped the bodies in carpets for burial in unmarked graves (RFE/RL, "Poisonings, Assassination, and a Coup," December 2019). A pre-recorded statement in Babrak Karmal's name went out over Radio Kabul, announcing the new government; Karmal was in place in Kabul on 28 December. The published Soviet justification cited Afghan requests for assistance, Article 4 of the 1978 treaty, and Article 51 of the UN Charter — requests historians treat as manufactured after the fact, since the government doing the "requesting" had been installed by the troops themselves (GlobalSecurity.org; Braithwaite, Afgantsy).

Two days earlier, on 26 December, Brzezinski had sent Carter the memorandum that would define the American telling: the invasion reflected "the age-long dream of Moscow to have direct access to the Indian Ocean" (Brzezinski memorandum, 26 December 1979 — Document 8 of the briefing book). The documents now available show that telling to be false — but it governed Washington's response, and the response is where chapter 2 begins. This chapter ends here, on 28 December 1979, with Karmal installed and the army across the border.

5. The machinery

The mechanism this chapter establishes has two gears that mesh. Gear one: patronage without control. Sixty years of aid bought Moscow influence but never obedience: Daoud took the money and courted Tehran and Washington; Taraki took the treaty and imposed reforms Moscow never ordered; Amin took the weapons and murdered the man Brezhnev had just hugged. Each client was radical in ways that served Kabul faction fights, not Soviet strategy — and each failure of control raised the price of the next intervention, from advisers to airlift crews to, finally, divisions. Gear two: the small group. In March 1979 Afghanistan was debated by the full Politburo with experts consulted, and the answer was no. In December it was settled by Andropov, Ustinov and Gromyko in Brezhnev's office, on a handwritten page, with the sceptics (Kosygin) absent and the doubting specialists (Ponomarev's International Department people, the generals overruled on the 75,000 figure) cut out. Drift became a decision the moment the circle shrank to the men who already agreed. Watch for both gears in every later chapter: patronage that cannot command, and rooms that cannot dissent.

7. Open threads

At the boundary of 28 December 1979, these are the period's own open questions — not later history smuggled in, but what a well-informed observer in Kabul that week could not yet answer. Would the Afghan Army, already deserting in 1979, fight for Karmal or dissolve and leave the 40th Army holding the cities alone? Would the insurgency, so far a patchwork of rural revolts, find outside patrons capable of sustaining it — and would Pakistan's pipeline, barely visible in 1979, become its lifeline? Would Washington's answer be the sanctions-and-boycott package already being drafted, or something covert and larger? And did Moscow intend a short "limited contingent" operation — the phrase in every December document — or had it just bought a war with no exit doctrine? The documents decide none of these. The next ten years do.

8. Retrieval check

Answer from memory — effortful recall is the point. Immediate feedback below each question.

Which treaty gave Moscow the legal language it later used to justify sending troops?

Article 4 of the 5 December 1978 twenty-year treaty, paired with UN Charter Article 51 and staged Afghan "requests," was the published justification. Rawalpindi (1919) ended the Third Anglo-Afghan War; Geneva (1982+) and SALT II belong to other stories.

Why did the Politburo refuse troops in March 1979?

Kosygin led the refusal: mixing Soviet with Afghan units would internationalise the war and destroy détente. In March Amin was still Taraki's deputy — nobody was trusting him with anything yet.

What turned Moscow toward invasion in autumn 1979?

Taraki's 8 October murder (Brezhnev's personal turning point) plus Amin's 27 October Blood meeting convinced Andropov's circle that Amin would flip to Washington. Karmal signed nothing; Daoud was dead; Carter recognised, never endorsed, the regime.

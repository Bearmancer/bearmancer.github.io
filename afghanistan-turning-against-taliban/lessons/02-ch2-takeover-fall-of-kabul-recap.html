Takeover and the Broken Bargain

The video defines a social contract as largely unwritten terms: people accept authority and taxation, while governments keep living standards, order, and services running. The International IDEA explainer on social contracts, one of the works the video's own apparatus points to, establishes the concept as a standard framing device.

The Taliban's version, the video says, was always "unconventional." A fundamentalist movement after decades of asymmetric insurgency promised theocratic rule, spoke openly of erasing women from society, and planned to rule by decree from a "supreme leader" with "virtually unlimited power." The BBC record of the 2021 takeover period covers the insurgency-to-power arc. The movement's inner intentions are the video's characterization, quoted here rather than asserted.

The video concedes that millions opposed the takeover, recalling Afghans who "tried to hold on to American cargo planes" during the withdrawal. The broad outline of that desperation is documented; the quoted details rest on the video alone.

The video's population of "40 to 45 million" holds in range: the World Bank population record puts Afghanistan at 43.8 million people in 2025. The "million-strong minority" phrasing and the claim that most of the country accepted the bargain, whether excited or resigned, are the video's own.

  • Authority for welfare: the exchange the rest of the course tests.
  • Unconventional contractor: a theocratic insurgency promising order.
  • Majority as the jury: the movement is judged by its supposed supporters, not just its opponents.

What the Social Contract Frame Does

  • It turns governance into debt: every failure reads as a broken promise.
  • It centers the majority: the verdict comes from the base, not the margins.
  • It sequences the case: economy first, repression second, revolt last.

Everything later in the course is argued inside this frame, so the frame deserves testing alongside the facts poured into it.

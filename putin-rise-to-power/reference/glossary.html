Canonical terms for this workspace. Definitions stay tight; where the wider field uses a loose term, the resolution is noted. Print-friendly.

People

Vladimir Putin
KGB officer (Leningrad Directorate → Dresden posting 1985–90), later St. Petersburg official, FSB director, prime minister, president. In this workspace, always distinguish Putin's own account from corroborated history.
Lyudmila Putina (née Shkrebneva)
Putin's wife 1983–2013; former Aeroflot flight attendant; lived in Dresden 1985–90. Second daughter Katerina was born in Dresden (31 Aug 1986).
Mikhail Gorbachev
Last Soviet leader (1985–91). Reform program perestroika + glasnost; refused to use force to save the East German regime in 1989.
Erich Honecker
East German leader 1971–89; opposed Gorbachev's reforms; out of office by Oct 1989. Avoid: "Honecker era" as shorthand for the whole GDR — he was the endpoint.
Anatoly Sobchak
Reformist Leningrad law professor and politician. In 1990 the KGB reassigned Putin from Dresden to shadow him; Sobchak later hired Putin — the bridge from spy to politician (lesson 02, pending).
Vladimir Usoltsev (documents name: Agartanov)
KGB colleague who shared an office with Putin in Dresden; later wrote a memoir (Sosluzhivets, 2003). Useful color — treat as a participant account, not neutral.
Erich Schmidt-Eenboom
German intelligence journalist; source of the 2011 "Lenchen" allegations (below). Cited by Munger; single-sourced — teach as allegation.
Douglas Selvage
Historian at the Stasi archive; the leading voice that the Dresden story has been "romanticized and mythologized" (LA Times, 2023). Use for the myth-critique lens.

Institutions & units

KGB
Soviet state security service 1954–91. In 1985–90 Putin served in its foreign-intelligence arm; First Chief Directorate.
First Chief Directorate (FCD)
KGB branch for foreign intelligence (Yasenevo). Vetted agent candidates forwarded by field posts like Dresden; controlled Directorate S.
Directorate S
FCD's "illegals" directorate: agents operating abroad under false identities. Putin's Dresden office belonged to it, on Line N.
Line N
The section handling recruitment of potential "illegal" agents. In Dresden this meant finding East Germans or foreigners with plausible Western travel cover, for vetting by the FCD.
KGB residency
The KGB's in-country office at an embassy/consulate; Dresden's secretly occupied floors of 4 Angelikastraße. Officers lived nearby and reported to Karlshorst (East Berlin).
Stasi (MfS)
East German Ministry for State Security — the GDR's secret police. Cooperated with, and was watched by, the KGB; its Dresden district HQ stood a few hundred metres from the KGB office.
BND
West Germany's foreign intelligence service. Reported (via Schmidt-Eenboom) to have had an informant — "Lenchen" — close to the Putins in Dresden. Source of the 2011 allegations.
Verfassungsschutz
German domestic intelligence service; second recipient of the Lenchen reports (after the BND).
COMECON (CMEA)
The Soviet-led "Council for Mutual Economic Assistance" (1949–91): the bloc's common market in name, a bilateral barter system in practice. Its price formula — a five-year rolling average of world prices — is what made Soviet oil a subsidy for decades. Avoid: "Soviet common market" as if it worked like the EEC.
Transferable ruble (TR)
The COMECON accounting unit on the books of the Moscow-based IBEC: not transferable between partners, not convertible, not money in any usable sense. IMF analysis of how it actually worked; the system dissolved 1 Jan 1991 when trade switched to world prices and convertible currencies, settled at roughly $0.90 per TR.

Places

Angelikastraße 4, Dresden
The villa housing the Dresden KGB office where Putin worked (upper floor, per Munger); today a Rudolf Steiner/Waldorf teacher-training center. Avoid: "Putin's Dresden bunker" — it was an office, not a fortress.
Karlshorst (East Berlin)
Site of the KGB's East German operations HQ; Dresden reported to it. Also where the German surrender was signed in 1945 — not relevant to Dresden coverage, care with search results.

Events

Berlin Wall, 9 Nov 1989
Border opened after mass pressure; the emblematic (not first or most decisive) turning point of the bloc's collapse. Putin watched from Dresden.
Dresden Stasi storming & KGB standoff, 5 Dec 1989
Crowds occupied the Stasi district HQ; a smaller group approached the KGB building. Putin's claim: he faced the crowd and called for army guards. Most accounts date it 5 Dec (Munger says 4th). See "Moscow is silent".
Chernobyl, Apr 1986
Nuclear disaster; historians treat it as a major milestone on the road to Soviet collapse. Context for the chapter's 1985–89 backdrop.
INF Treaty, Dec 1987
Reagan–Gorbachev intermediate-range nuclear forces treaty; Munger cites it as the product of glasnost-era negotiation.

Concepts & phrases

Perestroika
Gorbachev's domestic "restructuring": loosening economic controls (cooperatives, limited private business).
Glasnost
"Openness": loosened censorship and Cold War negotiation posture. The two reform tracks together are the chapter's backdrop.
"Moscow is silent"
Putin's account (First Person, 2000) of the army HQ reply when he asked for protection: "We cannot do anything without orders from Moscow. And Moscow is silent." The phrase he says left him with Soviet power as "a paralysis of power." Avoid: using it as a neutral historical fact — it is Putin's self-told story; verify against BBC/Daily Beast renderings.
Illegal (agent)
Intelligence officer or agent living abroad under a complete false identity, outside official cover. Line N's product line.
First Person
Putin's 2000 book of interviews (with Gevorkyan, Timakova, Kolesnikov). Primary source for his Dresden claims — written for his first presidential run; treat as carefully constructed self-presentation.
Bullshit job
Munger's (implied Graeber) term for Putin's Dresden work: busy, dossier-collecting, largely discarded. Munger's own framing — the record adds nuance (see lesson 01, "Reading it three ways").
Buffer (buffer zone)
In this workspace: the ring of East European satellite states kept as strategic depth — forward defense for the USSR's western approaches and a quarantine against outside influence, per Soviet military doctrine. A shield in military terms, a recurring bill in economic terms (see the deep-dive "The collapse, 1985–89", §0). Avoid: reading "buffer" as neutral geography — here it is a policy with a price.

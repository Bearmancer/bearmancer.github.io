Glossary — as of chapter 18

As of chapter 18 (Conclusion) · lessons must use these names

Canonical terms for this workspace. Definitions stay tight; where the wider field uses a loose term, the resolution is noted. Print-friendly.

People

Vladimir Putin
KGB officer (Leningrad Directorate → Dresden posting 1985–90), later St. Petersburg official, FSB director, prime minister, president. In this workspace, always distinguish Putin's own account from corroborated history.
Lyudmila Putina (née Shkrebneva)
Putin's wife 1983–2013; former Aeroflot flight attendant; lived in Dresden 1985–90. Second daughter Katerina was born in Dresden (31 Aug 1986).
Mikhail Gorbachev
Last Soviet leader (1985–91). Reform program perestroika + glasnost; refused to use force to save the East German regime in 1989.
Erich Honecker
East German leader 1971–89; opposed Gorbachev's reforms; out of office by Oct 1989. Avoid: "Honecker era" as shorthand for the whole GDR — he was the endpoint.
Anatoly Sobchak
Reformist Leningrad law professor and politician. In 1990 the KGB reassigned Putin from Dresden to shadow him; Sobchak later hired Putin — the bridge from spy to politician (lesson 02, pending).
Vladimir Usoltsev (documents name: Agartanov)
KGB colleague who shared an office with Putin in Dresden; later wrote a memoir (Sosluzhivets, 2003). Useful color — treat as a participant account, not neutral.
Erich Schmidt-Eenboom
German intelligence journalist; source of the 2011 "Lenchen" allegations (below). Cited by Munger; single-sourced — teach as allegation.
Douglas Selvage
Historian at the Stasi archive; the leading voice that the Dresden story has been "romanticized and mythologized" (LA Times, 2023). Use for the myth-critique lens.

Institutions & units

KGB
Soviet state security service 1954–91. In 1985–90 Putin served in its foreign-intelligence arm; First Chief Directorate.
First Chief Directorate (FCD)
KGB branch for foreign intelligence (Yasenevo). Vetted agent candidates forwarded by field posts like Dresden; controlled Directorate S.
Directorate S
FCD's "illegals" directorate: agents operating abroad under false identities. Putin's Dresden office belonged to it, on Line N.
Line N
The section handling recruitment of potential "illegal" agents. In Dresden this meant finding East Germans or foreigners with plausible Western travel cover, for vetting by the FCD.
KGB residency
The KGB's in-country office at an embassy/consulate; Dresden's secretly occupied floors of 4 Angelikastraße. Officers lived nearby and reported to Karlshorst (East Berlin).
Stasi (MfS)
East German Ministry for State Security — the GDR's secret police. Cooperated with, and was watched by, the KGB; its Dresden district HQ stood a few hundred metres from the KGB office.
BND
West Germany's foreign intelligence service. Reported (via Schmidt-Eenboom) to have had an informant — "Lenchen" — close to the Putins in Dresden. Source of the 2011 allegations.
Verfassungsschutz
German domestic intelligence service; second recipient of the Lenchen reports (after the BND).
COMECON (CMEA)
The Soviet-led "Council for Mutual Economic Assistance" (1949–91): the bloc's common market in name, a bilateral barter system in practice. Its price formula — a five-year rolling average of world prices — is what made Soviet oil a subsidy for decades. Avoid: "Soviet common market" as if it worked like the EEC.
Transferable ruble (TR)
The COMECON accounting unit on the books of the Moscow-based IBEC: not transferable between partners, not convertible, not money in any usable sense. IMF analysis of how it actually worked; the system dissolved 1 Jan 1991 when trade switched to world prices and convertible currencies, settled at roughly $0.90 per TR.

Places

Angelikastraße 4, Dresden
The villa housing the Dresden KGB office where Putin worked (upper floor, per Munger); today a Rudolf Steiner/Waldorf teacher-training center. Avoid: "Putin's Dresden bunker" — it was an office, not a fortress.
Karlshorst (East Berlin)
Site of the KGB's East German operations HQ; Dresden reported to it. Also where the German surrender was signed in 1945 — not relevant to Dresden coverage, care with search results.

Events

Berlin Wall, 9 Nov 1989
Border opened after mass pressure; the emblematic (not first or most decisive) turning point of the bloc's collapse. Putin watched from Dresden.
Dresden Stasi storming & KGB standoff, 5 Dec 1989
Crowds occupied the Stasi district HQ; a smaller group approached the KGB building. Putin's claim: he faced the crowd and called for army guards. Most accounts date it 5 Dec (Munger says 4th). See "Moscow is silent".
Chernobyl, Apr 1986
Nuclear disaster; historians treat it as a major milestone on the road to Soviet collapse. Context for the chapter's 1985–89 backdrop.
INF Treaty, Dec 1987
Reagan–Gorbachev intermediate-range nuclear forces treaty; Munger cites it as the product of glasnost-era negotiation.

Concepts & phrases

Perestroika
Gorbachev's domestic "restructuring": loosening economic controls (cooperatives, limited private business).
Glasnost
"Openness": loosened censorship and Cold War negotiation posture. The two reform tracks together are the chapter's backdrop.
"Moscow is silent"
Putin's account (First Person, 2000) of the army HQ reply when he asked for protection: "We cannot do anything without orders from Moscow. And Moscow is silent." The phrase he says left him with Soviet power as "a paralysis of power." Avoid: using it as a neutral historical fact — it is Putin's self-told story; verify against BBC/Daily Beast renderings.
Illegal (agent)
Intelligence officer or agent living abroad under a complete false identity, outside official cover. Line N's product line.
First Person
Putin's 2000 book of interviews (with Gevorkyan, Timakova, Kolesnikov). Primary source for his Dresden claims — written for his first presidential run; treat as carefully constructed self-presentation.
Bullshit job
Munger's (implied Graeber) term for Putin's Dresden work: busy, dossier-collecting, largely discarded. Munger's own framing — the record adds nuance (see lesson 01, "Reading it three ways").
Buffer (buffer zone)
In this workspace: the ring of East European satellite states kept as strategic depth — forward defense for the USSR's western approaches and a quarantine against outside influence, per Soviet military doctrine. A shield in military terms, a recurring bill in economic terms (see the deep-dive "The collapse, 1985–89", §0). Avoid: reading "buffer" as neutral geography — here it is a policy with a price.

Additions as of chapters 10–18 (ch7 base above intact)

Active reserve
KGB/FSB status letting an officer serve in civilian posts while remaining on the service's books. Putin's 1990 recall to Leningrad placed him in it; the channel into Sobchak's administration (ch7).
Furnace-burst account
Putin's First Person claim that on the night of 5 Dec 1989 he personally burned so much material the Dresden furnace burst. Teach as [P] self-account, never bare fact (ch7).
Lenchen / Balcony (2011 allegation)
Alleged BND informant close to the Putin household in Dresden, reported via journalist Erich Schmidt-Eenboom (2011 press flap; Verfassungsschutz corroboration claimed). Single-sourced — teach as allegation only (ch7).
Rainer Sonntag (contested handling)
Dresden-era recruitment case handled via Stasi channels; affirming (CORRECTIV, Atavist) vs dissenting (Spiegel file reading) accounts. Teach as contested range, never one line (ch7).
UN non-intervention signal (7 Dec 1988)
Gorbachev's UN speech renouncing force as an instrument of foreign policy plus a 500,000 troop cut; the signal the satellite regimes read as abandonment (ch7).
Vladimir Kumarin
Head of the Tambov group; PTK deputy president 1998–99. Named in the Gryzlov Aug 2001 "up to 100 enterprises" charge (ch11).
Alexander Malyshev
Head of a second St. Petersburg gang; arrested night of 5–6 Oct 1992 (28 netted). No source ties Putin to causing any later acquittal — do not teach causation (ch11).
PTK (Petersburg Fuel Company)
Founded Sep 1994; awarded by deputy-mayor Putin the sole city gasoline-supply right (ambulances, police, buses, taxis) (ch11).
SPAG
St. Petersburg real-estate firm; Putin on its advisory board (honorary, from 1992) to Mar 2000. May 2003 raids on 28 premises; probe closed 2009 on proscription. Co-founder Ritter indicted on money-laundering charges (ch11).
James Baker ("not one inch", 9 Feb 1990)
US Secretary of State; used the "not one inch eastward" formula thrice with Gorbachev on 9 Feb 1990. Oral assurances, never a written treaty pledge (ch12).
Yeltsin decree dissolving Congress (21 Sep 1993)
Yeltsin's dissolution of the Congress/Supreme Soviet without legal authority; deputies barricaded the White House (ch12).
White House shelling (4 Oct 1993)
Tank shelling of the Moscow White House at sunrise 4 Oct 1993 (not Oct 2). Toll: 187 official, up to 2,000 claimed — teach range only (ch12).
LDPR 1993 result (22.92%, 64 seats)
Zhirinovsky's party took 22.92% and 64 faction seats in the Dec 1993 Duma vote (Choice of Russia 62, CPRF 42) (ch12).
First Chechen War entry (11 Dec 1994)
Ultimatum 29 Nov 1994; ground entry 11 Dec 1994 (not November). Grozny taken by Mar 1995; control never established (ch12).
Pavel Grachev boast
Defence minister's quote that a single airborne regiment could seize Grozny in a couple of hours (ch12).
Akhmat Kadyrov
Chechnya's mufti 1995, jihad caller, sided with Moscow 1999; killed 9 May 2004 in the Dynamo stadium blast (ch12).
Chechen deportation (23 Feb 1944)
Stalin's deportation of ~500k Chechens/Ingush; mortality ~30–35%, never the video's 60% (ch12).
Diphtheria epidemic
St. Petersburg city+oblast 246 cases in 1991; 115,000+ cases across the former USSR 1990–97 (CDC). Dysentery/blackout figures unattested (ch12).
Gennady Zyuganov
CPRF chairman since 1993; 1996 challenger (32.49% first round, 40.73% runoff); distant second in Mar 2000; party supports the war on Ukraine (ch13, ch18).
Vladimir Zhirinovsky (1996 fifth; LDPR 1991–2022)
LDPR nationalist; fifth in 1996 with 5.79%. Party routinely voted with United Russia; died Apr 6 2022; Putin attended the funeral (ch13, ch18).
Alexander Korzhakov (postpone call, fired 20 Jun 1996)
Yeltsin bodyguard chief; called for postponing the 1996 election by two years; sacked with Barsukov and Soskovets 20 Jun 1996 (ch13).
Oleg Soskovets (nationalist-pivot strategy)
Author of the dropped nationalist-pivot campaign strategy; abandoned for Chubais's plan mid-Mar 1996 (ch13).
Tatyana Dyachenko
Yeltsin's daughter; behind-the-scenes campaign role in 1996; recipient of the American consultants' memos (ch13).
Alexander Lebed (14.73% third, kingmaker)
Third-place 1996 finisher; his voters broke for Yeltsin in the runoff (ch13).
Vladimir Yakovlev (St. Petersburg governor)
Fellow deputy mayor under Sobchak; beat him 47.5 to 45.8 in the 2 Jun 1996 runoff (28,000-vote gap) (ch13).
Lyudmila Narusova
Sobchak's wife; 1996 campaign role. "Wasted money / embarrassing posters" specifics unattested (ch13).
Vladimir Litvinenko (Mining rector)
St. Petersburg Mining Institute rector since the 1990s; oversaw Putin's 1996 dissertation work (alleged plagiarism/ghostwriting per Brookings and Olga Litvinenko's allegation); campaign manager 2000 and 2004 (ch13, ch18).
Ozero (10/11 Nov 1996, 8 founders)
Dacha cooperative registered 10–11 Nov 1996 at Solovyovka/Lake Komsomolskoye; eight founders incl Putin, Smirnov, Yakunin, Fursenkos, Kovalchuk, Myachin, Shamalov (ch13).
Yury Kovalchuk (Bank Rossiya)
Ozero co-founder; largest shareholder of Bank Rossiya, the network's cashbox per the Panama Papers (ch13).
Vladimir Yakunin (RZD president 2005–2015)
Ozero co-founder; Russian Railways president Jun 2005–Aug 2015. Correction: deputy minister Oct 2000, never transport minister (ch13).
Zelimkhan Yandarbiyev (27 May 1996 ceasefire)
Chechen counterpart in the May 1996 pre-election ceasefire; halt from midnight 31 May, status untouched (ch13).
IMF $10.2bn (Feb 1996)
Loan agreed Feb 1996, first tranche by April, pre-election. Clinton-leveraged motive contested; IMF denied politics (ch13).
Yevgeny Primakov (PM 11 Sep 1998–12 May 1999)
Ex-foreign minister/intelligence chief; premier after the 17 Aug 1998 default; sacked 12 May 1999 (stated economy reasons; impeachment-eve and popularity-threat readings contested) (ch15).
Sergei Stepashin (PM 12 May–9 Aug 1999)
FSB head turned premier; three-month stopgap; sacked 9 Aug 1999 amid the Dagestan counterattack (ch15).
Alexander Litvinenko (Nov 1998 press conference; 2006 finding)
FSB officer who alleged a 1998 order to kill Berezovsky; Nov 1998 press conference. Fled Oct–Nov 2000; poisoned with polonium-210 Nov 1 2006, died Nov 23. Owen inquiry: "probably approved" by Putin/Patrushev — teach as finding, never bare fact (ch15, ch16, ch18).
Berezovsky–Primakov war (1999)
Open 1999 battle: CIS sacking, Aeroflot/Andava arrest warrant, mutual public attacks; ended with Primakov's fall (ch15).
NATO-1999 (12 Mar three members; Allied Force 24 Mar–10 Jun)
Czech/Hungary/Poland acceded 12 Mar 1999; Operation Allied Force 24 Mar–10 Jun 1999 (78 days) to halt the Kosovo catastrophe (ch15).
Dagestan (7 Aug 1999 invasion)
Basayev+Khattab force (up to 2,000) seized mountain villages in support of Dagestan separatists; retreat by mid-Sep (ch15).
Putin PM (9 Aug appointed, 16 Aug 233-84-17)
Named acting premier and successor-apparent 9 Aug 1999; Duma confirmed 16 Aug (233 for, 84 against, 17 abstained; 226 needed) (ch15).
Gorki-9 ([P] summons)
Putin's account of Yeltsin's Aug 1999 summons days after his father's death; dialogue beyond his telling has no verdict (ch15).
Guryanova St (9 Sep 1999, 94–106 killed)
Nine-story block bombing; teach toll as range 94–106 killed, ~249 injured, never 106 alone (ch16).
Kashirskoye (13 Sep 1999, 118–124)
Second Moscow block bombing; teach 118–124 killed (ch16).
Buynaksk (4 Sep 1999, 62–64)
Car bomb; teach 62–64 killed, 133–174 injured (ch16).
Volgodonsk (16 Sep 1999, 17–19)
Fourth block bombing; teach 17–19 killed, 500+ injured (ch16).
Ryazan (22–24 Sep 1999, contested)
Three sacks, timer set ~5:30am, evacuation; men flashed FSB cards, released after a Moscow call; Patrushev 24 Sep "training exercise, only sugar" vs Tkachenko "live bomb" hexogen finding. Three live readings (live-bomb, Vympel exercise, staged-find); choose none (ch16).
Achemez Gochiyaev (ethnic Karachay, at large)
Officially named bombing commander; 13 Sep 1999 warning call led to two defusals (Borisovskiye Prudy, Kapotnya); at large if alive (ch16).
Khattab / Abu Omar al-Saif (official planners)
Per the official 2002 finding, ordered the bombings in retaliation for the Dagestan counteroffensive (ch16).
Nikolai Patrushev (24 Sep exercise announcement)
FSB chief; announced Ryazan was a training exercise with sugar (ch16).
Outhouse line (24 Sep 1999 Astana)
"We will pursue them everywhere... We'll wipe them out in the outhouse." Substance confirmed; exact approval-number arcs avoided (ch16).
Second Chechen War (Aug 1999–Apr 2009)
Late-Aug air campaign; 23 Sep Grozny raids; siege Dec 1999–Feb 2000. Retaliation narrative and Stepashin's pre-plan admission (decision from March) both taught (ch16).
APEC Auckland (Sep 1999 substitution)
Yeltsin cancelled; Putin went in his place. Sep 12 Auckland bilateral with Clinton; Yeltsin tipped Putin as probably-next-president (ch17).
Unity / Yedinstvo (Sep–Dec 1999, 72–73 seats)
Pro-Putin bloc formed three months before the vote; 72–73 seats (IPU 72 vs Essex/wiki 73 — teach range) (ch17).
CPRF 113/450 (Dec 19 1999)
Communists took 113 of 450 Duma seats (ch17).
ORT / Berezovsky (dirty-campaign charge + denial)
Charge: ORT ran the discredit drive for Unity/Putin, Berezovsky funds financed it. Denied by Gryzlov/Veshnyakov — teach with denial inline (ch17).
Dec 14–22 resignation-offer dispute
Yeltsin memoir Dec 14 vs Bulavinov/Moroz Dec 22 vs Putin "about ten days before New Year." Date disputed; substance confirmed (ch17).
Sunflower-seeds reply ([P])
Putin's self-accounted answer to the resignation offer: "foolish to say, no, I am going to go and sell sunflower seeds instead" (ch17).
Millennium message (Dec 30 1999)
"Russia at the Threshold of the Millennium" on the cabinet site; strong-state thesis (ch17).
Take-care-of-Russia (Dec 31 parting words)
Yeltsin's reported farewell to Putin, carried by three carriers as Putin-reported (ch17).
Forgiveness plea (Dec 31 1999 speech)
"I want to ask you for forgiveness, because many of our hopes have not come true" (BBC reprint) (ch17).
Mar 26 2000 election (June brought forward)
Resignation moved the vote forward three months; incumbency head-start mechanism (ch17).
Khasavyurt (Aug 30 1996)
Lebed–Maskhadov accord ending the First Chechen War; status deferred (ch17).
Putin parents (father Aug 1999, mother 1998)
Vladimir Spiridonovich died Aug 1999, the week of the PM appointment; Maria Ivanovna (1911–1998) died a year and a half earlier (ch18).
Vera Putina (Metekhi claim, denied, May 2023)
From 1999 claimed Putin ("Vova") is her son; Peskov denied. Died Tbilisi May 2023, age 96–97 (teach range) (ch18).
Lyudmila Putina (divorce Jun 6 2013; remarried early 2015)
Divorce announced Jun 6 2013 after 30 years; remarried Artur Ocheretny early 2015. Curator of a Russian-language fund. Traditionalist-friction motive unverified (ch18).
Maria Vorontsova (pediatric endocrinologist)
Elder daughter; endocrinologist, NOMEKO/genetics reporting; Kremlin never confirms paternity — keep attribution (ch18).
Katerina Tikhonova (MSU / Innopraktika)
Younger daughter; tech executive, MSU-affiliated foundation; math/physics reporting as reported (ch18).
Anatoly Sobchak (Nov 1997 Paris flight; death 19–20 Feb 2000)
Fled Nov 1997 flat scandal; case closed 1999 (credited to Putin); died 19–20 Feb 2000 (teach range) campaigning for Putin. Cause: autopsy heart failure vs Vaksberg poisoning claim — contested (ch18).
Anatoly Chubais (RAO UES to ~2008; Rosnano 2008–2020; resigned Mar 2022)
Ran UES, then Rosnano from 2008 (replaced Dec 2020); resigned Mar 23 2022 over the war and left Russia; Israel sighting, Italy hospitalization. "Day after" and "strategic-grounds opposition" rejected (ch18).
Boris Berezovsky (exile; Berkshire Mar 23 2013)
ORT kingmaker to Kursk fallout (sell-or-jail) to London exile; Ignite investor from 2003; mend-fences letter 2013. Death consistent with hanging, no violent struggle, no third-party evidence; murder rumors carried as rumors (ch18).
Boris Yeltsin (death Apr 23 2007)
12-day admission; died of heart failure Apr 23 2007. Apr 25 national mourning; first Orthodox head-of-state funeral since Alexander III. The 2% approval figure stays the video's, never record (ch18).

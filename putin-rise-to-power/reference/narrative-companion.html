Companion for watching · covers every chapter of the video, 1–18 · no tables, no figures, no arithmetic · each chapter ends with a one-line money note so the economics is never missing, only deferred · links point to the deeper documents

This is the context a viewer needs, chapter by chapter: when and where things happen, who the actors are, what each side wanted, and what was at stake. It is deliberately not a summary of the video’s claims — those get checked in the lessons, against the record. Where you want the mechanism behind a sentence, the money note gives you one line, and the condensed economics or the deep-dive gives you the rest.

Part 0 — the whole arc in sixty seconds

A boy is born in a ruined city that survived a nine-hundred-day siege. He grows up in a communal apartment, learns to fight, studies law, and joins the state security service — the one institution that promises order in a country that runs on it. He is posted abroad, in a small German city, and from there watches the empire he serves come apart without a shot. He comes home into a state that is dissolving, attaches himself to a reformist politician, and becomes a city official. The 1990s destroy the country’s savings, its factories and its faith, and hand its wealth to a handful of men. At the end of that decade, an ailing president and the men around him need a successor nobody has heard of, who is loyal, competent, and safe. He becomes acting president on the last day of 1999 — which is where the video begins.

Everything below is the same arc, chapter by chapter. Read the block for a chapter before you watch it.

Chapters 1–6 · the making of the man (1945–1985)

Chapter 1 · December 31, 1999 — the ending that opens the film

Where you are: a tired president resigns on live television and his prime minister becomes acting head of state. Who: Boris Yeltsin, the first elected president of Russia — large, ill, unpopular; and Vladimir Putin, his quiet successor. What’s at stake: who holds Russia’s wealth and who is protected from what the 1990s did. The film starts at the end so you watch everything that follows as the answer to one question: how did this man get here.

Money note: he inherits a bankrupt-feeling state that has just begun to recover — the recovery, not the collapse, is the luck of his timing. → condensed, Ch. 17–18

Chapter 2 · The Siege of Leningrad — a childhood shaped by memory

Where you are: Leningrad, the city that spent nearly nine hundred days encircled by German troops in the Second World War, when hundreds of thousands died, most of them of hunger. Who: Putin’s parents, who survived it; a brother who did not; a family that had already lost most of what it had. What’s at stake: the emotional furniture of the whole story — the war as a founding trauma, the conviction that outside forces are a threat, the prestige that came with having suffered it. The film gives you the city’s wounds, not the front line.

Money note: the siege is the family’s memory of scarcity — an era when state distribution of food was literally the difference between life and death. → deep-dive, Part 0

Chapter 3 · The Young Brawler — courtyards and fighting

Where you are: a Leningrad communal flat and the courtyard where children fought, in a city with few opportunities and much bravado. Who: a small boy who learns that being underestimated is a weapon, and takes up sambo and then judo — disciplines that reward patience, leverage and reading your opponent. What’s at stake: not sport medals — a temperament. The film’s portrait of the adult rests on these scenes: cautious, watchful, physically trained, not interested in fair fights.

Money note: nothing here yet — this is a poor country’s childhood: shared kitchens, hand-me-downs, a state that promises much and provides little. → condensed, the six ideas

Chapter 4 · Growing Up Red — the world he was promised

Where you are: the Soviet Union of the 1960s and 1970s — school, the Pioneers, the Komsomol ladder, patriotic history, and an official story in which everything is improving. Who: Putin as a normal, unremarkable schoolboy with a taste for the KGB’s glamour (the film keeps the boy’s own claim that he asked to join as a schoolchild). What’s at stake: the gap between the promise and the observation. By the 1970s the country had ceased to grow, shortages were normal, and the honest private joke was that everyone pretended to work while the state pretended to pay them.

Money note: the stagnation that later makes reform unavoidable begins in these years — the system is not collapsing yet, it is quietly corroding. → deep-dive, §1.1

Chapter 5 · Soviet Law — the ladder out

Where you are: Leningrad State University’s law faculty — a respectable career and, more importantly, the correct address for someone who wants the security services. Who: the young Putin, and his teacher — the reformist lawyer Anatoly Sobchak, a connection planted here that pays off in Chapter 8. What’s at stake: how careers actually worked: institutions recruited by hand, through people who vouched for you.

Money note: the state was the only employer that mattered; positions were assigned, and influence was the currency. Remember this when the 1990s invert it. → condensed, Ch. 11

Chapter 6 · The Early KGB Years — the institution he joined

Where you are: 1975: Putin joins the KGB, trains, and is posted to the Leningrad directorate, working counter-intelligence and watching foreigners and dissidents. Who: a competent, unglamorous officer who is later selected for foreign intelligence and prepared for work abroad. What’s at stake: the KGB was not merely a spy agency — it was the state’s nervous system, and, for its officers, a promised career, privilege and clarity in a system short on all three. That promise is what makes the next chapter’s collapse personal.

Money note: KGB service brought access — a flat, a car, foreign postings — in an economy where access was worth more than money. → Lesson 0001

Chapters 7–8 · the empire and the exit (1985–1991)

Chapter 7 · Dresden — watching the wall fall

Where you are: 1985–1990, East Germany, where Putin works in a KGB office in a villa in Dresden, disguised as a translator. His actual work — collecting contacts and proposals for foreign intelligence — produces little. What’s at stake: the bloc is coming apart around him. Poland’s regime negotiates its way out; Hungary cuts its border fence; tens of thousands of East Germans leave; the demonstrations grow; Moscow refuses to send troops. In December 1989 crowds sack the local Stasi headquarters, and Putin — by his own account — stands outside the KGB building while nobody in Moscow answers the phone. What to watch for: how the film tells a famous scene (the crowd, the burning documents, “Moscow is silent”) and how much of it rests on Putin’s own retelling. Lesson 0001 sorts the three layers.

Money note: the reason Moscow let the bloc go was not generosity — the state had no money to keep it and would not shoot for it. One line, enormous consequence. → deep-dive, Part 3

Chapter 8 · Sobchak — from officer to official

Where you are: February 1990: Putin comes home to Leningrad, where his old law professor Sobchak is now the city’s leading reform politician. The KGB sends Putin to watch him; Sobchak hires him instead. What’s at stake: a country running out of food, money and authority. Leningrad begins rationing; factories fail to deliver to each other; the republics stop handing their taxes to the centre; the bloc’s trade system ends. In August 1991 hardliners try to seize power, the coup fails, and the Union begins to fall apart. Putin resigns from the KGB and stays with Sobchak. What to watch for: this is the hinge of the biography — the moment the service stops being a future.

Money note: the officers’ exit into politics and business was driven by budget cuts and institutional collapse, not ideology. → condensed, Ch. 8

Chapters 9–13 · the 1990s (1991–1996)

Chapter 9 · 1991 — the country ends, prices are freed

Where you are: the Soviet Union is dissolved in December 1991 and Russia emerges as its largest successor. Within weeks the new government frees prices and begins to dismantle central planning. Who: Yeltsin, the new president, and the young economists around him; Putin, meanwhile, has become a deputy to Sobchak in St Petersburg, in charge of the city’s dealings with the outside world. What’s at stake: the entire shape of ordinary life — jobs, savings, salaries, pensions, and the meaning of an apartment or a factory.

Money note: freeing prices while paying everyone the same money as before destroyed savings within months; this is the trauma the rest of the decade is measured against. → condensed, Ch. 9

Chapter 10 · The Big Swindle — who got the factories

Where you are: 1992–1995, and the state is handing its industry to private owners — first through vouchers given to every citizen, then through deals that favour those with money and information. Who: the reformers who ran privatisation, the factory managers who gained control, and the small group of financiers who would soon be called oligarchs. What’s at stake: the country’s wealth, and the question of legitimacy — whether Russians would accept that this ownership was fair. Most concluded it was not, and the chapter takes its title from that verdict.

Money note: the state sold its assets cheap because the state was desperate for cash and the buyers helped write the rules. → condensed, Ch. 10

Chapter 11 · Putin’s Rolodex — networks as capital

Where you are: St Petersburg, where Putin works through the 1990s as a city official — licensing, foreign investment, trade, property. What’s at stake: in an economy where banks are untrustworthy and factories pay in goods rather than cash, what matters is whom you know and what they can authorise: a permit, a quota, a customs route, protection. His address book — KGB colleagues, judo friends, city officials, foreign businessmen — becomes an asset he never has to declare.

Money note: the “rolodex” is economic infrastructure: with cash scarce, access was the currency. → condensed, Ch. 11

Chapter 12 · The Time Of Troubles — the decade at its worst

Where you are: the mid-1990s: factories that don’t pay wages, workers who barter for food, taxes the state cannot collect, and an unprecedented peacetime fall in how long Russians live. Who: a government kept afloat by foreign loans, a population surviving on informal exchange, and criminal groups on the edges. What’s at stake: the state’s ability to do anything at all — pay teachers, hold its territory, keep its promises. The title is the old Russian name for an era of chaos and foreign-backed false claimants.

Money note: the state stops collecting taxes, so it stops paying salaries and pensions — that is what the “Troubles” feel like at street level. → condensed, Ch. 12

Chapter 13 · 1996 — an election that decides everything

Where you are: a presidential election Yeltsin looks certain to lose to the communists, who would reverse privatisation. What’s at stake: whether the new owners keep what they acquired — which is why the bankers fund the campaign, the media is mobilised, and the loans-for-shares deals are arranged: the state borrows money from a small group of financiers, and the collateral is the country’s largest companies.

Money note: the election is bought with shares of industry — the transaction that makes the oligarchs a political class. → condensed, Ch. 13

Chapters 14–17 · from court to throne (1997–2000)

Chapter 14 · At Yeltsin’s Court — Putin enters the Kremlin

Where you are: 1997–1999. Putin moves from St Petersburg to Moscow, enters the presidential administration, then runs the security service, then the security council. Who: Yeltsin, increasingly ill; his family and advisers; the oligarchs and officials who compete around him. What’s at stake: the succession, and the safety it implies — for the president’s family, for the men who ran privatisation, for the new owners of the largest companies. In a court like this, loyalty and discretion become the most valuable qualifications in the country.

Money note: the courtiers are protecting wealth, not ideology — which is why an obscure, loyal official is the safest choice. → condensed, Ch. 14

Chapter 15 · Endgame For Power — choosing the unknown

Where you are: 1999, and the search for someone who can be elected and will not turn on his patrons. Several candidates are tried and dropped; an older politician with a wider base is kept out; in August Putin is made prime minister. What’s at stake: the survival of the arrangements of the 1990s, and the personal fates of the people who made them. What to watch for: the mechanics of a succession inside a weakening state — nothing about it is inevitable.

Money note: the guarantee sought by the outgoing court is legal and economic: no revisiting privatisation, no prosecution of the family. → condensed, Ch. 15

Chapter 16 · The Apartment Bombings — the turn to war

Where you are: August–September 1999: bombs destroy apartment blocks in Moscow and other cities, hundreds die, and the country is gripped by fear of terrorism traced to the North Caucasus. Federal forces re-enter Chechnya. What’s at stake: a new politics of security, and the popularity of the untested prime minister, whose standing rises with the war. What to watch for: how the film handles contested territory — the bombings’ origin, and the extent to which the war built the man.

Money note: the war is affordable because the economy has just begun recovering, one year after the country’s worst financial crisis. → condensed, Ch. 16

Chapter 17 · Coronation — the last day of 1999

Where you are: back where the film began. Yeltsin resigns on New Year’s Eve; Putin becomes acting president; within months he wins an election. Who: the same cast as Chapter 1, now explained; and a new one — the officials and officers who arrive with him. What’s at stake: the end of the decade’s uncertainty, and the beginning of a state that will use a rising oil price to rebuild itself.

Money note: he takes office as the country’s finances are being rescued by devaluation and oil — his inheritance is luck as much as loyalty. → condensed, Ch. 17

Chapter 18 · Conclusion — what the story is a story about

Where you are: the film steps back. The question it has been answering is not “who is Putin” but “how does a state, and a man formed inside it, respond to humiliation and disorder.” Every chapter feeds that: the besieged city, the disciplined service, the empire that walked away, the decade that dissolved salaries and rules, the court that needed discretion, the war that supplied popularity, the oil that supplied money. What’s at stake for you as a viewer: keeping the layers apart — what he says about himself, what the film says about him, and what the record will support.

Money note: the arc’s two ends are money stories — a state that could not pay its officers, then a state that cannot spend its oil revenue fast enough.

All the money notes in one paragraph

A besieged city taught a family that distribution decides survival; a stagnating planned economy made every career a matter of access; a service that promised order began to lose its budget at the very moment its empire ran out of dollars; the officers’ exit led to politicians who controlled real resources; freeing prices destroyed savings while handing factories to insiders; with cash scarce, access and networks became the economy; a state that could not collect taxes borrowed against its own industry and lost the auction; it defaulted, the currency collapsed, and devaluation plus oil brought the money back — allowing an obscure loyal officer to inherit a recovering state. That is the whole economics of the video: scarcity, access, and the return of money at the end.

Putin: The Rise to Power · chapter 13 of 18

1996

Chapter 13 of 18 · 1:55:38–2:08:19

Lesson 07 · cast map · glossary · transcript slice

The chapter's hinge year: Yeltsin, written off at single digits, claws back the presidency with spin, a Chechen ceasefire, captured airwaves and Western tailwinds — while across town Sobchak loses Petersburg, the defeat that pushes Putin to Moscow, a degree, and a lakeside cooperative that becomes the seed of his power.

The video's counterfactual is stated plainly: had Sobchak won, Putin would have stayed his deputy and never reached Yeltsin's government. This lesson walks the year, then sorts what the record confirms from what it corrects — verdicts inline beside the quoted wording, citations in §7.

1. What matters — and what you can skip

Keep these four essentials:

  1. Yeltsin won from single digits. January polling sat at 5–8%, fifth place; by 3 July he took 54.40% to Zyuganov's 40.73%. The machine that closed the gap — Chubais, Dyachenko, the oligarch airwaves, the May ceasefire, the February IMF loan — is the chapter's real subject.
  2. Petersburg fell 47.5 to 45.8. Yakovlev beat Sobchak in the 2 June runoff and Putin lost his patron. The video's hinge reading (defeat sends Putin to Moscow) is interpretation, but the mechanism is confirmed: Sobchak's win would have kept Putin chief among his first deputies.
  3. Paper and property followed. The 1997 candidate-of-sciences degree is plagiarism-confirmed (16-plus pages verbatim); who held the pen is allegation. The August 1996 Moscow posting under Borodin and the November 1996 Ozero registration are confirmed; the fire, the nightmares and the exact moving day are the video's account, unverified.
  4. Names need correcting first. The captions garble nearly every surname — Zyuganov, Zhirinovsky, Korzhakov, Soskovets, Chubais, Sobchak, Yakovlev, Narusova, Ozero. Canonical forms are used throughout; the corrections log sits in the verification record.

What can be skimmed: the pizza anecdote (colour, Clinton-attributed); the 2016-meddling parallel (the video's own analogy, no verdict); the platform-jumping chessboard close (interpretation, carried as frame). The loans-for-shares mechanics sit behind the media story and are cited, not re-argued.

3. Yeltsin's comeback — polls, health, war, airwaves, help

"Going into the 1996 election his approval rating was about 10%" — confirmed as a single-digits range, corrected down: teach 5–8%, never one number. "Yeltsin's approval rating was barely 5% in January" (TIME on the Satarov turnaround plan), and the field put him fifth at 8% behind Zyuganov's 21% (1996 election results and polling baseline).

"He'd had two heart attacks the previous year alone" is partially correct — teach hospitalisations, not two formal infarcts. Yeltsin was hospitalised in July 1995 for "an exacerbation of cardiac ischemia" (LA Times on the July 1995 episode), and the record shows a fortnight then, a month in October, plus an attack in June or July 1996 that went unreported at the time (BBC health record).

"The Secret Service found Yeltsin blind drunk wandering around Pennsylvania Avenue in the middle of the night in his boxer shorts trying to hail a cab so he could go get a pizza."

— the video, via Clinton

Confirmed as a Clinton-attributed account, never bare fact: agents found him "alone on Pennsylvania Avenue, dead drunk, clad in his underwear, yelling for a taxi" for pizza, with a second-night basement repeat (History.com on the Branch/Clinton Tapes telling). The visit dates to autumn 1994, not 1995 — the year is corrected.

"Yeltsin's chief opponents were Zyuganov the Communist and Zhirinovsky the Nationalist" — confirmed with the field corrected: Zhirinovsky finished fifth at 5.79% while Lebed (14.73%) and Yavlinsky (7.45%) stood between him and the runoff; the final was Yeltsin 35.79 to Zyuganov 32.49, then 54.40 to 40.73 on 3 July, per the election results page cited above (see §7).

"One of his campaign managers, Korzhakov, counselled that because Yeltsin couldn't win he should declare a state of emergency and postpone the election for 2 years" — confirmed. Korzhakov publicly called for postponement past 16 June (RFE/RL on the May 1996 postpone call); Yeltsin rebuked him and said the vote would go ahead "according to the constitution" (Spokesman on the rebuke); after the first round the emergency faction was purged — Korzhakov, Barsukov and Soskovets were fired amid charges of plotting to cancel the runoff (LA Times on the 20 June sackings).

"That morphed into the Soskovets strategy that urged Yeltsin to pull votes away from Zhirinovsky by borrowing his rhetoric" — confirmed with the name corrected (captions hear "sasich"; the fit is unique). The plan "involved him pivoting towards the nationalist wing in order to directly compete for votes with Zyuganov and Zhirinovsky" and "was ultimately abandoned in favor of one devised by reformists" (Soskovets-strategy account).

"Yeltsin turned to Chubais — privatisation man, ex-deputy mayor of Petersburg with Putin, later head of the electricity monopoly — whose strategy was to run as the alternative to backsliding, with daughter Tatyana managing behind the scenes" — confirmed, with one label stripped: Chubais ran things but was never an owner-oligarch himself. "His passionate arguments against [cancelling] swayed Yeltsin and led the President to put Chubais in charge of the campaign," in "a strong working relationship with Tatyana Dyachenko" (TIME on the March turnaround); he "oversaw the mass privatizations" and "returned to run Yeltsin's campaign," then became chief of staff (PBS on Chubais); as "head of Russia's state power monopoly" he is the privatisation symbol, not a share-grabber (BBC profile).

"To combat the poor-health perception Yeltsin famously danced on stage at a music festival" — confirmed, dated and placed: 10 June 1996 in Rostov, during Osin's set, the AP frame that won a Pulitzer (Vote-or-Lose campaign account). "In May 1996 he concluded a ceasefire in the Chechen war" — confirmed, with the emptiness kept: the parties "agreed to halt all military hostilities as of Saturday" (LA Times on the 27 May signing); it ran from midnight 31 May while "the issue of Chechnya's future status was not raised" (RFE/RL Newsline); the press read it as timed — "three weeks before the election" (Independent).

"They ensured positive coverage by the major TV networks with carrots and sticks — licence threats plus letting media moguls acquire state assets" — confirmed in mechanism. The state still owned two of three national channels and "newspaper licenses and Moscow leases were 'under review'"; NTV's Malashenko "joined the Yeltsin campaign" in open conflict of interest; cash for coverage ("dollar journalism") is documented (Belfer Center election watch). The carrot ran through privatisation: the loans-for-shares auctions of November–December 1995 made a Yeltsin win the investors' financial interest (loans-for-shares account). "This cemented oligarch control of the media" stands as the chapter's consequence claim.

"There were three American consultants including one who had advised Clinton, later a Goldblum movie" — confirmed. Gorton, Shumate and Dresner worked February to July on $250,000 plus expenses, routing unsigned memos to Dyachenko, with Dresner tied to Clinton's guru Dick Morris (TIME's Rescuing Boris); the dramatisation stars "Jeff Goldblum, Anthony LaPaglia, Liev Schreiber" (IMDb on Spinning Boris). "Secret loans by American interests" is unfindable — no source in five passes; do not teach it. The IMF half is confirmed with the motive split: a $10.2bn loan agreed in February with money flowing by April (Spokesman), which Zyuganov called "blatantly political" while the Fund denied election intent (RFE/RL). Clinton's personal lever stays interpretive.

"So in the summer of 1996 Yeltsin was reelected" — confirmed (3 July, 54.40% — §7). The 2016-meddling mirror ("what goes around") is the video's analogy and is carried as frame, uncited.

4. Petersburg falls — Sobchak vs Yakovlev

"Putin was working with Sobchak, himself running for reelection as mayor" — confirmed. "Most thought the city needed a change; the business community found Sobchak an arts-promoting intellectual at the economy's expense" — confirmed as Yakovlev's pitch: Sobchak's "patronage of the arts (with city money) prevented him from solving the real problems" (Sobchak account).

"His most important opponent was Vladimir Yakovlev, one of the other deputy mayors; Sobchak had been both Putin's and Yakovlev's common patron" — confirmed. Day-to-day control "was handled by two deputies — Vladimir Yakovlev and Vladimir Putin" (same Sobchak account as above — see §7), and Yakovlev is "his former deputy" turned challenger (UPI).

"Putin urged a professional team; Sobchak hired his wife Narusova, who completely screwed up the campaign" — mixed: roles confirmed, the management verdict unattested. "Kudrin assembled shifting groups; Lyudmila Narusova assembled others. Putin was similarly involved, but played no visible role," and "it is not true" that Putin headed the campaign (Moscow Times retrospective). The embarrassing-poster buy and the takedown are unfindable as specifics — do not quote them.

"By end of April Putin stepped in with working-class TV commercials, and the deputies signed a pledge to resign if defeated" — unverified. No source in five passes; teach both as the video's account, never as record.

"First round in late May, runoff between the top two; most expected Sobchak by a nose; a disastrous debate made him look aloof" — confirmed in outline, corrected on the tag. First-round coalitions formed "on the eve of the first round, May 19" (same UPI report as above — see §7); the live debate "turned hostile several times" (RFE/RL), and it broke for Yakovlev — "aloof and out of touch" is the video's gloss on a real defeat.

"Yakovlev got 47.5% of the vote, Sobchak 45.8%."

— the video on the runoff

Confirmed exactly, with the day corrected: the vote was Sunday 2 June, results announced the 3rd–4th. "47.5 percent compared to incumbent Sobchak's 45.8 percent. Turnout was 44 percent" (same RFE/RL report as above — see §7); "won by the narrow margin of 47.5 percent to 45.8 percent" (Jamestown); "a runoff by a slim two percentage points" (LA Times); a 28,000-vote gap with "a deathly pale Putin" watching the count (same Moscow Times piece as above — see §7).

"This result literally changed the world — had Sobchak won, Putin would have stayed, never reaching Yeltsin's government or the 1999 premiership" — no verdict; it is counterfactual interpretation. The mechanism is confirmed ("had Sobchak won, he would have kept Putin as chief among his first deputies"), the "most important contest in recent world history" superlative is the video's own.

5. Paperwork and property — degree, Moscow, Ozero

"In spring 1996 he enrolled at the Mining Institute for a candidate-of-sciences degree" — unverified as dated; the degree itself is confirmed (1997). "Putin received his candidate's degree in economics from the St. Petersburg Mining Institute in 1997" (RFE/RL on the Brookings findings).

"The dissertation was fake — plagiarised from a textbook, cobbled from sources, compiled by director Litvinenko" — confirmed as a plagiarism finding; the pen-holder is allegation. "More than 16 pages of text taken verbatim" plus "six diagrams and tables lifted directly with no attribution" (same RFE/RL Brookings report as above — see §7). That Litvinenko personally wrote it — cutting, gluing, vacation dacha, Putin never consulting — is Olga Litvinenko's allegation, which her father denies (RFE/RL on the father–daughter dispute); the same account confirms his later campaign-manager role and PhosAgro stake. "Vladimir Litvinenko should not be confused with Alexander Litvinenko, murdered by Putin in 2006 with polonium" — confirmed with attribution corrected: the ex-spy was poisoned 1 November 2006 and died 23 November, and the 2016 inquiry found his killing "probably approved" by Putin — an attributed finding, never a bare fact (poisoning account); "no relation, so far as I know" is correct.

"Chubais, now in Moscow, called Putin; his name was floated as a go-to fixer; ex-Sobchak people were jumping" — partially correct, hedged: the channel is reported as "some say," the post is solid. "After Sobchak lost in June 1996 Putin quit; in August 1996 he was transferred as deputy chief of the presidential property directorate under Borodin, supervising law and foreign property, and moved to Moscow with his family" (TASS biography). "Head of the legal department of the General Affairs office, in charge of state property abroad — embassies, trade missions, unliquidated Soviet facilities" compresses that posting correctly in substance. "September 7th the family moved" is partially correct: month confirmed, the 7th unattested.

"Ozero — woods 50 miles north, first dacha 1990, burned 1996 while daughters 10 and 11 watched, August fire, instant rebuild, seven friends, cooperative November 1996" — mixed. Confirmed: eight founders registered 10–11 November 1996 at Solovyovka on Lake Komsomolskoye (Ozero account; Yakunin account); the daughters' arithmetic checks — Maria (1985) and Katerina (1986), so 11 and 10 in 1996 (Putin family account). Unverified: the 1990 start, the August month, the witnessing-and-nightmares detail, and the fire itself as a citable fact — teach those as the video's account. The count needs correcting once: Putin plus seven friends is eight founders, not seven.

"All but Putin were prominent businessmen who became oligarchs or officials — Kovalchuk of Bank Rossiya in the Pandora papers, two into Gazprom, Yakunin transport minister and Railways president, the seed of the post-1999 power web" — confirmed with two corrections. Kovalchuk and "Bank Rossiya built the network" around the Ozero gated community (ICIJ Panama Papers); the Pandora files are the broader inner-circle window (Guardian on Pandora) — correction: the Bank Rossiya network is Panama-documented (2016), Pandora is gloss. Yakunin "was president of Russian Railways from June 2005 to August 2015" (same Yakunin account as above — see §7) — correction: deputy minister from 2000, never "minister of transport." "Two members into Gazprom" is unverified as stated. Chubais's own St Petersburg start — deputy to Sobchak in 1990 — is confirmed separately (Chubais account).

6. Machinery — the hinge year as platform jump

The mechanism this chapter establishes is rescue-by-machine: polls, scalpels, ceasefires, airwaves and loans are fused into one reelection engine, and the engine's builders — Chubais, Dyachenko, the moguls — collect state assets as their fee. Petersburg is the same machine in miniature, except Sobchak refuses the professionals and the machine eats him instead.

Putin's 1996 is then told as three quiet acquisitions while louder men campaign: a credential (the degree), a posting (the property directorate), and a circle (Ozero). "Each time a platform looked ready to collapse — the KGB, then city government — he jumped to a higher one," until by year's end he stands "on the corner of the board" in Moscow. Whether he schemed for the top job ("personally I believe he probably wasn't") is the video's own hedge, and it is carried as such.

"No one knew it, but 1996 brought him within reach of national power" — the chapter's closing frame. The record gives it the foothold; the meaning stays the video's.

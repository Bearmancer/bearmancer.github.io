Putin: The Rise To Power · chapter 8 of 18

Sobchak

Video 1:04:57–1:12:03 · Lesson 0002 · ~7 min · full chapter transcript

Seven minutes that build the bridge of Putin's life: the reformist law professor who entered national politics in 1989, and the returning KGB officer assigned to his city just as he did. The chapter's argument is a double one — that the KGB sent Putin back to watch Sobchak, and that Sobchak, knowing it, used Putin right back. This lesson walks the chapter, then sorts what the record confirms from what it corrects.

Scope — boundary

This lesson covers chapter 8 only (1:04:57–1:12:03): Sobchak's rise, Putin's January 1990 return to Leningrad, and the Lensovet chairmanship of May 1990. The August 1991 coup, which the chapter teases in its last minute, belongs to the next chapter.

1. What matters — and what you can skip

Keep these four essentials:

  1. The professor and the student. Sobchak taught Putin at Leningrad State University in the 1970s; the prior link is why the KGB posting story is plausible and why Sobchak trusted him.
  2. The commission that made Sobchak. Chairing the Tbilisi inquiry and condemning the army on the Congress floor turned a law professor into a national opposition figure in months.
  3. The contested posting. Putin returned to Leningrad in January 1990 onto the KGB's active reserve at LSU — confirmed. That he was ordered to spy on Sobchak is allegation, not document.
  4. The two-way street. Sobchak hired the ex-student as his chief aide, set him to courting foreign investors, and thereby gave Putin his first taste of Western business — the mechanism everything after 1990 runs on.

What can be skimmed: the P&G joint-venture fee mechanics (the venture is confirmed, the bribe framing is the video's gloss); the Medvedev 2008–12 presidency aside, which is out of scope here; the city-versus-Moscow autonomy motive, which is the video's interpretation of a documented free-economic-zone plan.

3. From lecture hall to Congress

The chapter opens with the one prior tie that makes everything else work: Sobchak taught law at Leningrad State University while Putin studied there in the 1970s 1:05:11. Medvedev's own Kremlin biography puts the relationship plainly — "I first met him when I was in my second year at university. He gave a course of lectures there and examined me in civil law" — and Brookings' Hill and Gaddy note Putin "gravitated" to Sobchak's circle as a student. The video is careful here: it says outright that no contact between 1975 and 1990 is known 1:05:24, and the record's silence matches.

Then 1988–89 moves fast. Gorbachev retrofits the old Supreme Soviet into a Congress of People's Deputies with competitive elections 1:05:47 — held 26 March 1989, the first partially free nationwide vote, before the Party gives up its monopoly (Article 6 falls only in 1990). Sobchak wins a seat that spring, helps found the Interregional Deputies Group — the democratic opposition inside the Congress — alongside Sakharov and Yeltsin 1:06:25.

What makes him, in the chapter's telling, is Tbilisi. After the spring 1989 crackdown in the Georgian capital, Sobchak chairs the 24-member commission whose report to the Second Congress in December 1989 condemns the military — and, the video argues, makes future force against demonstrations harder 1:06:45. The video says he "even rebuked the KGB chief on the floor of the Congress" 1:06:57. Hold that line; §6 takes it apart.

"The idea was that they would find an agent who had some past connection to Sobchak and could insinuate himself back into his orbit and report back to the KGB on what he was doing."

— the video's account of the posting decision 1:07:06

4. The posting that followed him home

Putin, the video says, was "the perfect guy to do that": transferred from Dresden back to Leningrad to spy on Sobchak, under cover as assistant to the vice-rector of international affairs at Leningrad University while "funneling them dope on Sobchak" 1:07:20. The confirmed skeleton underneath: he returned to the USSR in January 1990 and from February 1990 served as assistant to the LSU rector for foreign affairs — on the KGB's active reserve, per Brookings, with his superiors assigning him to LGU in 1990. No primary document states a surveillance tasking, so the "sent back to spy" line stays an allegation, and the "pipeline" framing is the video's gloss.

The chapter's best passage is what happens next — the two-way street. Sobchak knew Putin was KGB, knew the job was probably to spy on him, and didn't care: he wanted a pipeline into useful KGB information for his own political advantage, "and Putin was quite happy to be used" 1:07:41. Putin becomes, in the video's words, Sobchak's "chief assistant" 1:08:11.

Two claims that stay the video's own

After five verification passes, two assertions in this chapter remain unfindable anywhere else: that the LSU international-affairs office controlled all foreign-student admissions USSR-wide, and that the office collected bribes as the economy fell apart. They are taught here as the video's account, unverified — see §6.

One dated detail needs its corrected neighbour now: the Procter & Gamble deal. The video frames the boss's joint venture as a 1990 arrangement giving P&G office space and access 1:08:45; the announcement actually came on 27 August 1991, P&G's first USSR office in the rector's mansion — and Putin's personal involvement is unfindable, a hedge the video itself makes ("Putin may not have been involved in this deal directly") 1:09:00.

5. Leningrad's outsider chairman

Early 1990 abolishes the Party monopoly, and Sobchak is suddenly at the forefront of national politics 1:09:17. The video says he "even tried to challenge Gorbachev as national leader" 1:09:26 — overstated: on 14 March 1990 only Gorbachev stood for the presidency, and Sobchak merely spoke "for realism" against immediate universal suffrage. The contender talk is post-coup press colour, August–September 1991.

The office within reach was local. Three factions deadlocked the Lensovet, so the city politicians drafted an outsider to lead it — and on 23 May 1990 Sobchak became chairman 1:10:04, a result the LA Times reported contemporaneously ("easily won… 223 of 320 votes," filed 25 May). Leningrad had no elected mayor; this was the closest thing to it.

Putin's brief, extended from the P&G episode, was to court foreign business and build the city's economy and tax base 1:10:20 — work only just becoming legal, which Sobchak and Putin read as autonomy from Moscow: a business-oriented Leningrad with foreign capital would be "less susceptible to control by Moscow" 1:10:39. The autonomy motive is the video's interpretation; the free-economic-zone plan itself is documented (TIME, December 1990). From June 1991 this becomes formal — on 28 June Putin takes over the Committee for External Relations — and Western companies follow (RFE/RL).

Staffing it brought the third man on stage: "a capable young bureaucrat named Dmitri Medvedev," ex-LSU law student turned teacher, who had run Sobchak's 1989 Congress campaign 1:10:54 — confirmed by Medvedev's own biography ("He invited me… to be his proxies"), with his work under Putin starting in 1990. The video's 2008–12 puppet-president aside is out of scope here. And the August 1991 core the chapter points at — Sobchak flying back, winning over General Samsonov, who promised not to move troops, with Putin coming out against the junta — is confirmed (Britannica; TASS, a state source), though Putin's own path ran through torn hesitation before his 20 August resignation (Moscow Times, 2021). Full mechanics belong to the next chapter.

6. Fact-check — the record behind the chapter

Claim by claim: confirmed where the record agrees, corrected where it differs, allegation where only the video says it. State sources are labelled as such.

  • Professor–student link, 1970s — confirmed. Medvedev's Kremlin biography ("examined me in civil law"); Hill & Gaddy's "gravitated" to Sobchak's circle. The 1975–90 silence matches the record.
  • 1989 Congress + Interregional Group — confirmed. Elections 26 March 1989; the Group formed June 1989 with Sobchak a founder/co-chairman alongside Sakharov and Yeltsin. Article 6 repealed 1990 — the sequencing is right.
  • Tbilisi commission — confirmed, one correction. Sobchak chaired the 24-member commission; its December 1989 report condemned the military. Correction: the crackdown was army and MVD troops under Gen. Rodionov (orders via Yazov) — the KGB's role was peripheral, so "the KGB cracked down" needs fixing. The Molotov–Ribbentrop commission was Yakovlev's, not Sobchak's.
  • "Rebuked the KGB chief on the floor" — not Sobchak. The famous May 1989 floor denunciation of the KGB was Yuri Vlasov's; no source shows Sobchak doing it. His documented clashes were with Rodionov and Yazov over Tbilisi. The video appears to compress Vlasov's moment into Sobchak.
  • "Sent back to spy on Sobchak" — allegation. Brookings confirms the 1990 LGU assignment on the active reserve; no document states a surveillance tasking. The "pipeline" is the video's gloss.
  • Dresden return and LSU post — confirmed. Back in the USSR January 1990; LSU rector's assistant for foreign affairs from February 1990 (TASS and the Russian government archive — state sources, labelled).
  • Resigned vs redundant — both dates, kept distinct. Britannica: "retired from active KGB service" in 1990; Putin's own account: resigned as lieutenant colonel on 20 August 1991, the coup's second day. Active service ended 1990 → active reserve at LSU → formal resignation August 1991. "Made redundant" has no direct source; the budget-cut context sits in the economics aid, cited not re-argued.
  • Lensovet chair, 23 May 1990 — confirmed contemporaneously (LA Times, 25 May 1990: 223 of 320 votes; the deadlock that produced his candidacy).
  • "Challenged Gorbachev" — overstated. Sole-candidate ballot 14 March 1990; Sobchak spoke "for realism." Contender talk is post-coup press colour (WaPo/LA Times, Aug–Sept 1991).
  • Foreign-investment role from June 1991 — confirmed (Committee for External Relations, 28 June 1991; RFE/RL on the Western firms). Autonomy-from-Moscow is interpretation; the free-economic-zone plan is documented (TIME, Dec 1990).
  • P&G–LSU venture — confirmed, date corrected. Announced 27 August 1991, not 1990; Putin's personal role unfindable, as the video hedges.
  • Medvedev ran the 1989 campaign — confirmed by his own biography; under Putin from 1990. The 2008–12 aside is out of scope.
  • August 1991, Leningrad — core confirmed, Putin qualified. Britannica: Sobchak flew back — aided by anti-coup KGB officers — and won over Samsonov. TASS (state source): Putin opposed the junta with the mayor's office; Moscow Times (2021): torn, deciding after thought, resigning. Any "positioning to survive either outcome" line is unsourced commentary.
  • Unfindable: two items. LSU office controlling all USSR foreign-student admissions, and the bribe-collection claim — the video's account only.

7. Open threads

Putin: The Rise to Power · chapter 11 of 18

Putin's Rolodex

Chapter 11 of 18 · 1:30:54–1:40:42

Lesson 05 · cast map · glossary · transcript slice

Ten minutes on how a 39-year-old city bureaucrat made himself the man everybody had to know: the KVS registrar every foreign firm passed through, the liquidator of seized party property, the regulator of a gambling boom — and the broker who did business with the gangs fighting over the city's gasoline.

The chapter's argument is that contact-hoarding was power: by 1994 Putin sat at the junction of foreign money, city assets and organised crime, and converted that junction into the rank of first deputy mayor. This lesson walks the chapter, then sorts what the record confirms from what it corrects.

1. What matters — and what you can skip

Keep these four essentials:

  1. The registrar became the database. Joint ventures had to register with Putin's KVS committee ($200 a head; ~300 by end 1991, 3,000+ by 1993) — so every foreign firm entering St Petersburg passed across his desk, and he kept the contacts.
  2. Three doors, one doorkeeper. Foreign-trade registration, liquidated Communist Party property, and gambling control all ran through Putin in 1991–92 — each one a legitimate office that doubled as a meeting point with money, buyers and criminals.
  3. Gangsters were laundered into businessmen. The Malyshev acquittal claim is unproven, but the PTK fuel-company arrangement is documented: deputy mayor Putin awarded the city's sole gasoline-supply right to a firm whose deputy president (1998–99) was Tambov boss Vladimir Kumarin.
  4. Indispensability converted into rank. After the October 1993 reorganisation concentrated power in the mayor, Sobchak delegated to his most capable aide: first deputy mayor, March 1994 — low-profile, behind the scenes, "fingers in everybody's pockets."

What can be skimmed: the French-bank thought experiment (colour for the registrar's pitch, not mechanism); the "wild East" auction catalogue (scene-setting for why investors came); the St Petersburg-as-portal history (true, but backdrop). The October 1993 constitutional crisis gets its mechanics in the economics aid, cited here not re-argued. Gunvor, the 1996 election and anything past March 1994 sit outside this chapter.

3. Everybody's registrar

The chapter opens by lowering Putin's rank and raising his position: "it wouldn't be accurate to say that Vladimir Putin was the most powerful man in St Petersburg in 1992" — but "you could make a case that he was the most strategic", the man with "the best and most useful contacts" who "held the future of the city and possibly all of Russia in his hands".

At the start of 1992 he was 39 — born 7 October 1952 — "a city bureaucrat in a badly fitted suit". Then the frame: the Union dead, Yeltsin's crash market transition, a Russia where private property had been "mostly outlawed for the majority of the previous century", and a city that was broke.

From Putin's chair the money could only come from outside — and here the video overreaches: "the West was providing no foreign aid", with Bush "the first" too vulnerable in an election year to be "soft on the former communist enemy". That flat claim is false — the packages were real, only slow and conditional — but the chapter's working premise stands: from St Petersburg, private foreign investment "was really the only option", "just as it had been in the food situation."

Russia was "essentially up for auction", and "Vladimir Putin was one of the auctioneers".

"Under St Petersburg City law any foreign business interest that wanted to do business in the city had to register with Putin's office."

— the video on the registrar's gate

Teach the gate with its limits: the rule attached to joint ventures — $200 a registration, ~300 by end 1991, over 3,000 by 1993 — not to literally every foreign business (see §6).

The French-bank vignette that follows is the chapter's best colour: the arriving executive meets "this nice man Vladimir Putin," who looks like "an insurance salesman," offers introductions — "how's Tuesday at 2" — and files another card. Dinners, Christmas cards, a bottle for the office, a holiday in Provence: "you are just one card in Putin's Rolodex — he's got thousands of them".

And the Rolodex scaled nationally: St Petersburg "the traditional portal through which Europe and the rest of the world does business with Russia", so the file was "basically a database of the vast majority of foreign business activity in Russia as a whole" — reason enough for Yeltsin's Moscow to want to know him too.

Two more portfolios widened the junction. After the party was abolished, its St Petersburg property — "office space, hotels, a farm, official party cars, even a summer camp for kids" — sat "up for grabs," and "Putin was in charge of liquidating all that property for cash", which "gave him personal access to investors with money". Teach one confirmed instance, not the catalogue: the Intourist-Leningrad → Sankt-Peterburg transfer of November 1991, with Putin tasked with oversight — the farm, cars and camp as itemised are unfindable (see §6).

Then gambling: Sobchak told Putin "to get illegal gambling in St Petersburg under control in 1992", when makeshift casinos and slots "were popping up all over the city like zits on a teenager's face". The paper trail is firmer than the chapter lets on — Sobchak's 9 October 1991 order, the 12 February 1992 municipal gambling entity founded by the KVS, the May 1993 count of 180 gambling points and 1,600 slot machines — and Putin's own First Person account admits the 51%-share scheme failed: "money left in black cash… showed losses" (see §6).

4. Crime, casinos, gasoline

Controlling gambling "meant coming in contact with the people who ran gambling operations — in other words, organized crime". The chapter concedes the scene "wasn't that organized in St Petersburg in 1992", then states the consequence flatly: "Putin quickly began to know all the major criminals in the city", because "there was a fine line between crime and more or less legitimate business" — "so of course he knew and did business with gangsters".

The atmosphere checks out: over 2,000 organised-crime members on the October 1992 police count, a US consulate warning by March 1993 (see §6). The video's "murder rate… utterly insane" line carries no rate figure to check — teach it as atmosphere, not data.

Then the chapter's most pointed charge: "Putin seems to have influenced the criminal trial of a gangster named Vladimir" Malyshev, who "ran the second largest gang in the city" — "Putin got him acquitted at a trial", "the objective was to balance the major gangs against each other so that no one group could take over the whole city". The arrest (October 1992) and the case (No. 534530, opened 1994 — acquitted 1995 on the main counts, convicted only on weapons) are documented.

The causation is not: no source ties Putin to causing the acquittal, and the "balancing" motive is unfindable (see §6). "Despite Putin's efforts it didn't work": Malyshev's gang "eventually lost out to a group called the Tambovsky gang, with whom Putin eventually had to do business".

"This company effectively turned the head of the Tambovsky gang, Vladimir Kumarin, from a gangster into a legitimate businessman."

— the video on the PTK arrangement

The mechanism as the video tells it: a Siberian oil company throttled gasoline to extort a higher price; Tambov threatened its directors; the company yielded and "allowed the gang to act as enforcers to ensure the flow of gasoline to the city"; "Putin approved this arrangement with the foundation of the St Petersburg petroleum fuel company".

Teach the documented skeleton and flag the sequence: PTK was founded September 1994 by the city property committee, Putin as deputy mayor awarded it the sole city gasoline-supply right — ambulances, police, buses, taxis included — and Kumarin was PTK's deputy president in 1998–99. The "throttled → threatened → enforcers" chain itself is unfindable; the documented gang war of those years is Tambov against the Kazan-group figure Dyakov, 1993–94 (see §6).

And the chapter's vocabulary needs a label: "the people who made this sort of transition from gang leaders to legitimate executives would eventually come to be known as oligarchs" — the video's idiosyncratic usage. Scholars reserve "oligarch" for the loans-for-shares bankers; say so (see §6).

The confirmed bribery exhibit belongs here too: the "Putin's Stamp and the $2M Heist" episode — Leonardov, Elegant Systems, Raduga, some 285 million rubles — where a court ruled the registration illegal (see §6). It is case-specific: one registration, one ruling, one set of figures. Do not let it stand for the whole Rolodex.

5. Machinery — indispensability converted into rank

The mechanism this chapter establishes is contact-hoarding as power: a registrar who files every entrant, a liquidator who meets every buyer, a gambling regulator who meets every operator — three legitimate offices, one private database. "He had made himself indispensable to Anatoly Sobchak, who had himself become indispensable to the national government".

The October 1993 crisis is the converter: after the "constitutional mishaps of October 1993" — mechanics deferred to the next chapter — "the St Petersburg city government was reorganized yet again, giving the mayor… even more power", and because Sobchak "had to delegate a lot of them to his aides, of which Putin was the most powerful and capable", the promotion followed: "in March 1994 Putin was appointed first deputy mayor of St Petersburg".

The Yeltsin decree abolishing the Soviet local councils (27 October 1993) is the documented framework; a St Petersburg-specific reorganisation text was not pulled, so caveat the city detail (see §6). The appointment itself is confirmed — as is the colleague the chapter names: Yakovlev was indeed the other first deputy, and he ran against Sobchak in 1996.

The chapter then turns to the question hanging over all of it: "was Putin still a KGB agent?" — the man "first assigned to trail Sobchak for the KGB." The video reports both of Putin's versions — resigned "officially from the KGB in June 1991, not long after Sobchak became mayor," or "a letter of resignation during the coup in August" — and rules the timing "really not clear".

Teach the variance, not a verdict: First Person says a first letter (1990, after a blackmail attempt) stalled, then the 20 August 1991 second letter signed after Sobchak called Kryuchkov; the BBC's taxi-driver version and Hahn's analysis call the timing murky ("hedging"); no June-1991 resignation document exists (see §6).

What the chapter asserts is the functional point: "by 1994 they were no longer giving him orders and he was no longer taking them — he had definitely pitched his star to Sobchak, and that was where his loyalties lie".

The paper trail the chapter leaves for later is SPAG: Putin on the advisory board — honorary from 1992 — until his March 2000 inauguration; the summer-2000 BND paper; the May 2003 BKA, customs and Darmstadt raids across 28 premises; the probe closed 18 June 2009 on proscription grounds. The rogatory letter and the suspect list (Smirnov, Ritter, Sauer, Rese) exist via the archives (see §6).

And the numbers need keeping straight now, because the course will reuse them: the "Putin case" is No. 144128 — the Twentieth Trust affair, a ~20-strong team led by Zykov, opened 1999, closed 2000 for insufficient proof — while No. 534530 is the 1994 Tambov-Malyshev indictment. "18/95-238278" is not a verified Russian criminal-case number. None of this is the next chapter's subject; it is the file the Rolodex years left behind.

6. Fact-check — the record behind the chapter

Claim by claim: confirmed where the record agrees, corrected where it differs, allegation where only one mouth says it. Video wording in quotes; corrections beside.

  • 39 at the start of 1992, chairing the KVS — confirmed. Born 7 October 1952, so 39 until that October; chairman of the Foreign Relations Committee.
  • Registration gate — corrected in scope. Joint-venture registration at a $200 fee: ~300 JVs by end 1991, 3,000+ by 1993 (RFE/RL; KVS bulletins). "Any foreign business had to register" overstates — the rule attached to joint ventures.
  • "The West was providing no foreign aid" — wrong as a flat fact. Bush's $6.6bn package (April 1992), the G-7 $24bn framework, the Freedom Support Act (October 1992: $410m bilateral plus the $12bn IMF quota). What holds: aid was slow and conditional — "too little, too late" in the histories — and from St Petersburg the private route looked like the only fast money.
  • CPSU-property liquidation — one instance confirmed, the list unfindable. Intourist-Leningrad → Sankt-Peterburg, November 1991, with Putin tasked with oversight, is documented. The itemised catalogue (farm, cars, camp) as stated is unfindable.
  • Gambling — confirmed in outline, with the paper trail firmer than the chapter. Sobchak's 9 October 1991 order put gambling control with Putin; the 12 February 1992 municipal gambling entity (founder: KVS) became Neva-Shans; the May 1993 check counted 180 gambling points and 1,600 slot machines. Putin's own First Person excerpt admits the 51%-share scheme failed ("money left in black cash… showed losses").
  • Malyshev — arrest and case confirmed, Putin causation unfindable. Arrest October 1992; case 534530 (1994); acquitted 1995 on the main counts, convicted only on weapons. No source ties Putin to causing the acquittal; the "balancing the gangs" motive is unfindable. Teach the trial as fact, the influence as the video's claim.
  • PTK — award confirmed, threat sequence unfindable. Founded September 1994 by the city property committee; Putin as deputy mayor awarded it the sole city gasoline-supply right, covering ambulances, police, buses and taxis. Kumarin was PTK's deputy president 1998–99 and the Tambov boss (Interior Minister Gryzlov, August 2001); the 1 June 1994 attempt on his life is documented. The "Siberian supplier throttled → Tambov threatened → gang became enforcers" chain is unfindable; the documented fight is Tambov vs the Kazan-group's Dyakov, 1993–94.
  • "Gangsters became oligarchs" — the video's usage, flagged. Scholars reserve "oligarch" for the loans-for-shares bankers of the later 1990s. The chapter means gang-leaders-turned-executives; teach the term as the video's idiosyncrasy, never as the scholarly one.
  • Post-October-1993 reorganisation — framework confirmed, city text caveated. Yeltsin's decree abolishing the Soviet local councils (27 October 1993) is the documented framework. A St Petersburg-specific reorganisation text was not pulled — the city-level detail stands on the video's account.
  • First deputy mayor, March 1994 — confirmed; the "three holders" framing corrected. The appointment is confirmed, and Yakovlev was indeed the other first deputy (later Sobchak's 1996 rival). ASR "Chayas" is Chubais — and he was not a 1994 co-holder: he had left for federal privatisation work in 1991–92.
  • The KGB question — variance, not verdict. First Person: a first letter (1990, after a blackmail attempt) stalled, then the 20 August 1991 second letter signed after Sobchak called Kryuchkov. The BBC taxi-driver version and Hahn's analysis call the timing murky ("hedging"). No June-1991 resignation document exists. Present all three; close nothing.
  • SPAG — confirmed as a paper trail. Putin on the advisory board (honorary from 1992) until the March 2000 inauguration; the summer-2000 BND paper; the May 2003 BKA/customs/Darmstadt raids (28 premises); the probe closed 18 June 2009 on proscription grounds. Rogatory letter and suspect list (Smirnov, Ritter, Sauer, Rese) exist via the archives — see the Havighurst Center's English document set on the St Petersburg 1990s.
  • Case numbers — kept straight. The "Putin case" is No. 144128 (Twentieth Trust / Dvadtsaty Trest; ~20-strong team led by Zykov; opened 1999, closed 2000 for insufficient proof). No. 534530 is the 1994 Tambov-Malyshev indictment. "18/95-238278" is not a verified Russian criminal-case number.
  • "Putin's Stamp and the $2M Heist" — confirmed via RFE/RL, case-specific. Leonardov, Elegant Systems, Raduga, ~285m rubles; the court ruled the registration illegal. One registration, one ruling — its figures travel no further.
  • 1990s crime atmosphere — confirmed as atmosphere. 2,000+ organised-crime members on the October 1992 police count; the US consulate's March 1993 warning. The chapter states no rate figure to check — none is checked.
  • Deliberately not imported: Gunvor (working together from 1997, formalised 2000 — the chapter never names it), voucher-privatisation mechanics (in the economics aid, cited not re-argued), and the October 1993 crisis mechanics (next chapter).

7. Open threads

  • Did the aid correction change the argument? The flat "no aid" claim fails, but the chapter's working premise — that fast money could only be private — survives it. Whether slow conditional packages could ever have substituted for the Rolodex is unresolved.
  • Who owned the auction? The party-property catalogue and the raw-material suppliers alike lack documented owners — the cargo left the docks, the buildings changed hands, and the paper names nobody.
  • Broker or beneficiary? The chapter shows Putin at every junction and proves enrichment at none of them — except the single ruled-illegal registration. Whether the Rolodex was a service to the city or a stake in it stands unresolved.

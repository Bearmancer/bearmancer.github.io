Putin: The Rise to Power · chapter 9 of 18

1991

Chapter 9 of 18 · 1:12:03–1:21:29

Lesson 03 · cast map · glossary · transcript slice

Nine minutes that end a country: Vilnius in January, an elected mayor in Leningrad in June, a botched hardliner coup in August, no Soviet Union by Christmas — and Putin, who began the year a KGB officer on holiday, ending it as the man Sobchak turns to when the city's food runs out. The chapter's argument is that 1991 made Putin twice over: the coup fixed his side, and the collapse handed him his first real job. This lesson walks the chapter, then sorts what the record confirms from what it corrects.

1. What matters — and what you can skip

Keep these four essentials:

  1. Leningrad held because Sobchak came home. He flew back from Moscow on 19 August — aided by KGB officers who opposed the coup — and won over Gen. Samsonov, who promised not to move troops. Hundreds of thousands of demonstrators and the police did the rest.
  2. Putin picked his side on paper. He resigned from the KGB on 20 August 1991, the coup's second day, as a lieutenant colonel — confirmed — though his own accounts vary between instant certainty and torn hesitation, and the video's "hedging to survive either outcome" framing is the video's gloss.
  3. The union died on a schedule the coup set. The Union Treaty was due for signing on 20 August; the plotters struck to kill it. What followed — Estonia 20 August, Latvia 21st, Ukraine 24th, Belovezha 8 December, Gorbachev's resignation the evening of 25 December — is confirmed throughout.
  4. Winter made the fixer. With central food distribution dead, rationing and aid held off famine (the video's "literally starving" overstates), and Sobchak turned to Putin for a barter deal — the on-ramp to the KVS affair, the most serious misstep of his early career.

What can be skimmed: the Curonian Spit travelogue (it misplaces Gorbachev's dacha — see §6); the plotters' personal ends (Pavlov's drinking, Pugo's death), which are colour, not mechanism; the 1917 Ukraine aside, a defensible compression in one line.

3. Vilnius to the mayoralty

The chapter opens a year before its title date. Gorbachev had signalled at the end of 1988 that he would not use force to hold Eastern Europe — but the Baltic republics, annexed by Stalin in 1940, were inside the Union, and he intended to hold them. On 13 January 1991 Soviet troops cracked down on demonstrators in Vilnius: 14 people were killed. The figure is confirmed — 14 civilians killed and more than 140 injured at the TV tower. Yeltsin, who had quit the Communist Party the previous July (12 July 1990, at the 28th Congress), condemned the crackdown, and Sobchak and the Lensovet stood with him.

Leningrad's own settlement came in June. After long disputes over the city government ended in a compromise favouring Sobchak, a citywide mayoral election was held — and on 12 June 1991, the same day Yeltsin won the Russian presidency, Sobchak won it and became the city's official mayor. Voters also chose to restore the name St Petersburg — a non-binding referendum; the council ratified it on 25 June and the USSR Congress enacted it that September, a three-month legal tail the video compresses. Note the anachronism the fact-check flags: until September 1991 the city was still Leningrad, so the video's "St Petersburg" for August scenes is a slip.

"The union treaty was concluded on July 23rd 1991. It was scheduled to go into effect on August 20th. For the hardline communists still in the government, the treaty was utterly terrifying."

— the video on the coup's motive

Gorbachev's compromise — a reorganised USSR with autonomous republics — was initialled on 23 July with ceremonial signing due 20 August. The hardliners, led by KGB chairman Vladimir Kryuchkov with several government department heads, decided to scrap it and restore the old Union. The signing date is the whole story of the coup's timing: it pre-empted the ceremony.

4. Three days in August — and what fell after

Here the video stumbles on geography and recovers on substance. Gorbachev left for his dacha before the signing — but the video places it on "the Curonian Spit," the Baltic strip near Kaliningrad where Putin himself was holidaying in a borrowed guest house. That is an error: Gorbachev was held at Foros, in Crimea, where he arrived 4 August and where the plotters' delegation confronted him on 18 August. The video conflates Putin's holiday locality with Gorbachev's dacha. What matters — phones cut, a committee claiming Gorbachev had resigned for health reasons on 19 August — is confirmed.

Putin heard the announcement on television, unsure at first what to do — the video reads this as calculation, "try to figure out which side of it to be on depending on what he thought the outcome was likely to be". No independent witness exists for his inward state; his own First Person account claims the opposite, instant certainty: "As soon as the coup began, I immediately decided whose side I was on." Both framings are taught here; neither is independently checkable. What is checkable is the tell he claims decided it: Yanaev's visibly shaking hands at the plotters' press conference — confirmed on the footage — from which Putin says he "realized then and there that the coup was doomed to fail."

On 19 August Putin reached Sobchak by telephone; the mayor told him to return, and on the morning of the 20th he was at Sobchak's side. Two claims attach to that return. First, that Putin arranged a phone call between Sobchak and Leningrad KGB chief Kurkov that bought Samsonov's neutrality — single-sourced to anonymous sources in a 2004 Observer report, taught here as allegation; what is confirmed is the outcome, via Britannica and the contemporary press: Sobchak flew back aided by anti-coup KGB officers, Samsonov promised not to move troops, and hundreds of thousands of demonstrators with the police behind them forced Leningrad Television to broadcast Sobchak's 19 August appearance. Second, that Putin "organized a contingent of bodyguards to protect Sobchak from possible arrest" — which the video itself immediately discounts: "but this is probably not true". It stays discounted here: no documentation surfaced in five verification passes, and the closest record is Putin handing out pistols on KGB visits, not a guard detail.

"His appearance standing on a tank in Moscow urging the people to resist resulted in a subtle but vitally important shift: the legitimacy of power in the Soviet Union passed from Gorbachev to him."

— the video on Yeltsin, 19 August

The coup's failure mechanics are confirmed point for point, with corrections of detail. The plotters held Moscow's broadcast media but lost places like Leningrad, where Sobchak went on air. Gorbachev, left alive, smuggled out taped statements — he filmed them in the small hours at Foros, and they aired in the US on 25 August. Yeltsin was never arrested despite a KGB list naming him, and his midday appearance denouncing the coup — from an armoured personnel carrier, not a tank — is confirmed, though "legitimacy passed" is the video's interpretation, flagged as such. The crews around the Moscow armour fraternised rather than fired; three protesters — Komar, Usov and Krichevsky — died in the Novy Arbat tunnel on the night of 20–21 August. Of the plotters' ends: Yanaev's tremor is on film (the drunkenness gloss is speculation); Pavlov spent the coup drinking or ill and handed duties to Doguzhiyev; Pugo was found shot dead on 22 August with his wife — sources differ over whether he shot her first, so it is taught as unclear.

The cascade the coup "supercharged" is confirmed throughout: Estonia declared on 20 August while the plotters were still nominally in charge, Latvia on the 21st, Ukraine on the 24th. The video's 1917 aside — Ukraine tried to leave the Russian Empire at the revolution and "it didn't stick" — compresses the 1917–21 Ukrainian People's Republic into a line; defensible, kept to one. Then Belovezha: in early December Yeltsin met fellow republic leaders at a lodge in the Belarusian forest and signed away the USSR for a Commonwealth of Independent States — corrected to three leaders (Kravchuk, Shushkevich, Yeltsin), 8 December 1991, a calculated move to drain what remained of Gorbachev's power. Gorbachev, "with no one returning his calls," resigned on Christmas night 1991 as the red flag came down — the evening of 25 December, powers including the launch codes passing to Yeltsin; the 74-year arithmetic checks out.

The chapter closes where the next begins: distribution dead, rationing nearly a year old, shelves bare, and Sobchak turning to his fixer. "Putin made a deal all right" — "the most politically serious misstep in Putin's entire early career," broken out into its own chapter. The economics behind that winter sit in the economics aid, cited here not re-argued: Leningrad had rationed food since 1 December 1990, and by late 1991 hard currency, gold and trade had all run out at once.

5. Machinery — how a coup fails and a city eats

Two mechanisms carry the chapter. The first is failure by omission: the plotters seized the centre but left every alternative channel open — Leningrad's airwaves, Gorbachev alive with a camcorder, Yeltsin unarrested with a vehicle to stand on, and, decisively, orders the army would not obey. Leningrad is the clean demonstration: one mayor flying home, one general promising stillness, one KGB chief taking a phone call, and the troops stay put. The single-sourced Kurkov call matters less than the structure it names — every coercive link in the chain had a human joint, and in Leningrad each joint bent away.

The second is substitution: when the Union's distribution infrastructure stopped functioning, the city replaced planning with barter — rare-earth metals, oil products and raw materials out, foodstuffs in. That barter is the KVS affair of the next chapter, and this chapter's closing minutes are its on-ramp: Sobchak "had to do something to feed the people of the city he governed so that they wouldn't riot", and Putin "already had a reputation as a fixer who could get things done especially if you didn't want to know the details". The Salye commission's numbers — over $100m of commodities out (one variant says $122m), the food never arriving, the council voting to sack Putin and the mayor promoting him instead — are confirmed in outline; the commission charged failures of oversight, not personal corruption, and no prosecutor took the case up. Full documents wait in the next chapter; the English set is held at Miami University's Havighurst Center.

6. Fact-check — the record behind the chapter

Claim by claim: confirmed where the record agrees, corrected where it differs, allegation where only one mouth says it. State sources are labelled as such.

  • Vilnius, 13 January 1991 — confirmed. 14 civilians killed, 140+ injured at the TV tower. Yeltsin quit the CPSU on 12 July 1990 and condemned the crackdown; Sobchak and the Lensovet backed the Baltic position.
  • Leningrad mayoral election + name vote, 12 June 1991 — confirmed, with a legal tail. First popular mayoral vote, held jointly with the Russian presidential election; Sobchak won. Correction: the name referendum was non-binding — council ratified 25 June, USSR Congress enacted September 1991. Anachronism note: the city was Leningrad until September — "St Petersburg" for August scenes is the video's slip.
  • Union Treaty motive and dates — confirmed. Initialled 23 July after the Novo-Ogaryovo session; ceremonial signing due 20 August, which the coup pre-empted. Kryuchkov the principal organizer is confirmed (National Security Archive; Chernyaev diary).
  • Dacha geography — video error, corrected. Gorbachev was at Foros, Crimea, not the Curonian Spit; the video conflates Putin's Baltic holiday patch with the dacha. Putin's Kaliningrad-enclave holiday and 20 August return are confirmed (Guardian; First Person).
  • Resignation, 20 August 1991, lieutenant colonel — confirmed, with the tension flagged. Putin's own account: a stalled 1990 letter, then a second statement on 20 August, with Sobchak calling Kryuchkov and then his division chief. Tension: a 2016-published 1991 Chas Pik interview has the resignation already filed and signed by summer, and the 2018 Sobchak film records him as "torn" before deciding (Moscow Times, 2021) — his accounts vary over time, and the video's hedging-to-survive framing is unsourced commentary. Active service ended 1990; formal resignation August 1991 (see Lesson 02 §6).
  • Sobchak–Kurkov call via Putin — single-sourced allegation. Anonymous sources, Observer 2004 only. Confirmed around it: anti-coup KGB officers aided Sobchak's return (Britannica); Samsonov promised not to move troops; Samsonov had formed an emergency committee chaired by Gidaspov; demonstrators and police forced Leningrad TV to air Sobchak (Stanford "Televorot" scholarship).
  • Bodyguard contingent — uncorroborated, and the video says so. "Probably not true" is the video's own verdict; five verification passes found no documentation. Nearest record: pistols handed out on KGB-factory visits, a service revolver left in a safe (First Person).
  • Yanaev's shaking hands — confirmed; the drunkenness gloss is speculation, labelled as such. Three tunnel dead confirmed with names: Komar, Usov, Krichevsky, night of 20–21 August. Pavlov drunk/ill and handing over duties confirmed in substance. Pugo's end confirmed in outline only — sources differ over his wife's death, taught as unclear.
  • Yeltsin on armour — corrected. An armoured personnel carrier, not a tank. "Legitimacy passed" is interpretation, flagged as such. Gorbachev's Foros tapes confirmed (filmed in the small hours, aired 25 August).
  • Dissolution cascade — confirmed throughout. Estonia 20 August, Latvia 21st, Ukraine 24th. Correction: Belovezha was three leaders (Kravchuk, Shushkevich, Yeltsin), 8 December — not "several." Gorbachev resigned the evening of 25 December as the flag came down (State Department history). The RSFSR absorbed union bodies; the KGB's end dates vary (24 October / 6 November / 3 December) — teach the range. The 74-year arithmetic is right.
  • Winter 1991–92 — confirmed via the economics aid, with one correction. Rationing from 1 December 1990, bare shelves, German aid shipments — all confirmed. Correction: "literally starving" overstates: rationing plus aid held, no famine; no German quota numbers were found. "No ration tickets left" and "18-hour days" are rhetoric without sourcing.
  • KVS / Salye on-ramp — confirmed in outline; detail belongs to the next chapter. $100m+ of commodities out (variant: $122m — flag the range), food never arrived, council voted to sack Putin, the mayor refused and promoted him, no investigation followed. The final report charged oversight failures, not personal corruption; it was forwarded to prosecutors and neither city nor federal agents took it up. English documents: Miami University's Havighurst Center.
  • Still unfindable (said plainly): Smolny/Mariinsky deliberation records for 19–21 August; the Shevchenko memoir text; bodyguard documentation; Putin's exact Kaliningrad address; winter 1991–92 German quota specifics; Salye-referral prosecution paperwork.

7. Open threads

  • What did the barter actually move? The chapter names the deal but not its cargo — the commodities, the licensees, and where the money went all belong to the next chapter.
  • Whose call bought Leningrad's quiet? The single-sourced Kurkov account stands against a documented outcome with many parents — demonstrators, police, Samsonov's own calculus.
  • Which Putin account of August holds? Instant certainty (First Person), summer-signed papers (Chas Pik), torn hesitation (the Sobchak film) — three self-accounts, no outside witness.

The Time Of Troubles

Chapter 12 of 18 · 1:40:42–1:55:38

Lesson 06 · cast map · glossary · transcript slice

Fifteen minutes that give the course its hinge: the chapter argues post-Soviet Russia replayed the 17th-century Time of Troubles — constitutional shelling in Moscow, a humiliated president abroad, a losing war in Chechnya, a society in medical and spiritual collapse — and that Russians would accept an autocrat to end it, as they accepted Mikhail Romanov in 1613.

Putin, in this telling, is the modern Romanov, and everything he later does is the turning of his back on this decade. This lesson walks the chapter, then sorts what the record confirms from what it corrects.

1. What matters — and what you can skip

Keep these four essentials:

  1. October 1993 ended parliamentarism by force. Yeltsin dissolved the Congress on 21 September without authority; deputies barricaded the White House; street fighting ran 3 October and tanks shelled at sunrise 4 October — not the video's "October 2nd" (see §7). The 12 December referendum then locked in a strong presidency.
  2. Two humiliations compounded it. At home, the December 1993 Duma gave LDPR 22.92% and the Communists a foothold; abroad, the 1990 oral "not one inch" assurances gave way to 1994 expansion — "cold peace" by December. Teach both halves: Russian perception of broken assurances vs Western denial of a formal pledge (see §7).
  3. Chechnya proved the army flabby. Ultimatum 29 November 1994, ground entry 11 December — not "November invaded" (see §7). Grozny fell by March 1995, control never followed, tens of thousands died with atrocities on both sides.
  4. The chapter's thesis is the Romanov analogy. Chaos ends, in the standard Russian telling, when nobles rally behind an autocrat — Putin as modern Mikhail Romanov, the oligarchs as the noble families. Everything else in the chapter is evidence for that frame.

What can be skimmed: the False Dmitry pageant (colour for the analogy, compresses a century — the "Ivan the Terrible first via Cossacks" single-origin line is flagged as colour in §7); the famine-third and Zemsky Sobor mechanics (backdrop for 1613); the Articles-of-Confederation aside (one-line parallel). The economics of the decade sits in the economics aid, cited not re-argued.

3. October 1993 — dissolution to shelling

The chapter opens with the analogy, then lands it: after two years in power Yeltsin "had proved incapable of working with the Congress of people's deputies" — president-with-legislative-input or deputies-with-presidential-clerk, "an old argument that confronts many societies coming out of revolutions".

On 21 September 1993 Yeltsin, "totally fed up, dissolved the Congress of people's deputies" — and the video concedes at once: "he had no legal right to do so but something had to be done". That concession is confirmed (see §7).

"The deputies took refuge here in this building and blockaded it — it's known as the Russian White House but it's not the residence of the Russian president."

— the video on the seat of the standoff

Teach the correction with the quote: "tanks and troops were called out on October 2nd" and "Russian tanks under Yeltsin's orders shelled the White House" compresses two days into one — street fighting and Ostankino ran 3 October, the tank shelling came at sunrise 4 October (see §7).

The toll the video gives — "the official death toll was 187 but that's probably a low estimate" — teach as a range, never one number: officials 124–187, unofficial up to 2,000. "The worst episode of street fighting in the Russian capital since the Revolutions of 1917" is the standard contemporaneous line.

Yeltsin "came out on top — after all he had tank battalions", and the constitution he favoured "was confirmed by a popular referendum in December 1993" — the 12 December vote for a strong presidency.

Then the chapter's coo: "guess who was the legal scholar who took the laboring oar in drafting the new constitution — Anatoly Sobchak of course", "Russia's preeminent constitutional scholar" moonlighting "as Russia's James Madison". Strip the puff, keep the post: the draft came from a trio plus an 800-participant conference, Sobchak chaired the Assembly, and the result is informally "Sobchak's constitution" (see §7).

While he chaired in Moscow, "he delegated increasing amounts of authority to Putin" — documented in mechanism: his deputies handled everyday control and Putin was often regarded as acting mayor (see §7). What the video adds — "Putin, who had military police stationed around the city to head off any incidents" — is unfindable after five passes: do not teach it.

The St Petersburg settlement "gave the mayor Sobchak more power" mirroring the national one; a city-specific charter act is likewise unfindable, so teach the mirror as the video's parallel, not a documented text.

4. Assurances, expansion, "cold peace"

"After 74 years of being called the world's bad guy Russia had finally jettisoned communism but found no reward" — talk of a Marshall-like programme, "nothing like that materialized", the West "indifferent" and keeping Russia "at arm's length". Teach this as the video's judgement, not a ledger: large flows existed (IMF ~$22bn per the economics aid), and the arm's-length character is documented — "indifferent" is gloss (see §7).

"Baker phrased this assurance categorically" — NATO "would not seek to expand by gaining new members among the Eastern European countries."

— the video on February 1990

The core is confirmed with the form corrected: Baker said "not one inch" thrice on 9 February 1990; Genscher–Hurd lines of 6 February and Kohl's match; Gorbachev got parallel words from French, West German and British leaders (see §7).

The corrections: never a written treaty pledge — the final settlement only restricted forces in the ex-GDR; Gorbachev himself said in 2014 that "NATO expansion" was not discussed as a formal topic; Bush's letter wording (borders) differs from Baker's question (jurisdiction); Cheney's sabotage role is interpretive, no primary; Thatcher's line runs only through the Hurd channel. "Bush himself typically tried to split the difference" while Cheney "believed the US should still be wary" — teach Cheney's half as reading, not record.

Then the turn: "in 1994 Bill Clinton reversed that and made it clear that NATO would expand" — "Yeltsin blew his top, he felt betrayed and one can understand why". The dated spine: "not whether but when" (Prague, January 1994); Partnership for Peace pitched to Yeltsin as the track to membership; the Budapest blow-up ("cold peace," December 1994); "nothing but humiliation" (May 1995); the German 1994 papers' "my way or no way" and Ushakov's "betrayal" (see §7).

"Was NATO really a threat to Russia? One could argue it was more symbolic than real" — "that was until 1999 and NATO intervention in the Balkan Wars, but we're not there yet". Kosovo stays a forward reference; the chapter's own hedge — "I don't have a dog in this fight" — is the licence to teach both halves straight.

5. Chechnya and the social crisis

"Now we have to get into the Chechen thing. This is complicated". Status first: the Checheno-Ingush ASSR sat inside the RSFSR and split in June 1992; the independence election was 27 October 1991 and the sovereignty decree 1 November 1991 as the Chechen Republic — "Ichkeria" dates only from 14 January 1994, so the video's "November 1991 … the Chechen Republic of Ichkeria" is close on the decree, anachronistic on the name (see §7).

The deep past the video sketches — "Ivan the Terrible was the first Russian ruler to try to extend his influence into Chechnya through the Cossacks" — compresses a century: flag as colour, not a single-origin finding. The deportation passage needs its numbers corrected: 23 February–13 March 1944, 494,456 arrived in Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan, first-year deaths 23–35% regionally (up to 49% for Balkars) — the video's "about 60% of them died" is high; "Stalin feared they were Nazi sympathizers" is the stated pretext, its sincerity debated; return from 1956, the ASSR restored 9 January 1957 (see §7).

"Annoyed with the Chechen refusal to take orders from Moscow and an increasing escalation of military incidents, in November 1994 Yeltsin said eff it and invaded Chechnya."

— the video on the war's start

Correct the month: the ultimatum came 29 November 1994, ground entry 11 December — the video conflates ultimatum month with invasion (see §7). The flabbiness passage stands: "his military was generally pretty flabby, the command structure wasn't good, logistics were sometimes botched", columns blocked by civilians in Dagestan and Ingushetia, Grozny taken by March 1995, control never established.

Grachev's "couple of hours with a single airborne regiment" bloodless-blitzkrieg boast is confirmed as a quote. "The Russians also committed a number of war crimes and atrocities … terror bombing, human shields, torture, ethnic cleansing" plus "tens of thousands died, atrocities by both sides" — teach the toll and the both-sides frame as general statements; do not itemise war crimes as legal findings without HRW/Memorial primaries (see §7).

"Being Muslims, the Chechen resistance called for volunteers across the Islamic world to come to Chechnya to fight a jihad" — the Khattab-network presence is real, but "numerous terrorists who would later turn up fighting Americans in Afghanistan after 9/11 did tours of duty in Chechnya first" overstates: write "some" (see §7).

"One of the Chechen commanders in this conflict was a fellow called Akhmat Kadyrov" — mufti 1995, jihad caller, fought with the rebels, sided with Moscow in October 1999, killed 9 May 2004 in the Dynamo-stadium Victory Day blast; Ramzan his successor from March 2007; the 1990s break with Maskhadov also ran through clan and power politics, not jihadism alone (see §7). His 150-kill fatwa stays attributed via the Al Jazeera piece, never as bare fact.

The chapter then folds Petersburg politics back in: Sobchak "eventually became something of [Yeltsin's] nemesis" — overstates a real rift (Chechen-war criticism, sparring); the loyalty half is confirmed (backed Sobchak in 1996, got the 1999 charges dropped, Sobchak campaigned for Putin) (see §7).

The December 1993 Duma "didn't go well for Yeltsin": "a right-wing nationalist party led by a man called Vladimir Zhirinovsky also gained a lot of seats" — LDPR 22.92% (64 seats), Choice of Russia 15.51% (62), CPRF 12.40% (42); the ultra-nationalist surge and the military LDPR vote (Kantemir division) are documented (see §7).

"A guy, an off-screen character in the submarine thriller Crimson Tide, was based on him" — the film has an ultra-nationalist villain "Vladimir Radchenko," but no source ties him to Zhirinovsky: do not teach the link. "Sobchak blamed Yeltsin for these legislative defeats" and Sobchak spreading "the possibility that Zhirinovsky might take over the government" are unfindable as attributed specifics: do not quote them.

"By the end of 1995 Yeltsin, who would have to stand for reelection the following year, was drowning in problems" — confirmed (May 1995 memcon) (see §7). The social ledger: "Russia's suicide rate was also skyrocketing" — national rise confirmed peer-reviewed, but "St Petersburg's was among the highest in the world" is unfindable as a city ranking.

"Diseases like dysentery and diphtheria were running rampant" — diphtheria confirmed (Russia 603 cases in 1989 to 39,703 in 1994; city plus oblast 246 in 1991, CDC); a dysentery epidemic figure is unfindable. "Many areas had rolling blackouts" — city-specific incidents unfindable. "There was a major social, spiritual and collective mental health crisis in 1990s Russia" stands as the chapter's closing frame — and as Putin's motive: "this period was extremely formative on Putin's philosophy of governance".

6. Machinery — the autocrat-ending as governing promise

The mechanism this chapter establishes is legitimation-by-analogy: the standard narrative "that it took a political compromise, specifically the accession of an autocratic ruler, to end the Time of Troubles" is projected onto the 1990s so that "Putin is the modern equivalent of Mikhail Romanov" — "one could even argue that the oligarchs are the modern equivalent of the noble landowning families".

The video admits the stretch ("you could stretch that analogy too far") and keeps the point: "Putin certainly wants you to think that he's the modern Mikhail Romanov". Each crisis in the chapter feeds that machine — the shelling proves parliamentarism fails, "cold peace" proves the West exploits weakness, Chechnya proves the state cannot coerce, the wards and the suicide figures prove society itself is dying — until autocracy reads as rescue rather than regression.

"It is exactly what he wanted to turn his back on once he got into power". The next chapter's "pivotal year" of 1996 then inherits a reader already primed to accept the ending.

8. Open threads

  • Who counts the dead of October? The chapter inherits the video's 187-with-a-nod; the record offers 124–187 against up to 2,000. Which count the course trusts changes how "worst since 1917" reads — still open.
  • Assurance or pledge? The oral record favours Moscow's memory; the paper record favours NATO's denial. Whether "cold peace" was betrayal or tureen-staring stands open — and Kosovo will sharpen it.
  • Could anyone have held Chechnya? Flabby army, blocked columns, a Grozny taken but never governed. Whether a different commander or a different settlement could have ended it in 1995 is the question the next war will answer.

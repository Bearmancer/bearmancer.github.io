Endgame for Power

Chapter 15 of 18 · 2:15:23–2:22:31

Lesson 09 · cast map · glossary · transcript slice

Putin's year at the FSB ends with everyone else's crisis becoming his ladder: a whistleblower scandal he buries, a Kosovo war that turns NATO into the enemy, a sacked premier, a Dagestan invasion — and a summons to Gorki-9. The chapter's arc is convergence: foreign alarm, domestic scandal and a failing presidency line up until the loyal security chief stands a heartbeat from the head of state.

Every claim below carries its verdict inline beside the video's wording, with citations in §7. Names are given in canonical form; caption garbles (Berezovsky, Primakov, Stepashin, Chechnya, Dagestan, Chubais, Gorki-9) are silently corrected.

1. What matters — and what you can skip

Keep these four essentials:

  1. The FSB year is confirmed; the budget trim is not. Putin ran the service from 25 July 1998 to 9 August 1999 and reorganised it thoroughly, dismissing most of the top personnel. The counterterrorism refocus checks out in substance; the budget-trimming detail is the video's gloss, unattested.
  2. The Litvinenko scandal is confirmed; the mafia and TV details are not. FSB officers went public in November 1998 with an assassination-plot claim against Berezovsky, Litvinenko's meeting with Putin went nowhere, and the 2006 murder carries a probably-approved inquiry finding — never a bare fact. "On Russian Mafia orders" and the TV-denouncement staging stay the video's account.
  3. Kosovo and the sackings are confirmed; motives stay split three ways. Three states joined NATO in March 1999, Allied Force ran 78 days from 24 March, Primakov fell on 12 May and Putin was confirmed 233 to 84 with 17 abstentions on 16 August. Whether Primakov fell over the economy, impeachment-eve tactics or popularity threat is contested — teach all three, choose none.
  4. Dagestan and the handover are confirmed; the backstage dialogue is not. The 7 August invasion by Basayev and Khattab, the "two towns" corrected to a multi-village seizure, the 9 August appointment and the counterattack already under way all check out. The father's death year is confirmed but the exact day is unattested, and the Gorki-9 conversation plus the advisers' resistance are carried as the video's account — the first as Putin's own telling.

What can be skimmed: the Slav-protector and July-crisis history (the video's own frame, no verdict), the apartment-bombings teaser (flagged for the next chapter, no verdict here), and the 1997 Asian-crisis chain, which is corrected once — Asia was fuel, the August 1998 default was cause — and then left behind.

3. The FSB year — reorganisation and the whistleblower

"Putin's time as head of the FSB, though brief, was to have lasting consequences" — confirmed as tenure: director from 25 July 1998 to 9 August 1999, "reluctant to take over" yet conducting "a thorough reorganization, which included the dismissal of most of the FSB's top personnel" (FSB account of the Putin reorganisation). "Reorganizing, downsizing, merging departments and trimming down the agency's budget" is partially correct — the reorganisation is the confirmed part; no source ties a budget trim to Putin, so teach the budget line as the video's gloss. "He refocused the FSB's mission... to fight terrorism and political extremism" is confirmed in substance: counterterrorism sits inside the service's domestic remit (same FSB account as above — see §7), with a dedicated counterterrorism directorate in the period structure (GlobalSecurity on the FSB counterterrorism arm).

"Several officers alleged the agency was corrupt and that there was a plot within it to assassinate Berezovsky" — confirmed. In November 1998 Litvinenko and colleagues said "they were ordered to kill the influential Russian oligarch and politician Boris Berezovsky" (DW on the November 1998 press conference); he had "been instructed to kill Berezovsky" and his superiors "threatened him with violence when he refused" (Guardian obituary account). Two trims: "on the orders of the Russian Mafia" is unverified — the sources name FSB superiors, never the mafia — and "Putin denounced Litvinenko on a TV news program" is unverified as staging; what is confirmed is the buried meeting, "not a productive meeting at all" with no action after, following Litvinenko's complaint to the newly appointed FSB head (Guardian on the Litvinenko–Putin meeting). He feared afterwards he would "soon be killed or arrested" (BBC on the aftermath).

"The rogue agent whom Putin would eventually have murdered in 2006" — corrected to attributed finding, never bare fact: poisoned on 1 November 2006 and dead three weeks later, with the 2016 inquiry finding the killing "probably approved" by Putin and Patrushev (same DW report as above — see §7), paired with the full poisoning account (poisoning account with the inquiry finding).

4. Spring 1999 — Primakov, NATO and Kosovo

"Primakov, whom Yeltsin had appointed in the wake of economic problems caused indirectly by the 1997 Asian financial crisis" — corrected on mechanism. Primakov took office on 11 September 1998 after the 17 August 1998 default; the Asian crisis of mid-1997 "fueled a sharp deterioration" as background fuel, not trigger (TASS on the 1998 default chain). "Primakov hated Berezovsky but Putin actively cultivated the oligarch's friendship for strategic reasons" — confirmed as a mutual war, with the motive kept hedged. The pair "were engaged in public attacks on one another" while a Primakov-era investigation pursued Berezovsky, who returned to the centre only after Primakov's fall (Jamestown on the public war); prosecutors issued an arrest warrant over the Aeroflot/Andava affair days after Berezovsky was sacked from the CIS post (Independent on the warrant); Primakov "unleashed the prosecutor general on Berezovsky" (New Yorker on the prosecutor lever). The friendship is confirmed — they met in the early 1990s, "became friends," holidayed and "skied together in Switzerland," and Berezovsky became the kingmaker who "shepherded Putin to power" (Guardian on the friendship and kingmaker role); "for strategic reasons" is the video's motive reading, taught hedged.

"In March of 1999 three new members joined the alliance — Hungary, the Czech Republic and Poland" — confirmed: instruments of accession delivered 12 March 1999 after the 1997 Madrid invitation (State Department on the accession), "the first former members of the Warsaw Pact to join NATO" (NATO on the 1999 enlargement). "For the first time in its 50-year history NATO took coordinated military action, again at the behest of President Clinton" — confirmed with the billing trimmed: Operation Allied Force ran 24 March to 10 June, 78 days, "to halt the humanitarian catastrophe that was then unfolding in Kosovo" (NATO on Allied Force). "First" means first major combat operation; "at Clinton's behest" is shorthand for a US-led alliance decision. "Air strikes against Serbia... to stop the Serbs' repressions against the mostly Muslim province of Kosovo where the Serbs were instigating ethnic cleansing" is confirmed in substance (same NATO page as above — see §7); "forces dominated by British and Americans" is unverified — teach it as the video's gloss. The Slav-protector sentiment and the July-crisis-to-World-War-I passage take no verdict; they are the video's historical frame.

"On May 12th 1999, while the Kosovo war was still going on, Yeltsin fired Primakov as prime minister" — confirmed: dismissed 12 May on stated economic grounds with Stepashin named, one day before impeachment debate opened (RFE/RL on the dismissal); the Kremlin line was economic failure while the press read impeachment-eve tactics and a strike against a rival outshining the president (Guardian on the stated and likely reasons). "Because he feared Primakov, who was popular, might threaten him in the 2000 election" is contested analysis — teach it alongside the economy and impeachment readings, all three live, none chosen. "Yeltsin replaced him with Stepashin... a temporary stopgap... yet another reorganization to survive the Duma elections" is partially correct: the three-month tenure is confirmed — "prime minister for only three months" in the fourth government in 17 months (Guardian on the Stepashin fall) — with December parliamentary elections indeed looming (same Guardian report as above — see §7); "stopgap to survive" is the video's motive reading, taught hedged.

5. Dagestan and the August handover

"There was another restive region just next door called Dagestan; in the summer of 1999 Islamist extremists established two breakaway towns across the border and Chechen separatists flocked to defend them" — confirmed with the count corrected. On 7 August 1999 Basayev and Khattab led "up to 2,000" fighters into Dagestan in support of separatist rebels, seized villages and retreated into Chechnya by mid-September (Dagestan-war account); the first wave ran "between 200 and 500 armed men" around mountain villages with a second September wave, aimed at an Islamic state (GlobalSecurity on the incursions); the August infiltration stands as prelude to the Second Chechen War (Second-War account). "Two towns" compresses a multi-village seizure — teach corrected. "Stepashin urged Yeltsin to invade Chechnya again in spring 1999 but Kosovo postponed that" is unverified — no source in three passes; teach it as the video's account only.

"In early August 1999 Putin was in St Petersburg where his father, sick with cancer, passed away on August 5th" — partially correct: the father belongs to 1911–1999, confirmed (Putin family account), but the exact 5 August day is unattested — teach the season, never the day. "Yeltsin called Putin to his dacha outside Moscow called Gorki-9 and told him he would appoint him prime minister" takes no verdict beyond Putin's own telling — it is self-account, carried as such. The reported reply — "I had not thought about that, I don't know if I am prepared for that" — is likewise Putin's own words, cited to the video, not the record. "Yeltsin's advisers including Chubais were skeptical... feared the Duma would refuse to confirm him and trigger early elections" is unverified as stated — no source names Chubais objecting; contemporaneous doubt is real (a fifth premier in 17 months) but the names and the mechanism stay the video's account.

"On August 9th 1999 Vladimir Putin was appointed prime minister... approved a week later on August 16th, narrowly" — confirmed. Yeltsin fired Stepashin on 9 August and named the FSB head and Security Council secretary acting premier, hinting at the succession (RFE/RL on the 9 August appointment); the Duma confirmed him by "233... 84 voted against and 17 abstained" against a 226 threshold (RFE/RL on the confirmation vote), with 439 participating (same figures in the TASS biography — TASS on the appointment and vote). "The Russian army was already counterattacking in Dagestan" is confirmed — Stepashin himself "had flown to the region to take charge of the Russian counterattack" (same Guardian 9-August report as above — see §7). "Yeltsin's heart was weak and already ravaged by numerous heart attacks" is confirmed: the full 1995–1999 record of bypass, pneumonias and hospital months (BBC health record), and a presidency "plagued for years by heart and other health problems" (CBS on the caretaker presidency).

"Perhaps the most infamous event — the Moscow apartment bombings of 1999" — no verdict; it is a flagged teaser for the next chapter, carried as the video's frame. "A heartbeat away from being the Russian head of state" is the video's closing line, not a claim.

6. Machinery — convergence as selection

The mechanism this chapter establishes is convergence-as-selection: the buried FSB scandal removes Putin's vulnerability by removing his accuser's audience, the Kosovo shock converts NATO from theoretical irritant to potential enemy, and the premiership's three-month turnover burns through every alternative until only the security chief remains. Whether fear, loyalty or kingmaker design moved each piece is the video's psychology; the appointments, votes and invasions are record.

Putin "starting to see the end game" and the long Chechnya problem "blowing up in Yeltsin's face" are the video's readings of motive and timing — carried as frame. What the record supplies is the sequence: 12 March enlargement, 24 March bombing start, 12 May sack, 7 August invasion, 9 August appointment, 16 August confirmation. The chapter ends where the next begins: with the bombings unexamined and the succession one weak heart away from completion.

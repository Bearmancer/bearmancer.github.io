An obscure prime minister fetches his crown in three moves: a debut in Auckland, a war that turns 2 percent into a frontrunner, and a resignation timed to make him the incumbent. The chapter's arc is succession as stagecraft — every dated act confirmed, every number taught as a range, and the video's adjectives carried as the video's, never as record.

Every claim below carries its verdict inline beside the video's wording, with citations in §7. This is a mechanics chapter: the Auckland substitution, the poll climb, the Duma count and the New Year's Eve handover are all dated and sourced, while the side portraits — Clinton's read of Putin, the vermin sentence, the farewell's tone — stay attributed to the video wherever no second carrier exists. Names are given in canonical form; caption garbles (Apex, Chechnya variants, Berezovsky, Yedinstvo, Guryanova-style slips aside) are silently corrected.

1. What matters — and what you can skip

Keep these four essentials:

  1. Auckland is confirmed as debut and tip-off. Yeltsin stays home, Putin goes in his place, and Yeltsin tells Clinton beforehand that Putin will be backed as the 2000 candidate — memorably capped at Istanbul with "Putin will be the successor." The prior St Petersburg meeting is confirmed; Clinton's harsh-security displeasure and his formidable-but-not-taken-in read are unverified video-only portraits.
  2. The war makes the ratings; every figure is a range. The 50,000-strong force is confirmed as a contemporary estimate, Grozny's ruin is confirmed through the winter siege, and the polls run August 1–2 percent to roughly 30 percent in November (the video's figure, trajectory-consistent) to 49–51 percent by year's end. Pavlovsky's danger counsel is confirmed in substance through his own account; the cemetery wording is the video's quotation.
  3. The Duma count is confirmed with one range inside it. The vote sits on 19 December 1999, the Communists hold 113 of 450, and Putin's Unity sits at 72–73 with the ORT dirty-campaign charge carried beside its denial. The resignation offer is contested in date (14–22 December across three accounts) and confirmed in substance: an incumbency head-start with the election pulled forward from June.
  4. The handover is confirmed act by act; its tone is the video's. The forgiveness plea, the three-month acting presidency, the nuclear briefcase and the "take care of Russia" parting words are all sourced, and the Millennium article is banked as Putin's published program. "Feeble," "dull," "halting" and "rise complete" take no verdict; they are the video's frame.

What can be skimmed: the Clinton–St Petersburg backstory after the prior meeting is banked (the security detail is video-only), the vermin sentence after it is marked as the video's wording (the era's verified harsh line is the outhouse one from the previous chapter), and the Y2K-party backdrop, which sets the scene without deciding anything.

3. Auckland — the debut and the tip

"President Boris Yeltsin cancelled at the last minute his scheduled trip to the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation conference in Auckland, New Zealand; he decided to send Putin in his place" — confirmed. "President Yeltsin is not going. Prime Minister Putin is going," with Yeltsin "glad you are meeting with Prime Minister Putin" (White House briefing with the substitution). "Yeltsin was supposed to meet with President Bill Clinton; now it was Putin meeting with him" is the same substitution in the video's words, and "Yeltsin even told Clinton when informing him of the change that Putin was probably going to be the next president of Russia" is confirmed as the succession tip: "[Putin] will be supported as a candidate in the year 2000. We are working on it accordingly," capped months later at Istanbul with "Putin, of course. He will be the successor to Boris Yeltsin" (National Security Archive with the phone call and both bilaterals).

"The two leaders had met before; Clinton toured St Petersburg in the nineties on a trip for which Putin was responsible for arranging security" — partially correct: the prior meeting is confirmed while the security detail is not. "Putin reminds Clinton that they have already met face-to-face in St. Petersburg when Putin worked for the liberal Mayor Anatoly Sobchak" (same Archive account as above — see §7); a dedicated counter-search found no carrier for the security-arrangement half or for "Clinton in fact hadn't liked that visit and felt the security measures were too harsh" — teach the meeting confirmed and the harshness as the video's detail. "They tried to size each other up" and "Clinton thought Putin was formidable and capable but he wasn't taken in as George W. Bush and Donald Trump would eventually be" are unverified: the memcon shows Clinton lecturing on elections while Putin answers with the faces-not-programs line (same Archive account as above — see §7), and no citable Clinton assessment of Putin from Auckland was found — teach both sentences as the video's read. "His first turn on the world stage as a potential leader," "most of the world missed its importance," "Yeltsin was sick and starting to fade," and "virtually unknown outside Russia in 1999" are confirmed in substance: the "previously obscure Putin" (same Archive account as above — see §7), the "first meeting with the new Russian Prime Minister" (same White House briefing as above — see §7), and "poor health has led to increasing doubts about his ability" (BBC with the resignation-day health context).

4. The war that makes the frontrunner

"By the end of September the Russian invasion force was gauged at 50,000 troops" — confirmed as a contemporary estimate, never a headcount: Putin "did not rule out a full-scale invasion by the estimated 50,000 troops" gathered around the republic (RFE/RL with the 30 September force report). "We must stamp out the vermin; if we do not do it today it will be worse tomorrow" is unverified as a sentence: the era's "stamp out" idiom is real, but the vermin wording was found in no source after a dedicated search — teach it as the video's wording, and note the verified September harsh line is the outhouse one banked in the previous chapter. "Within a few months Russian forces had turned Grozny into a pile of smoking rubble" is confirmed in substance: the siege runs December 1999 to February 2000, and "in 2003, the United Nations called Grozny the most destroyed city on Earth" (Second-War account with the siege and the UN verdict). "He was prime minister, not president, and his boss Yeltsin had signed a ceasefire with Chechnya in 1996; Putin was publicly repudiating the policy of his own superior while Yeltsin was still in office" is confirmed: the accord ending the first war was "signed in Khasavyurt in Dagestan on 30 August 1996 between Alexander Lebed and Aslan Maskhadov" (Khasavyurt account with the signatories), and the second war runs "from August 1999 to April 2009" (same Second-War account as above — see §7).

"He was hesitant at first and a little unsure of himself; that was the opinion of Gleb Pavlovsky" with the "hoped that he would distance himself from Chechnya because without exception all politicians who have taken on Chechen wars have bitten the dust; it is a political graveyard" quotation — partially correct, with the substance confirmed and the wording attributed. Pavlovsky's own account gives the danger counsel and the outcome: "Chechnya was a very dangerous thing... Everyone who dealt with Chechnya, they all lost," matched with "the decision to start a war in Chechnya was his decision. It was his decision to go to the end" (Frontline interview with the adviser); the exact cemetery sentences are the video's quotation — teach them as such. "In August 1999 Putin had about 2% support; by November he was at 30% and rising; 30 or 40% in December is an overwhelming frontrunner in multi-party Russia" is corrected to ranges: "approval rating was only 2 percent" for the first five weeks with "spectacular increases in the fall of 1999" (National Security Archive with the Embassy polling record), "stood at 1 to 2 percent... climbing at about 5 percent per week, until by the end of the year it stood at about 49 percent" (Wilson Center with the pollster's trajectory), and "if in August 1999 only 2%... by the end of the year... 51%" (rise account with the end-year figure). Teach August at 1–2 percent, November at roughly 30 percent as the video's figure on a supported trajectory, and year's end at 49–51 percent — never a single December number. The career retrospective — obscure St Petersburg official in the summer of 1996, number two three years later deciding war alone, never a candidate, none of deputy mayor, deputy administration head, FSB chief or premier requiring a single vote — is confirmed in substance: the rise from "second-tier roles in the presidential administration, the intelligence services, and the Security Council, to the presumptive presidential winner" (same first-election account as above — see §7) after appointment as the "fourth prime minister in 17 months" to a public that found him "virtually unknown" (Meduza with the succession narrative).

5. The Duma and the dated offer

"The elections for the Duma were scheduled for December 19th 1999; it was a dirty and acrimonious campaign" — confirmed. "Legislative elections were held in Russia on 19 December 1999 to elect the 450 seats in the State Duma" (legislative account with the date and seat table), and the campaign ran through a "smear" drive of "corruption and murder" charges via the Kremlin-linked airwaves (Christian Science Monitor with the ORT machinery). "The television network owned by Boris Berezovsky pitched in to talk up Yeltsin's party and firehose mud at the opposition" is confirmed as a reported charge: "television station ORT, in which a major stake is held by... Boris Berezovsky," orchestrated the discredit drive (same Monitor account as above — see §7), and "ORT did indeed mount a vigorous and, according to many, dirty campaign to support Unity and Putin and to discredit their enemies" (Jamestown with the funding-and-airwave claim). "Berezovsky obviously expected a quid pro quo to keep the oligarch fortunes intact; spoiler, there would be none" is confirmed as expectation with the denial riding alongside: the financing claim for Unity and the presidential campaign sits in the same Jamestown report beside its denial by the Unity faction head and the elections chief (same Jamestown account as above — see §7).

"On December 14th, five days before the election, Yeltsin met with Putin and told him he was going to resign; Putin would be his successor" — contested in date, confirmed in substance. The memoir dates the first offer conversation to December 14 while post-resignation reporting places it on December 22, three days after the vote (same Meduza succession narrative as above — see §7), and Putin's own account puts the interruption "about ten days before the New Year" — the later reading (see §6). Teach 14–22 December as disputed, never the 14th alone. "Give Putin the incumbent's advantage in the June election and spare him Yeltsin's further missteps; it couldn't be announced before the Duma vote" is confirmed as mechanism: "bringing the date of the election forward three months... made it harder for Putin's opponents to organise" while letting him "act on his popularity while it remains at its height" (same BBC resignation-day account as above — see §7). "It would be stupid to say no; I'd rather sell sunflower seeds" is confirmed as Putin's own telling: "it would have been foolish to say, no, I am going to go and sell sunflower seeds instead, or go into private legal practice" (official-biography mirror with the recollection). "The Communist Party won the largest single bloc, 113 seats out of 450; Putin's Yedinstvo won 73 and with two other anti-communist parties would have a working majority" is corrected to carry the one-seat dissent: 113 and 73 per the settled table (same legislative account as above — see §7; Essex archive with the matching 113 and 73) against "Unity... took 72 seats" (IPU parline with the 72 variant). Teach Unity at 72–73 and the working-majority arithmetic as the video's reading.

6. Machinery — incumbency as selection

The mechanism this chapter establishes is incumbency-as-selection: the resignation manufactures the incumbent it pretends merely to reveal. "On December 27th Yeltsin and Putin met again to hammer out the transfer details; Putin was already working on the Millennium message" — partially correct. Putin's account confirms a late-December follow-up — "we met again, I think, on December 27 or 26" — with the Bethlehem-trip question on the table, and separately confirms the "about ten days" interruption in which Yeltsin demanded the answer at once: "He said: You have had enough time to think already, answer now" (Los Angeles Times with the January interview). The "hammer out the transfer" half is the video's gloss; the meeting itself is Putin-reported. The Millennium piece is confirmed as program: "an article... entitled Russia at the Threshold of the Millennium" on the new cabinet site, arguing the country "needs a tough government" and "strong state power" (Guardian with the 30 December article). "The world preparing for the big Y2K party would overshadow the handover in the headlines" takes no verdict; it is the video's scene-setting, carried as such.

"On the afternoon of New Year's Eve Yeltsin gave a feeble and forgettable farewell address begging forgiveness for his mistakes, looking old and sick" — corrected to separate record from adjectives. The plea is confirmed: "I want to ask you for forgiveness, because many of our hopes have not come true" (BBC with the full speech text); "feeble," "forgettable," "old and sick" are the video's adjectives, with only the health-doubt context sourced (same BBC resignation-day account as above — see §7). "The last thing Yeltsin said walking out was 'take care of Russia'" is confirmed as Putin-reported: "His words were simple: Take care of Russia" (same Los Angeles Times interview as above — see §7), independently given as "His parting words to Putin were just: Take care of Russia" (RBTH with the farewell-day reconstruction). "A dull halting speech before a Christmas tree while the world partied" and "his rise to power was now complete" take no verdict; they are the video's closing judgments, and the lesson keeps them that way.

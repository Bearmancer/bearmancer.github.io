Putin: The Rise To Power · chapter 10 of 18

The Big Swindle

Video 1:21:29–1:30:54 · Lesson 0004 · ~9 min · full chapter transcript

Nine minutes on the episode that almost ended Putin's career before it started: a winter food crisis, a barter scheme worth about $100m in city commodities, licensee firms that sold the cargo and never sent the food — and a council investigation that voted to sack him. The chapter's argument is that this was the most serious misstep of his early career, and that the record leaves the worst question open: swindled fixer, or willing accomplice? This lesson walks the chapter, then sorts what the record confirms from what it corrects.

Scope — boundary

This lesson covers chapter 10 only (1:21:29–1:30:54): the late-1991 food crisis, the KVS barter licences, the Salye commission, and the aftermath that kept Putin in his job. Putin's later city-government years, which the chapter teases in its last minute, belong to the next chapter.

1. What matters — and what you can skip

Keep these four essentials:

  1. The deal and its scale. Putin's KVS committee licensed firms to export about $100m in commodities (reports range $92–124m) for food for a city of some 5 million; the food never arrived and the shell firms vanished.
  2. Oversight failed; enrichment is unproven. The Salye commission charged failures of oversight — not personal corruption — and recommended dismissing both Putin and his deputy Anikin; prosecutors refused the case. No personal gain on this deal is proven.
  3. The two readings. The chapter's own pair: the charitable reading (chaos, corner-cutting, betrayal by his own contacts) against the sinister reading (kickbacks arranged or acquiesced in) — with the truth possibly between the poles.
  4. Survival, not exoneration. Sobchak refused to sack Putin, the city neither starved nor rioted, and the scandal became exhibit A against him anyway — "stealing food from the starving people of St Petersburg."

What can be skimmed: the Stalin-war-effort aside (colour for the old distribution system, not mechanism); the backyard-garden callback (scene-setting for how the city coped); the voucher-privatisation mechanics, which sit in the economics aid, cited here not re-argued. SPAG, Tambov, Gunvor, German raids and Schröder get zero mentions in this slice — they belong to a later chapter, not this one.

3. A city that could not feed itself

The chapter opens with the stakes stated outright: the 1992 food scandal "almost single-handedly" ended Putin's rise, and had the fallout been worse, "the whole course of global history since 1999 might be different" 1:21:41. Then the mechanism of the crisis. Under the Soviet system the breadbaskets — Ukraine above all — gave their produce to the state, which distributed it; after December 1991 that distribution system shut off. The shortage was logistical, not absolute: food existed in the former Union but could not reach the cities, and Leningrad was "particularly vulnerable" 1:23:00. People grew what they could in individual and community gardens — the Putin family had done the same in their 1950s courtyard — but gardens could not replace the state supply. The video flags this early: "remember that, it becomes important later" 1:23:21.

Sobchak charged Putin with getting food onto the docks before the populace starved and rioted 1:23:25. Putin was the man for it: by now chairman of St Petersburg's Foreign Relations Committee, with experience dealing with foreign companies — and with no cash in the city, only barter to offer. What went out was raw material, "much of it looted from the Soviet military or other arms of the now dissolved Soviet state" 1:23:56: timber, copper, fuel oil, scrap metal — the video's list adds "concrete," which is Munger's colour, not a document term (see §6). What was meant to come back: potatoes, sugar, meat, dried milk, butter, baby formula, feed for livestock.

"The deal was worth the equivalent of 122 million US for enough food hopefully to get the city through the winter."

— the video on the barter's scale 1:24:30

Teach the value with its range: about $100m, with reports spanning $92–124m — the video's $122m sits at the top of that range (see §6). Putin signed licensing agreements with several import-export companies; the commodities went out of St Petersburg, and "very little if any of the food that these companies supposedly contracted for actually arrived" 1:24:55. The firms sold the raw materials on the open market and kept the proceeds — and the contracts let them take commissions "in some cases between 25 and 50%" 1:25:14. "In other words it was a huge swindle" 1:25:17.

4. Friends, contracts, and two readings

Then the turn from failure to suspicion: "some of the import export companies were owned by Putin's friends and associates, such as the brother of one of Putin's old law school friends" 1:25:21. That last identification is unfindable — no source names the brother, and it is taught here as allegation only (see §6). What is confirmed is the Timchenko end of the same pattern: Gennady Timchenko at Kirishineftekhimexport, one of the named enterprises, with a March 1992 city document tying the firm to a 100,000-ton diesel barter. The appearance, the video says, was that "Putin had arranged the whole thing to provide kickbacks and cushy profits to his friends while people's bellies rumbled back home" 1:25:31 — and it asks the chapter's central question: "is that a fair reading of the situation?" 1:25:39

The record's answer, the video admits, "is unclear" 1:25:43. The contracts were incompetently drawn — "a high school student could have done better," in Philip Short's verdict as the video reports it 1:25:51 — with most carrying no penalty language for non-delivery, and the rest probably unenforceable in a Russia where "no one knew what laws applied to anything" 1:26:11. The exact "13 contracts, 8 without penalties" wording is unfindable in Short's text; teach the substance — Munger citing Short — not the count (see §6). Out of that fog the video builds its charitable reading: with state channels gone, informal networks of friends were "kind of the only way to get things done" 1:26:34, and Putin turned to the only people who might help, cut corners on a bare hope — "then he got swindled by those unscrupulous import export guys" 1:26:55. Against it, the opposite end of the spectrum: he arranged or acquiesced in the whole thing "knowing the food would never arrive" 1:27:10, gaining at least "valuable contacts with the West that he could use in the future" 1:27:17.

"I think we're safe in discerning at least some nefarious motives behind the deal — though I stress again the historical record isn't clear."

— the video's own verdict 1:28:14

Munger leans sinister but will not close the case: "it may not be a zero sum game. The truth may lie somewhere between those two polls" 1:27:32. His one structural argument for suspicion is worth keeping: thirty years on, nobody knows who actually supplied the raw materials — "the records have all been lost" 1:27:46 — and if the firms had truly burned Putin, an innocent man would never trust them again, forfeiting "the bargain basement flea market that Russia had become" 1:28:07. Yet they stayed in his orbit.

The scandal broke into an investigation: the city government "opened an investigation and recommended to Sobchak that he fire Putin and another city official called Anikin" 1:27:25 — Aleksandr Anikin, Putin's deputy, whom the commission charged alongside him. "Anikin was eventually fired," the video adds 1:28:33; that firing is unfindable and likely wrong (see §6). "Sobchak refused to sack Putin" 1:28:36 — that part is confirmed. Leading the charge was Marina Salye, chair of the food-supplies commission and a leader of the Lensovet faction whose deadlock had lifted Sobchak to the chairmanship in the first place: "in other words she was a political enemy of Sobchak" 1:28:56. Her enmity is real, and so — the video insists — may be her evidence: "that doesn't mean that Putin didn't do what she accused him of doing. He probably did" 1:29:40.

Salye's second charge predates the scandal by a year. In May 1991 she went to Berlin to arrange meat and potato imports, found Putin had already made a deal and cut her out, and "determined also that the food in this deal was shipped not to Leningrad but to Moscow" 1:29:19 — a 60-tonne meat order, on her account. Single-sourced to Salye via Munger; teach as allegation (see §6). The chapter closes with the uncontested outcome and two unfindable explanations: a $500m Spanish-bank food loan that "did in this case arrive," a milder winter, and the vegetable gardens — St Petersburg "neither starved nor rioted, and Putin kept his job" 1:30:16. The loan and the weather-and-gardens rescue are both unfindable; the survival without famine or riots is not disputed, and its causes are (see §6).

5. Machinery — licences without oversight

The mechanism this chapter establishes is licensing without enforcement: a committee chairman signs export licences to firms he chose through personal networks, the contracts omit or neuter penalties, the cargo leaves, and nothing in the paper compels anything to come back. The 25–50% commissions (via Dawisha and Wilson) are part of the same machinery — firms paid themselves first by design, not by theft after the fact. That is what the commission could prove, and all it charged: oversight failures, not personal corruption. Personal enrichment on this deal is not proven (TBIJ), and the distinction matters because the video's rhetoric — "stealing food from the starving people of St Petersburg is a pretty low thing to do" 1:30:38 — invites the stronger charge the paper trail never sustained.

The second mechanism is political survival converting a paper defeat into a career non-event. The commission recommended dismissal; prosecutors refused the case; the mayor refused the sack vote — and the man who should have been fired stayed, with no prosecution of the food deal ever following. The later case numbers that surface around this affair — #114128, #18/95-238278, 144128 — belong to separate, later proceedings, each delayed for years and dropped on immunity or insufficient proof; none is a prosecution of the food deal, and §6 keeps them distinct. Putin's on-record denials run alongside all of it: wrongdoing denied and the companies blamed in First Person, later "nothing and nobody to sue" and a "KGB-smear" dismissal. The record contradicts the denial where signatures and oversight are concerned — and nowhere else.

6. Fact-check — the record behind the chapter

Claim by claim: confirmed where the record agrees, corrected where it differs, allegation where only one mouth says it. State sources are labelled as such.

  • The crisis and the barter outline — confirmed. Late-1991 food crisis in a city of ~5m; the KVS committee signed export-for-food deals and the food never arrived while shell firms vanished (RFE/RL; Salye obituaries in the Boston Globe and NYT; the workspace aid for the rationing backdrop). Correction: "concrete" in the video's barter list is Munger's colour, not a document term.
  • Putin's chairmanship — confirmed. He chaired the KVS Foreign Relations Committee after Sobchak's 12 June 1991 mayoral win; licences named enterprises including Kirishineftekhimexport.
  • The value — teach with the range. About $100m, with reports spanning $92–124m (RFE/RL ~$100m; Channel 4/Salye $124m; Guardian/SMH $92m). The video's "$122m" sits at the top of the range, not its middle.
  • Oversight proven, enrichment not (TBIJ). The commission's paper proves failures of oversight; personal enrichment on this deal is not proven. The lesson keeps that line exactly where the record draws it.
  • 25–50% commissions — confirmed via Dawisha and Wilson. Contracts improperly prepared, commissions up to half, firms vanished.
  • Timchenko confirmed; the "brother" unfindable. Timchenko at Kirishineftekhimexport is confirmed (openDemocracy; a 23 March 1992 city document: 100,000 tons of diesel for barter). "Brother of a law-school friend" names nobody in any source — do not teach it as fact.
  • "13 contracts / 8 no-penalty" and the Short line — attribute, don't quote. The exact wording is unfindable in Short; teach the substance as "Munger, citing Short": penalty clauses mostly absent, the rest likely unenforceable.
  • Both men charged; neither sacked on the vote. Correction: "Anikin was fired" is unfindable and likely wrong. His later promotion is reported but dated 1994 — do not tie it to the 1992 vote.
  • Salye — confirmed in outline. She chaired the food commission, led the Radical Democrats, and moved in the Sakharov circle (Guardian and NYT obituaries).
  • May 1991 Berlin trip and the 60-tonne meat order — Salye's allegation. Her account only, via Munger; "shipped to Moscow" is single-sourced. Teach as allegation.
  • No prosecution of the food deal — keep the later cases separate. #114128 (St Petersburg Fuel Company/Tambov fuel monopoly) and #18/95-238278 (mayor's contingency fund) were delayed for years and dropped on immunity; case 144128 (Twentieth Trust, opened June 1999, closed 2000) ended for insufficient proof.   • Putin's denial — on record, and contradicted in part. First Person denies wrongdoing and blames the companies; later "nothing and nobody to sue" (Globe) and a "KGB-smear" dismissal (Channel 4, 2012). The record contradicts the denial on signatures and oversight — not on personal gain.
  • Spanish loan, mild winter, gardens — unfindable as causes. The "$500m Spanish-bank food loan" and the "mild winter plus gardens saved the city" accounts are both unfindable. Uncontested: no famine, no riots. The causes are disputed — teach the outcome, not the mechanism.

7. Open threads

  • Who supplied the raw materials? The records are lost, and thirty years on nobody knows whose timber, metals and fuel actually left the docks — the cargo has no documented owner.
  • Swindled or complicit? The chapter's own pair of readings stands unresolved at the boundary: the corner-cutting fixer betrayed by his contacts, or the arranger who never cared whether the food arrived — "it may not be a zero sum game."
  • Does the Berlin charge hold? Salye's May 1991 meat-and-potatoes account predates the scandal and suggests a pattern — but it rests on her word alone, and the food's Moscow destination is single-sourced.

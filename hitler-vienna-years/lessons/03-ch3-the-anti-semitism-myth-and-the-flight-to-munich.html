The Anti-Semitism Myth and the Flight to Munich

Hitler later claimed he underwent an early, decisive conversion to anti-Semitism in Vienna. Modern historians, most notably Brigitte Hamann, have shown this to be a self-invented autobiographical myth. In reality, Hitler maintained cordial daily interactions and commercial partnerships with Jewish residents in the Meldemannstrasse men's home, selling paintings to Jewish dealers and living alongside them without apparent friction.

By 1913, Hitler was ready to leave Vienna. On May 16, he collected an inheritance payout of nearly 820 crowns from the district court in Linz. On May 25, he moved to Munich, renting a fourth-floor room from tailor Joseph Popp on Schleissheimerstrasse with Rudolf Häusler.

When Austria-Hungary moved toward war in 1914, Hitler sought to avoid service in the Austro-Hungarian army. A Salzburg military medical commission declared him physically unfit for combat or auxiliary military service on February 5, 1914. Rather than face conscription in Austria, Hitler had already established himself in Munich—where he would soon volunteer for the Bavarian Army at the outbreak of World War I.

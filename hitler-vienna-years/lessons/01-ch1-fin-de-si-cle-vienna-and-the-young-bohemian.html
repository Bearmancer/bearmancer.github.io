Fin-de-Siècle Vienna and the Young Bohemian

Adolf Hitler spent five years in Vienna from 1908 to 1913, a period he later described in Mein Kampf as his most difficult and thorough school. At the time, Vienna was the imperial capital of the Austro-Hungarian Empire and Europe's fourth-largest city, home to around two million people. Its population had doubled between 1880 and 1910, driven largely by Czech immigration, which made up roughly one-fifth of residents by 1910. A diverse metropolis, it was also home to more than 175,000 Jews—about 8.7% of the population.

In late February 1908, Hitler and August Kubizek rented a room together in Maria Zakrey's apartment on Stumpergasse. There, Hitler lived a bohemian existence, spending long nights reading Germanic mythology, heroic sagas, and architectural histories. He rejected Viennese modernism and abstraction, preferring the traditional romantic works of Arnold Böcklin and Eduard Grützner.

Hitler's ambition was to become a painter, but his hopes were repeatedly dashed. He applied to the Academy of Fine Arts Vienna a second time in September 1908 and was rejected without being invited to take the entrance examination. This second rejection marked a decisive turning point, leaving him adrift in the city and setting the stage for the destitution that would define much of his remaining time in Vienna.

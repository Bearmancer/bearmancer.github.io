Political Influences, the Men's Home, and the Flight to Munich

Hitler arrived in Vienna a rejected art student and left it, five years later, a man with a political education. The city gave him two very different teachers, two shelters in place of a home, a business partnership that ended in a courtroom, and a memoir story about his own conversion that historians have since dismantled. This chapter follows those years from the street politics of fin-de-siècle Vienna to the train he caught to Munich in 1913.

The first teacher: Georg von Schönerer

The more radical of the two teachers was Georg Ritter von Schönerer, a wealthy Waldviertel landowner who had sat in the Imperial Council since 1873 and led the pan-German movement in Austria. Pan-Germanism, as he practised it, meant that the German-speaking part of Austria should abandon the Habsburg empire and attach itself to Bismarck's German Reich. His 1882 Linz Program — drafted with allies who later broke with him over its direction — demanded German as the sole official language of the Austrian half of the monarchy and a customs union with Germany. In 1885 he added the Aryan paragraph to the program: membership in the movement was to be restricted to people of German and no Jewish descent. That one clause matters more than everything else he wrote, because it redefined the political enemy not as a rival party or a rival faith but as a race — the idea Hitler's own movement later made its core.[1]

Schönerer's answer to the Habsburg-Catholic bond was the Los von Rom ("Away from Rome") movement, which pressed German Austrians to leave the Catholic church for Protestantism, and he himself converted in 1900. His followers wore blue cornflowers in their buttonholes as a badge of loyalty to the Bismarckian Germany the empire stood against. They addressed him as "the Führer" and greeted each other with "Heil" — vocabulary that would be heard again, in a very different register, after the First World War. Hannah Arendt later called Schönerer Hitler's "spiritual father", and his imprint is easy to trace: racial ideology, a single enemy, an authoritarian leader-cult, and the dream of Anschluss with Germany all travelled from his movement into Hitler's.[1]

The second teacher: Karl Lueger

Karl Lueger, founder of the Christian Social Party, worked the same city as a mass politician rather than an ideologue. Viennese voters elected him mayor five times, and Emperor Franz Joseph refused to confirm him four times before Pope Leo XIII's intercession finally opened the office to him in 1897; he held it until his death in 1910. In office he was a genuine city-builder: gas and electricity municipalised, streetcars and hospitals and schools expanded, suburbs incorporated, and the Second Mountain Spring Aqueduct bringing alpine water into Viennese taps. He was also a pioneer of modern populism, and he discovered early that raising the "Jewish question" earned enormous applause among petit-bourgeois tradesmen who resented their competitors.[2]

But Lueger's antisemitism was flexible rather than doctrinal. He kept Jewish friends, and his most quoted remark — "I decide who is a Jew" — says more about a mayor's cynicism than about a campaign of persecution. The Viennese Jewish writer Stefan Zweig, who grew up under his administration, recalled it as perfectly just. Hitler later paid Lueger an enthusiastic tribute in Mein Kampf, seeing in him a model of how a mass movement is actually built. The standard reading of the pairing is blunt: from Schönerer Hitler absorbed a racial worldview; from Lueger, the craft of turning grievance into ballots and crowds into a movement.[2]

Destitution: the Meidling shelter and the men's home

While Vienna's politicians staged their battles, Hitler's private fortunes collapsed. By late autumn 1909 he was destitute, in and out of the Meidling homeless shelter. On December 21, 1909, in the Meidling shelter, he met Reinhold Hanisch, a Bohemian casual labourer who took him under his wing — and by February 1910 both men were lodged in the municipal men's dormitory on Meldemannstrasse, a modern institution in the working-class Brigittenau district. Hitler entered the home on February 9, 1910, and stayed until 1913.[3]

The painting trade and the quarrel with Hanisch

The years at the men's home supported Hitler through an unusual trade: he painted postcards and copyist watercolors of Viennese landmarks and buildings, and business partners carried them to the shops that would resell them. In early 1910 the seller was Hanisch, splitting the proceeds equally. Regular buyers included the Jewish frame dealer Jakob Altenberg and the Jewish picture dealer Samuel Morgenstern. The partnership broke that summer, Hitler accusing Hanisch of selling one of his paintings — a view of the Parliament building — and keeping the entire proceeds for himself; Hanisch denied it. Within days the two were filing complaints against one another at the local police station; the police found that Hanisch was living in Vienna under the false name Fritz Walter, and on August 11, 1910, a Viennese court sentenced Hanisch to seven days in prison. Hitler reported Hanisch; Hanisch, for his part, answered the courtroom in kind with an anonymous 1912 complaint about the unearned title “academic painter”, probably at Hanisch's instigation.[3]

The quarrel outlived the First World War, in which Hanisch served as a soldier from 1914 to 1917. After 1930 he made a small industry of his memories — selling forged “Hitler” watercolors of his own making and telling newspapers of the man he had known in the dormitory. He was repeatedly convicted: a fraud charge in 1933 that Hitler himself had instructed to be filed, and again in December 1936, dying — officially of a heart attack — in February 1937 after two months in custody. Some biographers, notably Joachim Fest, believe he was killed on Hitler's orders; the prison records say only heart attack. Either way, he is one of the few independent witnesses to Hitler's Vienna years, and his testimony has been examined and found largely reliable by historians.[3]

The witnesses against the memoir

What Hanisch's account chiefly undermines is the story Hitler told about himself. Mein Kampf presents the Vienna years as the schooling of a convinced antisemite, with a story — Hitler's own — about how he was converted to hatred there. The record in the dormitory and the painting trade says otherwise: the best friend of his home years, by Hanisch's account and the historians who checked it, was Josef Neumann, a Jewish copper cleaner; he had Jewish business partners and buyers in Morgenstern and Altenberg; he lived, worked, and quarrelled alongside Jewish residents without apparent friction. Brigitte Hamann's study of Hitler's Vienna, the modern standard work, concludes that the dramatic early conversion is a self-invented autobiographical myth — written after the fact to make the biography tidy.[3]

The flight to Munich

By 1913 the men's home years were ending. On May 16, 1913, Hitler collected his father's inheritance of about 820 crowns from the district court in Linz, and on May 25 he left Vienna for Munich, taking a rented room from tailor Joseph Popp on Schleissheimerstrasse that he shared with Rudolf Häusler, a recent acquaintance from Linz. Munich was the right city for the politics he had absorbed — and, it later turned out, an escape: when the war came, the Salzburg commission exempted Hitler from the Austro-Hungarian army as unfit in February 1914, months before he volunteered for a Bavarian regiment in August 1914. He had walked past his own conscription into his adoptive country's army.

Aa

1858–1961

Summary

The opening chapter runs from the French attack on Da Nang in 1858 to the founding of the National Liberation Front in December 1960 and John F. Kennedy's inauguration. It follows Ho Chi Minh's three decades of exile, the Viet Minh's war against France, Washington's drift from neutrality to paying most of that war's costs, the partition agreed at Geneva, and Ngo Dinh Diem's republic in the south.

The French Conquest

French ambition in Vietnam began with a military assault on the ancient port of Da Nang in 1858, where Franco-Spanish ships stormed the harbour defences. The Encyclopaedia Britannica history of the conquest of Vietnam places the landing on 1 September 1858 and describes a campaign that took half a century to complete. Protectorates over Annam and Tonkin followed in 1883, Cambodia was absorbed in 1863 and Laos in 1893, and by 1897 France administered the whole region directly. Resistance was immediate and never extinct. The Vietnamese had survived a thousand years of Chinese rule by fighting it, and under France anyone who dared resist risked exile, prison or the guillotine. By the 1920s nationalism was rising and a generation of leaders was emerging from that pressure, among them a slender young man named Nguyen Tat Thanh.

Ho Chi Minh's Long Exile

Ho Chi Minh was born in 1890, the son of a poor country scholar rather than an official of the French regime, as the Encyclopaedia Britannica biography of Ho Chi Minh makes clear. Expelled from school after joining a demonstration against the puppet emperor and his French advisers, he left Vietnam in 1911 and stayed away for thirty years. He shipped out as a cook's helper on a French liner, and in Boston he worked in the basement pastry kitchen of the Parker House hotel (no employment records from those years survive). He shoveled snow in London and tinted photographs in Paris. In Paris he joined the French Socialist Party, and reading Lenin's anti-colonial writings turned him into a communist; study in Moscow and organizing work in China followed, including a part in founding the Indochinese Communist Party.

The Viet Minh in the Mountains

By 1940 Germany had seized western Europe and Japan occupied Vietnam, tolerating the collaborationist French administration as long as the colony kept producing. Ho judged the moment right to rally peasants, workers, merchants and soldiers against both occupiers. He crossed back into Vietnam in January 1941 after three decades abroad and set up headquarters in a cave near the village of Pac Bo, naming the mountain for Marx and the stream for Lenin. There, at a party plenum in May 1941, he founded the Vietnam Independence League, the Viet Minh. To build and lead its fighting force he turned to Vo Nguyen Giap.

Giap had taught French history at a lycée in Hanoi to the children of the city's elite, and his hatred of the French sharpened when his wife died in Hoa Lo prison after a three-year sentence, a detail the Encyclopaedia Britannica biography of Vo Nguyen Giap records. He had already begun shaping a method that would run from guerrilla campaigning to a final conventional offensive, and he expected his army, when the fight for independence came, to be everywhere and nowhere.

The OSS and the Declaration of 1945

By the spring of 1945 the United States was hunting for allies behind Japanese lines in Vietnam, and it was contacted by Ho himself. An Office of Strategic Services team dropped into the jungle to meet the Viet Minh leadership: the Deer Team, landed in July 1945. Its medic, Pfc Paul Hoagland, was told before anything else that he must treat the movement's leader, who lay desperately sick in a grass shack; the patient was Ho Chi Minh. The National WWII Museum account of the OSS in Vietnam describes the team training Ho's guerrillas to use bazookas, carbines and grenades, and the birth of the Vietnamese-American Force.

When the atomic bombs ended the war, Ho called on Vietnamese to rise before the Free French could restore the old regime, and they did, in cities and towns across the country. On 2 September 1945, the day Japan formally surrendered, hundreds of thousands filled Ba Dinh Square in Hanoi to hear him proclaim independence, opening with Thomas Jefferson's words, "All men are created equal", with an OSS officer standing nearby. The full text survives in the Vietnamese declaration of independence published by Columbia University. Ho's letters and telegrams to President Truman, arguing that Vietnam and the United States believed the same things, were never answered and are believed never to have reached him; the Truman Library keeps no copy in its holdings.

France Returns and the War Starts

Fresh French troops landed in Saigon in the fall of 1945, took the city within days and set out to reoccupy the country. Ho still hoped to win independence without war and still hoped Washington would intervene on his behalf; his approaches to Truman went nowhere. In June 1946 he traveled to Paris for negotiations that produced nothing, and while he was away Giap consolidated communist control of the revolution, sanctioning the execution of many non-communist nationalists. On 19 December 1946 fighting erupted in Hanoi between the Viet Minh and the French. Outmatched by firepower, Ho, Giap and their comrades slipped out of the city for their mountain stronghold, and Ho went on the radio calling for nationwide guerrilla war by whatever means people had to hand.

The country he hoped to unite was already splitting apart. Duong Van Mai's father served the French as a deputy governor of a province east of Hanoi, and yet her older sister and her husband trekked from the capital to the Viet Minh base to join the resistance. France poured in thousands of regulars, European mercenaries and colonial troops from Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia and Senegal, held the towns and province capitals, and watched the Viet Minh mine roads, blow bridges and ambush patrols before vanishing. Retaliation fell on villages; the Viet Minh, in turn, targeted anyone linked to the French. It was, in the account of one witness, a civil war down to the family level.

Washington Picks Up the Bill

The Cold War changed the arithmetic. Communist forces under Mao Zedong seized China, and in January 1950 Mao formally recognized Ho's insurgency and promised arms, equipment and training; the Soviets recognized the Viet Minh as well. President Truman, blamed for losing China, approved an aid program for the French, and in the summer of 1950, a month after North Korea invaded the south, Washington sent transport planes, a shipload of Jeeps and thirty-five military advisers who arrived on 3 August 1950 to oversee their use. The United States was officially in Vietnam.

Two developments deepened the commitment. In 1951 a young Massachusetts congressman, John F. Kennedy, spent two hours in Saigon with Seymour Topping, a seasoned American correspondent, who told him the French were losing and that Vietnamese who had admired the Americans were beginning to despise them for backing France; Kennedy went home warning that the effort would end in failure unless the United States proved as opposed to injustice as to communism. And in 1952 Dwight Eisenhower was elected president on a pledge to be tougher on communism. By 1954 American taxpayers were covering nearly eighty percent of the French war bill. The logic was public: at a news conference on 7 April 1954 Eisenhower explained what he called the "falling domino" principle, one column falling and the rest following.

Dien Bien Phu and the Partition of Vietnam

By 1953 France had been fighting for seven years and its seventh commander, General Henri Navarre, wanted a decisive battle before talks began. He fortified a remote valley in north-western Vietnam called Dien Bien Phu, where about eleven thousand men dug in on the valley floor, and he was certain French firepower and air support would crush any assault. Vo Nguyen Giap saw the chance the terrain offered, and a corps of civilian porters carried rice and disassembled artillery through the jungle on foot to ring the valley, a documented 261,451 people by one careful count. On 13 March 1954 the bombardment began; the airstrip was destroyed and the garrison could be supplied only by airdrop. The French government pressed President Eisenhower to intervene, and he declined without allied and Congressional support.

On 7 May 1954, after fifty-five days of siege, the garrison surrendered, with roughly eight thousand French soldiers killed, wounded or missing. Diplomats then took up the Indochina question at Geneva, where a war-weary France and a Viet Minh short of supplies settled for a settlement neither side wanted. Under the Geneva Agreements of 20 July 1954, Vietnam was divided at the seventeenth parallel, separated by a demilitarized zone, with between fifty thousand and ninety thousand Viet Minh soldiers regrouping in the north and elections to reunify the country promised within two years. Everyone knew Ho Chi Minh would win them.

Diem's Republic in the South

The United States set about backing a government in the south, and it found one in Ngo Dinh Diem, a Roman Catholic and a Confucian in a largely Buddhist country, a former seminarian who loathed both the French and the Communists. Diem proved aloof and autocratic, but also shrewd, and when his forces beat a French-backed syndicate in the streets of Saigon the French withdrew from the south for good, ending nearly a century of occupation. He called a referendum, and when the ballots were counted he had claimed 98.2 percent of the vote; on 26 October 1955 he proclaimed the Republic of Vietnam and named himself its first president. The reunification election promised at Geneva was dropped, and the American diplomatic record of 1956 shows Diem with no disposition to accept it.

Diem became Washington's ally and, as one American put it, its master, because the need to keep the south out of Communist hands left the United States unable to afford his failure. In the north, meanwhile, Ho rebuilt a country devastated by a decade of war and imposed harsh land reforms modeled on China's, while in the south Diem's security apparatus jailed tens of thousands without trial — by the documentary's own account — and southern Communists began attacking officials in reply.

Le Duan and the National Liberation Front

As violence intensified, new men took charge in Hanoi. Ho remained the face of the revolution, but he began sharing power with hardliners impatient with his caution, above all Le Duan, born around 1907 in Quang Tri Province to a family of blacksmiths and scrap collectors, who had co-founded the Indochinese Communist Party and spent years in French prisons. By 1959 Le Duan and his allies were ascendant, pushing the party to help southerners remove Diem by force; that year's Resolution 15 endorsed the turn, and he became First Secretary informally in 1959 and formally in 1960. Bands of armed men began slipping back into the south along jungle paths through Laos that Americans would soon call the Ho Chi Minh Trail.

Then two events closed the chapter. On 8 November 1960 Kennedy and Johnson narrowly beat Nixon and Lodge for the presidency, and six weeks later, on 20 December 1960, representatives of southern revolutionary groups founded a new organization to replace the Viet Minh, with Le Duan and his comrades orchestrating from Hanoi, as the Encyclopaedia Britannica entry on the National Liberation Front records. It took the name National Liberation Front, its armed wing the People's Liberation Armed Forces, and its enemies in Saigon and Washington called it the Viet Cong, a contraction of Vietnamese Communists. A month later Kennedy took the oath and promised that America would pay any price, bear any burden to assure the survival of liberty.

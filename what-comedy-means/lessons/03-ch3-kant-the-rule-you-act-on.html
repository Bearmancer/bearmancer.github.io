Kant: The Rule You Act On

Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) gave the word "dignity" its modern meaning, and he built it from the same idea comedy quietly relies on: a person is not a thing, so their worth cannot be spent. (SEP overview.)

What makes a thing right

If the reason to act is some result you want — happiness, reward, heaven — the rule is only conditional: "if you want X, do Y." A conditional rule can never say must to someone who wants something else. So Kant argued the moral rule must bind through its form alone:

Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law. (Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, 1785, Academy pagination 4:402)

"Maxim" means the rule you are actually acting on. The test: name it honestly, then ask whether everyone could live by it. The false promise is a rule that eats itself — if everyone used it, promises would mean nothing, so nobody could be tricked by it. The liar is a cheat: he lives off a rule while claiming a private exception to it. Some rules fail instead by being self-frustrating for anyone who universalizes them — never helping, never growing your talents; those failures yield duties with leeway (help sometimes, develop yourself eventually), not one exceptionless "never."

People are not price tags

His second wording: treat every person — yourself included — always as an end, never merely as a tool (4:428). You may buy a taxi's service; you may not use the driver the way you use a hammer, because the arrangement has to be one he could agree to. Things can be replaced for money; what admits no equivalent stands above price — that is dignity (4:434).

Why the motive is the whole story

The shopkeeper who never cheats the inexperienced customer is doing the right thing — for the wrong accounting. Right outcome, no moral worth; mere "legality" (4:397 note). The cheerful helper is likelier, but she may simply enjoy it; enjoyment is a mood, not a principle. The one who helps because it is right, when he feels nothing at all — that act has moral worth (4:398). Worth sits in the principle, not in the pleasure.

The hard clause

In 1797 Kant answered Benjamin Constant directly: even to a murderer at the door asking where your friend hides, you may not lie (On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy, 8:426). Why anyone would sign up for that: the lie claims a private exception from the only kind of rule that can bind everyone, and you never owned the results anyway — you own the act. This is the wall between Kant and "the greatest good for the greatest number."

Freedom, on his definition

Being pushed around by appetite is not choosing, however pleasant the push. Freedom is giving yourself the law you keep even against your wishes — autonomy. Kant's deeper claim: the moral law itself is how we know we are free, since it commands what a merely determined creature could never be obliged to do (his "fact of reason", Critique of Practical Reason, 1788, 5:31).

Keep

  • Rightness = the rule willed universally, not the outcome purchased.
  • Persons are ends; things have prices; dignity has no exchange rate.
  • Worth comes from duty's principle, which is why a kind temperament is praiseworthy but not the moral thing itself.

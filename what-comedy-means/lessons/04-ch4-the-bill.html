The Bill

Two ledgers for Kant: what his ideas bought, and what the critics sent back. Then what comedy did with the same contract — which is where this course started.

What it bought

  • Germany's Basic Law (1949), Article 1: "Human dignity shall be inviolable. To respect and protect it shall be the duty of all state authority." Drafted by lawmakers answering exactly what a state that treated people as expendable had done.
  • The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) opens on "the inherent dignity ... of all members of the human family" — rights that the majority may not trade away.
  • Nuremberg: the Tribunal's Charter, Article 8 (1945) refused "I was only following orders" as a defense — a person is an agent, never a tool of another's will.
  • Philosophy's version: Dworkin's rights-as-trumps (individual claims beat utility sums), and Rawls describing his original position as an interpretation of Kant's kingdom of ends — people legislating together the rules any of them could accept.

What came back

  • Hegel (Philosophy of Right, addition to §134): the universal test is empty — almost any rule can be dressed to pass it; real duties have to be borrowed from a community's actual life.
  • Schiller's jest (letter to Körner, 1793): serving virtue with hatred of it, and counting that as moral worth, makes the duty-bound servant a wretched knave, not a saint. The cold-heartedness objection has run ever since.
  • Bernard Williams (1973): a man who saves his wife because the moral law permits it has "one thought too many."

What comedy did with the contract

The comic deal — nobody pays, so laugh — slowly moved out of the plot and into the format:

  • Rom-com: the couple must end together. Dante's arc survives as a genre clause; the jokes are optional.
  • Sitcom: chaos by the midpoint, order restored by runtime, nobody permanently hurt; the reset is a happy ending signed in advance.
  • Waiting for Godot (Beckett, premiered January 1953) carries the subtitle "tragicomedy in two acts": nothing resolves, yet nothing pays — the bracket is held by tone alone, the stylized waiting, the clowning. Here the old word finally split in two.
  • Dark comedy never states the contract aloud; only style signs it. That is why it is perpetually misread as cruelty, and why arguments about "can we laugh at this?" are arguments about whether the frame is real.

One sentence, twice

Comedy is the frame that declares nobody pays. Kant's dignity is the part of a person that can never be put inside any frame — not as a butt of a joke's expense, not as a cost in a policy, not as an exception to a rule.

Dante and Orff called poems about Hell and the end of the world "comedies" because each promises that frame around everything in the end: the suffering is real, and it is not the last word. The laughter of the modern kind is the same promise heard at close range — you are permitted to laugh precisely because, somewhere, someone has guaranteed nobody pays.

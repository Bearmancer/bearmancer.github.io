The Shape Before the Joke

The word comedy did not start out meaning "funny". In Greek, then medieval, usage it named a shape: a story that goes from trouble to good fortune. A title like The Divine Comedy is not a joke. It is a spoiler: this ends well.

Aristotle set the shape

In the Poetics (around 335 BC) Aristotle sorted stories by which way fortune moves. Tragedy: a prosperous person turns toward ruin. Comedy: ordinary, somewhat ridiculous people land safely. His definition of the laughable itself:

The comic is ... a mistake or ugliness that is not painful and not destructive. (Poetics 1449a)

The word too: kōmos was the rowdy song of a festival procession; kōmaidia means "revel-song". Aristophanes' plays are the full package: rude jokes on stage, feasts by the end. From the start, two halves were welded together — the laughter, and the promise that nobody really pays.

The medieval dictionary dropped the jokes

By late antiquity the definition was pure shape. Isidore of Seville, Etymologies (around AD 615, book I chapter 37): comedy is the kind of play whose beginning is troubled and whose ending is calm; tragedy is the reverse. Nothing about laughing appears in it at all.

Dante titles accordingly

Dante's poem begins with a lost man in a dark wood, descends through Hell, and ends with the sight of God. He called it Comoedia, and his letter to Cangrande (around 1316) says why: a comedy "starts with things harsh and terrible, but in every other respect ends in everything desirable and perfect," and it is written in the everyday style of the street rather than the high style of epic. The word "Divine" was Boccaccio's addition after the poet died (Divine Comedy). So the title claims two old facts — humble manner, happy ending — and says nothing about jokes, which the poem mostly spends on the damned.

Orff claims it for time itself

Carl Orff (1895–1982) put the same old label on the largest subject available: De temporum fine comoedia — "a comedy at the end of time". His last large work, roughly ten years in the making, premiered in Salzburg in 1973 and was revised in 1977 and 1981; it is sung in Greek, Latin and German, and the Orff-Stiftung describes it as a play of the end of time in which ancient and Christian worldviews merge. Seven judges, a Sibyl, a fallen Lucifer pleading; the Narrator asks that his sins be not forgotten but forgiven (classical-music.com); the work ends on light. Time itself runs Dante's arc: the worst possible opening, a guaranteed good ending. That is the most literal possible "comedy" in the old sense.

Keep

  • Old "comedy": begins badly, ends well, humble style. "Tragedy": the reverse.
  • Dante and Orff both use that word on purpose, despite zero laughs in the titles they carry.
  • The laughter belongs in the same package as the safe landing — the next page is about why.

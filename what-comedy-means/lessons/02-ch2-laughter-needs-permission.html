Laughter Needs Permission

Why did the "ends well" word come to mean "makes you laugh"? Because both are one deal seen from two ends: you can laugh at misfortune only while nobody has to pay for it. The happy ending was the receipt.

The scene, slowly

A man steps on a banana peel and lands in the mud. Everyone laughs. In play:

  • Three parties — whoever showed you the scene, whoever laughs, whoever fell.
  • Two broken norms — inside the scene, a body failed at its job; outside it, you were invited to enjoy that instead of helping.
  • One suspension — pity, for the length of the laugh.
  • One condition — nobody pays. He is clean by the next cut. That is why mud flies in cartoons and not in crime reports.

Revoke it and watch what happens: learn the actor really broke his neck, and the same footage turns sickening mid-laugh. The frame is a permission slip that truth can cancel at any second.

Three versions of the same theory

Bergson, Laughter (1900): "for the comic to produce its full effect ... it requires, as in a dream, something of a momentary anaesthesia of the heart." You agree not to feel — that agreement, not the fall, is what makes the fall funny. His other line: the comic is "things mechanical encrusted upon the living" — the mud-man has gone rigid, automatic, briefly a thing.

Freud, Jokes and Their Relation to the Unconscious (1905): an aggressive or indecent joke works because a third party's permission lets it past a normal inhibition; the laugh is the energy you no longer need for holding the door shut.

McGraw & Warren, "Benign Violations" (Psychological Science, 2010): laughter = something wrong + nothing at stake, at the same time. Their experiments move the lever: the same accident clip reads as horrifying at a minute's distance and funny at a year's.

Two proofs you can feel at home

  • You cannot tickle yourself. Same finger, same ribs — the brain predicts its own touch and cancels the surprise (Blakemore, Wolpert & Frith, NeuroReport, 2000). The identical poke from someone else works, because there the contact is a surprise under contract.
  • Slapstick must recover. Wile E. Coyote flattens and pops back every cartoon; the recovery clause licenses the hundredth cliff. One real broken ankle and the show is over — not because it stops being funny-looking, but because the contract is void.

The oldest punchline about this

Marx, quoting Hegel in The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte (1852): world-historic facts appear twice — "the first time as tragedy, the second time as farce." A doomed order repeating itself is the same trick in public scale: repetition is the bracket that makes the old suffering safe to mock.

Keep

  • The comic frame = consequences suspended, everyone in on it, revocable at any moment.
  • The old happy ending was that same frame drawn around a whole story; modern "funny" is the feeling of the frame itself.
  • What the mud-man never loses is his worth. Posture got knocked down; the person did not. Making that precise is Kant's job — next page.

Kant Against the Calculus

The utilitarian rule: an act is right exactly as far as it tends to produce pleasure and prevent pain, "everybody to count for one, nobody for more than one" (Bentham, Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, 1789); Mill's version: actions right as they "promote happiness," wrong as the reverse (Utilitarianism, 1861, ch. II; SEP history).

Kant's attack

  • The sum has no face. Welfare must belong to someone; an aggregate is an abstraction. Sacrificing one healthy traveler to harvest his organs for five patients may maximize the sum and still treats a person as a container. For Kant persons are ends (4:428); the calculus makes them fuel.
  • Rights are trumps, not the usual winners. Dworkin's formula (1984): individual claims beat majority calculations — which is unintelligible to pure utilitarianism, for whom a "right" is only the rule that tends to work best. When the working best says break the rule, the right dissolves.
  • Rule-worship, their word for his view. Mill himself anticipated the charge: if an act clearly serves the general happiness, "it is admissible" — and sticking to the rule anyway is, on his account, superstition (Utilitarianism, ch. II). The moral point of a rule is only its consequences.

The calculus strikes back

  • The catastrophe clause. The murderer at the door: Kant says no lie; the utilitarian says the lie obviously pays, and calling truthfulness worth a murdered friend is rule-worship at its most expensive. Few readers have followed Kant off this ledge — he did, on purpose, because the rule's cost is not what makes rules (8:426).
  • His test smells like their rule-book. A fair half-victory: universalizing a rule and asking whether everyone acting on it goes well are near-relations; rule-utilitarianism claims the whole test for itself once results enter. Kant's defense: the contradiction in the false promise is logical, not statistical — you are cheating the very institution you lean on — so the test needs no calculus at all.

Where Kant wins by their own scoreboard

  • Self-sacrifice. Utilitarianism demands I keep giving until more giving would cost me something of comparable moral weight (Singer's pond: if you can prevent bad things without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, you ought — Famine, Affluence, and Morality, 1972). Kant's wide duties carry latitude (4:424): help others, yes; but never a demand to be nothing for yourself. Williams made the same complaint the utilitarian tradition's own: a doctrine that makes you neutral about your own projects attacks your integrity ("Jim and the Indians", 1973).

Scoreboard: utilitarianism explains why results matter and cannot ground the untouchable person; Kant grounds dignity and pays with the murderer at the door. Both bills are still open.

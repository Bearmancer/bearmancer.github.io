One Question, Four Answers

Every moral argument you have ever had was about one of four things: how the act turns out, whether your rule could apply to everyone, what kind of person does it, and what an onlooker feels about it. Ethics is the attempt to say which of those decides right and wrong — and the four answers have been fighting for two thousand years.

Two demands make an answer count as moral at all. It must say why the right thing binds — why you cannot simply opt out if you want something else — and it must say why some acts are not for sale, even when the price is right. Every one of the four answers buys one of those demands by spending the other, and the whole history of the subject is the receipts:

  • Outcomes bind because everyone wants results — but a result can always justify a horror if the math pays.
  • Kant's rules bind and never are for sale — but are they too empty, or too cold, to live with?
  • Character needs no justification — but it explains nothing to the person outside the circle who disagrees about which traits are excellent.
  • Sentiment explains why morality actually moves people — but feelings disagree, and no feeling says must to the man who lacks it.

Five more lessons: Kant's own system set out at full price, then him face to face with each rival — the calculus, the character ethic, the sentimentalists — and finally the bill his ideas ran up for law and philosophy.

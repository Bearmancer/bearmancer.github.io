Kant Against the Sentimentalists

Hume's challenge is aimed at the root of Kant's tree: morality comes from feeling, and reason only serves. "Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other office than to serve and obey them" — A Treatise of Human Nature (1739–40, II.iii.3; SEP overview). In the same section he catches the oldest sleight of hand in ethics — no "ought" ever arrives from a chain of "is" statements. Smith added the machinery: we approve acts that a sympathetic, impartial onlooker could feel with (The Theory of Moral Sentiments, 1759).

What they see in Kant's machine

  • Why care about consistency? The universalization test punishes a contradiction in willing. But a man who calmly wants a world of cheats has contradicted nothing in his logic — only in his wishes; and wishing is exactly what sentiment, not reason, is for. Hume's position says Kant's test borrows its teeth from feelings it claims to derive.
  • Moral language reports a feeling. When you call cruelty "wrong," you name a strong, human response, not a feature of the act: "morality ... more properly felt than thought of" (Treatise 3.1.2). Reason discovers facts; no fact moves anyone by itself.

Kant's countermove

  • Respect is a feeling with the wrong cause. He grants that morality involves a feeling — but it is "an effect of the law," produced in me by recognizing a demand I cannot trade away, not its source (second Critique 5:73, 5:81). Only a being able to feel that can feel moral respect; animals and calculating selfishness cannot.
  • The order of the proof runs his way. Hume assumes a rational agent with fixed wants, and asks what reason can do for them; Kant starts from the undeniable datum that you find yourself addressed by obligation — the "fact of reason" (5:31) — and infers from the ought a genuine can (5:30 note). Pure reason can be practical, he says; that is precisely what "reason is only a slave" was invented to deny, and a theory cannot decide its own rival's first premise by fiat.
  • Freedom is on the table. In the phenomenal world every choice has a prior cause — Hume is right — but the moral "you" belongs to the intelligible side where a self-given law is possible; his whole ethics lives on the two-standpoints solution (Groundwork 4:450–459; Critique of Practical Reason 5:4–6), which Hume never engaged.

The modern score

The argument never closed. Williams' "Internal and External Reasons" (1979) replayed Hume's move — reason only rearranges what you already care about — and moral-psychology research on intuition-first judgment (Haidt's social-intuitionist program from 2001) reported ordinary people behaving exactly as the sentimentalists predict. Kantians answer at the level Hume set: psychology explains how we make moral judgments; the question was never whether the "ought" binds — and every critic who says cruelty is genuinely wrong, even for the man with no sentiment about it, is arguing on Kant's ground.

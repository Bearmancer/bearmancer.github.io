Kant Against the Character Ethic

Aristotle's ethics is not a rulebook. The question is not "what should I do?" but "what is a life spent well?" — the human ergon, our activity done excellently, is happiness (eudaimonia), and the excellent state of character that produces it is virtue, trained by habit, steered by phronesis — practical perception of the case (Nicomachean Ethics, I.7, II, VI).

Why Aristotle looks past Kant

  • Rules are learned last, not first. A child becomes just by just acts under guidance long before any test of universalization; the rulebook describes the mature person, it does not make one.
  • The good person also feels right. Temperance for Aristotle includes liking the right things; Kant's worth lives in duty against inclination (4:398), so his saint — helping while hating it — reads as a wounded man, not a virtuous one. Schiller's 1793 jest made the point permanently: obeying virtue from duty alone, and being proud of the coldness, is not a saint but a wretched knave.
  • Phronesis does the work no formula can. Aristotle's brave act has no rule for every case; ethics is more like navigation than arithmetic.

What Kant answers (and what he has himself)

  • Character is unstable fuel. A kindly temperament is luck — the cheerful helper's mood can drown by Tuesday; it needs a governing maxim to count as moral at all (4:398). And an excellence must be praise-worthy in oneself: courage for a tyrant is courage serving evil (4:392).
  • Happiness is too vague to be the law. The word names different things to different people, so a system built on it can never say no firmly (4:418) — which is exactly what makes Aristotle need the expert with perception, and his critics ask: perception of whom? who trains the trainers?
  • Kant's own virtue theory. He is not anti-virtue: in the Metaphysics of Morals (1797, Academy vol. 6) virtue is the moral strength of a human being's maxim, and he lists feelings (sympathy, goodwill) as the natural aids duty should cultivate — but always as servants, never as the master.

The modern scoreboard

  • Anscombe reopened the whole fight ("Modern Moral Philosophy", 1958): both Kantian duty-talk and utilitarian maximizing inherited the legal picture of ethics — obligation by lawgiver — after the lawgiver left the building; she called the "moral ought" a superstition worth retiring, and started the modern revival of Aristotle (MacIntyre's After Virtue followed in 1981).
  • Hegel's version of the same charge: Kant's pure test is empty — it cannot generate any concrete duty without smuggling content in — and real morality lives in the institutions of an actual community (Philosophy of Right, addition to §134).

Standing result: Aristotle explains how moral ability is built and when to make exceptions; Kant is what stops the community's training from authorizing cruelty with a good feeling about it. Most modern ethicists quietly keep both debts.

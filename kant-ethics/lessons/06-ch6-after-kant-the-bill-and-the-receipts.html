After Kant: The Bill and the Receipts

Whatever the referee says, Kant's ethics paid out in the century after him, and then received its long list of objections. Both sides belong to any honest picture of ethics today.

What the bill includes

  • Law. The dignity clause at the top of postwar constitutions: German Basic Law, Article 1 (1949) — "Human dignity shall be inviolable"; the UDHR preamble (1948) names "the inherent dignity ... of all members of the human family" as the ground of freedom, justice and peace; the Tribunal's Charter (1945), Article 8, refused superior orders — a person is an agent who answers, never an instrument. (the Code of the 1947 Doctors' Trial then put consent first: a person may not be used without permission they give themselves.)
  • Medicine. The Belmont Report (1979) leads with respect for persons — autonomous choice, and protection where choice is absent; Beauchamp and Childress made autonomy a pillar of modern bioethics (principlism, 1979).
  • Political philosophy. Rawls read his original position as "an interpretation of" Kant's kingdom of ends; Scanlon's contract ("what no one could reasonably reject", 1998) is the universalization test given a second life; Korsgaard made self-constitution through practical reason the whole of normativity (1996).

The receipts filed against him

  • Hegel: a pure test generates no duties by itself; it feeds on the customs it claims to sit above.
  • Schiller (1793) and every coldness joke since: worth located against feeling enshrines the grudging servant.
  • Butler (1877): the motive test either demands hypocrisy or makes duty an obsession no good person would have.
  • Gilligan (1982) and the ethics of care: a morality of impersonal rules and contracts misses the first moral facts — dependence, need, and relations that bind without consent (overview; In a Different Voice).
  • Williams (1973): a person must not be required to be neutral about his own life; and the man with "one thought too many" before saving his wife has mistaken the point of friendship — and, maybe, of ethics.

Why the case never closes

Both ledgers stay open because the four grounds answer different questions: results matter, rules bind, character explains, sentiment moves. Kant's permanent contribution is the question he made unavoidable — can you will your rule as one for everyone, addressed to people who could answer back? Every critic who calls exploitation wrong even when it works, and every defender who cannot say why the person is more than a resource, is still arguing inside the building. The other three traditions keep filing windows; nobody has torn the structure down.

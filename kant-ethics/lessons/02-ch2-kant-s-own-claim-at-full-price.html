Kant's Own Claim, at Full Price

Why rules, and not outcomes or feelings? Kant's starting knife: every rule that gets its force from a wanted result is conditional — "if you want X, do Y." A conditional rule cannot say must to anyone who shrugs at X. If morality really binds everyone, its law must bind through its form alone: the rule itself, willed by you, not the prize attached to it. Full statement: SEP overview.

The parts

  • Good will (Groundwork 1785, Ak. 4:392–393). Everything else — intelligence, courage, wealth — can serve evil; a good will shines "like a jewel," good in itself, not for what it achieves.
  • The maxim. The rule you are actually acting on. Test: can everyone live by it at once? The false promise fails by eating itself (if universal, promises mean nothing, so nobody is fooled — no cheating device exists). Failing this way marks a strict duty — never. Other rules fail more softly: a world of no-help-anytime is conceivable but one you cannot consistently will as its resident — those are wide duties, with built-in leeway (4:421–424).
  • The humanity clause (4:428). A person is a being who sets ends by reasons; using them as a mere tool treats a person as a thing. Things get a market price or a "fancy price"; what admits no equivalent is dignity (4:434). The associated ideal: a kingdom of ends — everyone legislating the rules anyone could accept, each treated as end never mere means (4:433).
  • Autonomy versus being pushed (4:440–449). Every rival ground — happiness, sympathy, God's command, social approval — is, for Kant, heteronomy: the will steered by something outside itself. Freedom is exactly the capacity to be bound by the law you yourself legislate.
  • Motive, not mood (4:397 note, 4:398). The honest shopkeeper and the cheerful helper may be delightful and carry no moral worth: their lightness could vanish with a bad day. Worth sits in the governing principle; the cold benefactor who helps from duty alone outranks the warm one.

The costs he paid on record

  • The Groundwork's own limit (4:458–463). The book ends admitting it cannot prove that we are free in the way its ethics needs; the test was on trial here and lost.
  • The 1788 reversal. In the second Critique the order flips: consciousness of the moral law is itself the given — a "fact of reason" (5:31) — and through it we know we are free; ought precedes can and proves the can.
  • The postulates (5:122–130). Since the law demands a completeness this life cannot reach, practical reason "postulates" immortality and God — not proofs, but conditions of trusting the demand. A moral argument for faith, which neither churches nor atheists ever fully forgave him.
  • No exceptions even in the worst case. The 1797 essay on lying to the murderer at the door (Ak. 8:426): the lie claims a private exception and dissolves the only kind of trust that can bind a whole society.

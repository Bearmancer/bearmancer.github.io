What Could Riyadh Achieve?

The video closes with a counterfactual. If Riyadh ever chose to convert wealth into power, what could it achieve? Scholarship on Gulf militaries treats the weakness as a regime choice rather than an accident.

"A strange amalgamation of international hardware intentionally hamstrung by the very regime that seeks to use it"

Weakness by Design

Scholarship on Gulf militaries reads the weakness as deliberate. Gulf rulers accepted forces that were more hapless in exchange for coup resilience, per the rentier-militaries study.

Coup-proofing research says the same. Parallel militaries trade usable power for regime survival, per the RAND study and its Belfer companion.

The force split has a plain reason: regime security. A Washington assessment agrees the duplicate ground forces lack a combat rationale, per the CSIS study. The rationale is political, not military.

Where Saudi Arabia Stands

Saudi Arabia ranks fifth in the Middle East and 25th worldwide on the published ranking. The region's top spender lands mid-table. That gap is the paradox in one number.

The ranking's scoring method is opaque and widely criticized.

The India comparison sharpens the point. India spent 86.1 billion dollars against Saudi 80.3 billion in 2024, per the SIPRI press release. India directs most capital outlays to domestic makers. Riyadh still imports the bulk of its kit.

Defense ties between the two run through a ministerial committee, the SADA TANSEEQ army exercise and the Al-Mohed Al-Hindi naval rounds. No mutual-defense pact exists. These links give context for the regional balance.

Three Futures the Video Floats

First, a Saudi mercenary enterprise modeled on the Wagner group. The past practice behind the idea comes from an investigation: thousands of Sudanese fighters, including children, were recruited for Yemen, per reporting on the investigation. The proposal itself remains the video's speculation.

Second, Riyadh as a build-shop for European designs lacking factory capacity. Third, a pivot into military-adjacent high technology: artificial intelligence, robotics, cyber tools and secure communications. These are the video's own projections.

What Money Can Convert Into

Counterfactual capacity in Riyadh runs through three channels. Money converts fastest into capital-heavy imports: aircraft, ships and air defense. It converts slowest into mass infantry, which needs willing recruits and trusted commanders.

Spending scale underwrites every path. Saudi outlays reached 83.2 billion dollars in 2025, eighth worldwide, per the global expenditure factsheet. Few states can match that purchasing power.

The regime-security tax sits between the money and the outcome. Parallel forces, loyalty-first promotion and foreign-operator dependence each subtract combat power. Scholarship treats this subtraction as the intended price of coup-proofing.

Dependence shapes the ceiling. America supplies roughly three quarters of Saudi arms imports, per the transfers factsheet. Any Saudi force design must stay compatible with that pipeline or pay a steep switching cost.

Regional integration is the other ceiling. Gulf missile defense remains un-integrated because member states withhold sensitive data from each other, per the Georgetown assessment. Saudi capacity grows inside a fragmented neighborhood.

The highest-leverage path is therefore narrow and technical. Capital-intensive systems plus niche technologies, built with imported know-how, maximize power without threatening the palace. That is exactly the future the video sketches.

A Choice, Not an Accident

The closing line is polemic.

"Riyadh is not the defense leader that one might expect, but that's not an accident. It's a choice"

The scholarship in this chapter points the same way. Big money plus deliberate fragmentation equals mid-tier outcomes. The paradox resolves into a strategy.

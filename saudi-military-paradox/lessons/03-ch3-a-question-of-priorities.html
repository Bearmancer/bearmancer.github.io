A Question of Priorities

Saudi weakness looks like failure until the priorities behind it show. The undersized force, the imported arsenal, and the atrophied arms industry are the product of deliberate choice: scholarship on Gulf coup-proofing finds rulers accepted weaker forces in exchange for regime resilience, and the kingdom chose regime safety and oil-backed influence over a military that could fight.

An Imported Arsenal

The video calls the Saudi arsenal peculiar and entirely imported. Tanks, fighters, and warships all come from abroad, but the Al Fahd armored vehicle has been built in the kingdom since 1998, alongside later domestic designs.

The video then reaches for a comparison: no country this import-dependent spends this much until the Netherlands. The ranks hold but the equivalence does not: the SIPRI tables place Saudi Arabia near the top and the Netherlands far lower.

"Only around two percent of our military procurement is domestic."

That late-2010s figure is the kingdom's own stated baseline in the 2017 Public Investment Fund release on localizing military industries.

Localizing Military Industry

The Vision 2030 answer to that baseline is a localization target of over half of spending by 2030, stated on the official page of the General Authority for Military Industries.

Progress: about nineteen percent localized by the end of 2023 per the United States trade guide for the Saudi defense market, rising to nearly twenty-five percent by the end of 2024 per the authority's own 2024 report.

The facility count behind those percentages grew from a handful of licensed firms to 311 establishments in the military-industries sector by the end of 2024.

Vision 2030 treats localization as an economic machine: oil revenue funds a regulator, the regulator licenses domestic firms, and licensed firms absorb foreign technology through joint ventures.

The regulator is the General Authority for Military Industries, and the vehicle is the Saudi Arabian Military Industries company founded in 2017. Complex work arrives through partnerships, such as the combat-management system built with a Spanish shipbuilder and drone co-production accords with Turkish firms.

The mechanism has a ceiling built in. Suppliers transfer assembly and maintenance readily but guard their most sensitive designs, which is why licensed establishments can triple while the localization share climbs only gradually.

Dependence as Reassurance

Import dependence also doubles as reassurance. Foreign suppliers hold decades of spares and software leverage over the kit they sold, so oil buyers sleep better — a dynamic the coup-proofing literature recognizes as part of the regime-safety bargain.

Wasteful Spending

"Far too many major Saudi security decisions have not been part of any coherent force improvement plan, with limited regard to cost-effectiveness."

That 2018 assessment comes verbatim from Anthony Cordesman's study of the other side of Saudi security, and it states the wasteful-spending case.

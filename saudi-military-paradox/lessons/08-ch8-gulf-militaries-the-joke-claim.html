Gulf Militaries: The Joke Claim

The WarFronts video argues the Gulf state militaries are "a joke—for now." This chapter teaches the picture underneath that claim: what Gulf forces actually field, what happened when Iran tested them in the spring of 2026, and where the video's telling runs ahead of the record.

When Iran Hit the Gulf

Iran struck the United Arab Emirates harder than any other Gulf state: an estimated 550 ballistic missiles and 2,200 drone strikes, according to the Emirati defence ministry, up to the 8 April ceasefire, per the RUSI commentary.

Oman was hit at the ports of Duqm and Salalah, facilities used by the United States, per the summary of the Iranian strikes and Bloomberg's reporting on the Salalah fuel tanks. Two drones fell in the vicinity of Dubai International Airport.

Saudi Arabia's East-West Pipeline was hit too: April 2026 strikes damaged one pumping station on the line and cut its throughput by about 700,000 barrels a day, per the attack record and CNBC's 9 April reporting, while the same round of strikes on the Manifa and Khurais production facilities cut the kingdom's oil output by 600,000 barrels a day, per the same reporting. Riyadh said full capacity was back by 12 April. A separate September attack on the line came from Iraq-based militias rather than Iran directly, per Al Jazeera.

Qatar's Ras Laffan complex, which processes and exports 20 percent of global LNG supply, per Reuters, lost 17 percent of the country's LNG export capacity — two of fourteen trains damaged — per Reuters' 19 March reporting.

American Bases and Emptying Magazines

The damage to American bases was real but smaller than "devastated" suggests: Iranian strikes damaged and destroyed hundreds of buildings and structures at United States bases in eight countries, per the Christian Science Monitor's account of the Pentagon Inspector General report.

Interceptor stocks ran down hard. Patriot stocks fell from 2,330 at the start of the conflict to fewer than 850 by the end of July, and THAAD stocks from 452 to about 250, per The National's reporting, citing CSIS figures.

The Gulf Strikes Back — Quietly

Saudi Arabia carried out covert attacks on Iranian soil — the first time the kingdom is known to have acted militarily directly against Iran — per Reuters' 12 May reporting. The Emirates ran their own secret attacks on Iran, per a Wall Street Journal report relayed by Reuters. Saudi jets also bombed targets linked to Tehran-backed Shi'ite militias in Iraq, per Reuters' 13 May reporting. All three accounts rest on anonymous sources; neither Gulf state has acknowledged the strikes.

New Partners: Kyiv, Jerusalem, Delhi, Islamabad

Saudi Arabia, the Emirates, and Qatar each reached 10-year defence agreements with Ukraine, in President Zelensky's words — "We have already reached 10-year agreements with three key countries: Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, and Qatar" — per the Ukrainian presidency and the Kyiv Post. The Emirati deal was still pending on 28 March, per Reuters. The video adds a detail about hundreds of Ukrainian experts on Gulf soil for which no source turned up; read it as the video's own telling.

The Emirates accepted direct Israeli military support: Israel provided an Iron Dome air-defence system, and an Israeli-operated battery reportedly intercepted an Iranian missile over the Emirates on 4 May, per the RUSI commentary. Washington said Israel deployed Iron Dome batteries and technicians to the Emirates, per Middle East Eye.

On India, the video's phrasing mislabels the news. India and the Emirates already held a Comprehensive Strategic Partnership before 2026; the January visit deepened it, per the visit reporting. What was new was defence: a Framework for the Strategic Defence Partnership agreed on 15 May 2026 that will strengthen defence-industrial cooperation, per the deal reporting.

Saudi Arabia took in 8,000 Pakistani troops, a squadron of fighter jets, and an air-defence system, per Reuters' 18 May reporting and Middle East Eye. Pakistani sources put the confidential pact's ceiling at up to 80,000 troops — a cap in the pact text, not a deployment.

Launchers, Interceptors, and Their Prices

The Saudi army fields four rocket-launcher systems: 180 American M270s, 34 Russian TOS-1As, 36 South Korean K239 Chunmoos, and 77 Brazilian Astros IIs, per the Saudi Army equipment list. The TOS-1A is a thermobaric rocket launcher, so calling it a multiple-launch rocket system stretches the label.

A PAC-3 interceptor costs an estimated $4 million against a typical $35,000 price tag for an Iranian Shahed drone, per the Military Times' 20 July reporting — the upper end of the low five figures.

Hormuz and Oil Politics

When the war opened, large numbers of ships stayed put: about 20,000 mariners and 2,000 ships were stranded in the Persian Gulf, per the Hormuz crisis record, with roughly 500 still moored in July, per UN News. Iran closed the strait as much through insurers as through force: war-risk premiums rose and cover was withdrawn from 5 March. No ship-level share for the "vast majority" phrasing turned up, so keep it as direction rather than measure.

The Emirates quit OPEC — "The United Arab Emirates on Tuesday said it was quitting OPEC," per Reuters' 28 April reporting — effective 1 May, per the US Energy Information Administration.

Tanks on Order and a Non-Aggression Pact

Qatar agreed to buy up to 100 Turkish Altay main battle tanks, per Army Recognition's 2019 reporting. The Altay is built on South Korean K2 technology, but no direct Korean tank order for Qatar turned up — only K2 exercises on Qatari soil, per Army Recognition's 2024 reporting — so read the "South Korea and Turkey" phrasing as one Turkish order with Korean roots.

In mid-May the Financial Times reported that Saudi Arabia had floated a Helsinki-style Middle Eastern non-aggression pact including Iran — "Saudi Arabia floats Middle Eastern non-aggression pact with Iran" — per the Financial Times, dated 14 May in Iran International's relay. The follow-on details are reported too: the Middle East Eye's account has European countries and EU institutions supporting the proposal and urging Gulf states to back it, with the Emirates taking the hawkish position against Iran.

What the Record Leaves Open

Some of the video's figures have no citable counterpart. No source gave a combined count of nine frigates and 31 corvettes across all six Gulf navies; Saudi-only figures conflict by source and year — one 2020 overview gives 11 frigates and four corvettes — so the total can be neither tied down nor set aside.

Population shares come out roughly as the video states where numbers exist: Emiratis at 1.31 million, 11.5 percent of 11.3 to 11.5 million in 2025 estimates, against the video's 1.4 million or about 12 percent; Kuwaitis at 32.09 percent in 2025 against about 30 percent; Bahrainis at 46.6 percent. Figures under 60 percent for Saudi Arabia and Oman did not turn up.

Left unexamined here: Saudi ballistic-missile stockpiles at four or five bases, satellite images of dozens of Patriot launchers before the war, and the claim that Iran's navy was largely destroyed. The "joke" thesis itself, and the video's account of why Gulf militaries were built weak, are interpretation rather than checkable fact; what can be checked fills the rest of this chapter and the next.

Answering the Joke

So does the record sustain the joke? Only as exaggeration. Gulf forces absorbed an estimated 550 missiles and 2,200 drones over the Emirates, per the RUSI commentary, struck back covertly on Iranian soil for the first time, per Reuters' 12 May reporting, and collected new partners from Kyiv to Islamabad. What survives is the hedge — "for now." Patriot stocks fell from 2,330 to fewer than 850 between the start of the conflict and the end of July, per The National's figures; part of the shield is foreign-crewed, with Israeli batteries and technicians deployed to the Emirates, per Middle East Eye; and the arsenals stay a patchwork of four launcher families and mixed fleets, per the Saudi Army equipment list. The punchline names a direction, not a present fact.

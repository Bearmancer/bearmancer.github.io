The Identity Tangle: Tribe, Creed and Command

Every Saudi command post sits where three loyalties meet: tribe, creed and dynasty. The course so far has followed money, hardware and reform. This chapter looks at what the kingdom's military is made of, because the mixture is both the glue of the system and the ceiling on its fighting power.

The Guard and the Army

The National Guard's ancestry runs through the Ikhwan, the brotherhood of settled Bedouin that Ibn Saud formed in 1912 and taught Wahhabi precepts. By 1918 they were his elite army. Then they grew uncontrollable, revolted, and were crushed at the Battle of Sibilla in 1929, per Britannica's account. The loyal survivors were incorporated into the National Guard, making the modern force the institutional heir of the religious tribal army.

The Guard as it exists today was founded in 1954 to counterbalance the regular army once that army had professionalised, and its ranks are drawn from the most loyal tribal clans of central Najd, per a NATO Defense College Foundation study.

American records treat the duplication as deliberate. A 1976 National Security Council paper in the Foreign Relations of the United States series describes the army and the National Guard as intentionally maintained autonomous and counterbalancing forces, notes that the Guard answers to the third-ranking member of the royal family rather than the defence ministry, and judges the army incapable of successful combat against a modern force, blaming a dearth of trained manpower and poor leadership.

Recruitment splits along the same line. The regular forces use foreign contract soldiers in the enlisted ranks, while the National Guard is restricted to citizens, per the Carnegie study of Gulf contract soldiers.

The Tribal Balance

The officer corps pays for the balance. A study in Middle Eastern Studies argues that the emergence of a professional officer corps was sacrificed to sustain the tribal and family ascendancy of the al-Saud, with recruitment kept voluntary rather than conscript.

The fear runs the other way too. Saudi rulers avoid putting large numbers of soldiers under arms because they fear a coup, per Carnegie commentary on defence reform. The tribally based National Guard exists partly for that reason.

In November 2017 the arrangement was shaken when Prince Miteb bin Abdullah was removed by royal decree, ending five decades of his branch's control over the Guard, per Reuters.

Creed in the Ranks

Religion has been armed, and then watched, from the beginning. The Ikhwan precedent showed what happens when zeal outruns the palace. In 1979 the leader of the Grand Mosque seizure, Juhayman al-Otaybi, turned out to be a former National Guard soldier whose training helped him organise the takeover, per the BBC's reconstruction.

After that, faith in uniform became a security file. A US Army War College study records reports that National Guard officers and personnel were arrested for ties to extremists. The same study notes the 1990-91 fatwa in which the senior ulema legalised inviting non-Muslim soldiers to defend the kingdom.

The messaging still runs through religion. In September 2026 the Grand Mufti issued a rare direct address to front-line troops, framing obedience to rulers and commanders as a religious duty, per the statement carried by Arab News.

Foreign Hands

Outside manpower has always been part of the machine. A permanent US training mission has advised the Saudi armed forces since the 1950s and remains in place, per US Central Command. The National Guard modernisation programme extended in 2013 was backed by roughly 250 US government personnel and 650 representatives in country, at an estimated 4.0 billion dollars, per the Defense Security Cooperation Agency notification.

Pakistan is the other long thread. Nearly 15,000 Pakistani troops were posted in Saudi Arabia throughout the 1980s, and 1,379 were still deployed in 2018, per an Institute for National Security Studies paper.

The Yemen Stress Test

The war next door tests all of it. Cash underwrites participation: when Riyadh restored state allowances in April 2017, it added a two-month salary bonus for forces fighting in Yemen, per Reuters. Casualties reached the public by 2018, when state media put the toll above 1,000, per Al Jazeera.

The coalition has also turned on itself. Saudi-backed forces have clashed with the UAE-backed Southern Transitional Council inside the same nominal camp, per France 24. Riyadh now budgets at least 3 billion dollars for Yemen, taking over payrolls previously paid by Abu Dhabi, per Reuters reporting.

Across the lines, the other side mobilises differently. Houthi fighter numbers rose from roughly 220,000 in 2022 to 350,000 in 2024, per ACLED's study of Houthi militarisation.

The Tangle

Put together, the strands explain a force that is built to survive its own society rather than to maximise power abroad. Tribal recruitment buys loyalty and costs competence. Religious legitimacy buys obedience and carries blowback. Foreign hands fill the gaps and deepen the dependence. The identity tangle is not a flaw in the Saudi military. It is the design.

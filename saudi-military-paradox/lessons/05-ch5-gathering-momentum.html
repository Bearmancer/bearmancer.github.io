Saudi Arabia's Super-Rich Military
Aa

Gathering Momentum

After decades of buying finished foreign weapons, Riyadh is trying to build a domestic arms industry while signing a flurry of new supplier deals. The record shows how much of the momentum is real.

The Localization Goal

Vision 2030 set a defense goal alongside the giga-projects. The video quotes it as localizing over half of military spending by 2030.

"localize over half of Saudi Arabia's military spending within the nation by 2030 at the latest"

The regulator's official goal page states that goal. The record shows real movement too.

Localization reached 19.35 percent at end-2023, per the trade guide citing GAMI data. By end-2024 the regulator reported 24.89 percent, per the official achievement notice.

The video says about 20 percent as of 2024, which slightly understates the latest number.

Facility growth is real as well. Five sites in 2019 became 311 registered establishments by end-2024, per the GAMI licensing record.

But the video argues this growth is mostly relocated assembly. It says complex-weapons programs simply do not exist.

"programs to develop complex military equipment simply do not exist"

The state firm SAMI was founded in 2017 with units covering weapons, missiles and defense electronics, per the founding announcement. Complex work happens through foreign joint ventures, so the absolute wording overstates the case.

Planning and Ambition

The institutional critique lands harder. A 2018 Washington study found Saudi purchases followed no coherent plan.

"far too many major Saudi security decisions ... have not been part of any coherent force improvement plan ... limited regard to cost-effectiveness"

The quotation appears verbatim in the original CSIS study. It is the video's best-sourced passage.

The fighter-jet anecdote holds in substance. Riyadh asked into the British-Italian-Japanese sixth-generation program and was turned away.

"the Saudis could start by assembling a few Eurofighter jets on an assembly line"

The remark came from a Leonardo co-director general, reported by Defense News. The condescension framing is the video's own editorial gloss.

In the 2019 export-controls interview, asked about selling where Western controls bite, the GAMI governor said exports were not the priority, per the interview transcript. Modest ambitions, on the record.

The Shopping Spree

The video puts the May 2025 American package at nearly 150 billion dollars.

"Washington agreed to sell Saudi Arabia nearly $150 billion US worth of weapons"

The White House recorded 142 billion dollars. The F-35 was discussed but left out of the package, per trade-press reporting.

The video says Beijing now supplies four times the equipment it once did.

"China now supplies four times the amount of equipment that it once provided"

SIPRI data puts America at 74 percent of Saudi imports with Spain and France next, leaving China outside the top three, per the transfers factsheet. Real Chinese sales are niche drones and missiles, documented in an archived Army assessment.

The other supplier claims hold. The Korean air-defense purchase was 3.2 billion dollars for ten KM-SAM Block II batteries, per Defense News. Germany returned as a supplier with 150 IRIS-T missiles approved after the post-2018 ban, per Reuters.

Turkey's drone deal explicitly includes technology transfer and joint production, per Reuters. Spain's HAZEM combat-management system comes through the SAMI-Navantia venture, per the shipbuilder. The Korean research pact runs through the joint-committee memorandum, per Reuters.

Missile cooperation with Beijing is contested. American intelligence reporting described Saudi ballistic-missile infrastructure built with Chinese help, per CNN. Beijing denied it.

The Hardware Counts

On hardware, the video rounds upward. Fleet data counts 232 F-15 airframes rather than 300-plus, per fleet reporting. The Saudi-standard subset is real, 84 new jets plus 70 upgrades, per the Air Force announcement.

European fighters total 153, with 81 Tornados plus 72 Typhoons, per fleet data. Attack helicopters number 58 Apaches ordered across three services plus legacy airframes, per Janes-derived delivery reporting. Only light-attack types push the count near one hundred.

Frigates reach eleven hulls only when four under construction are counted, per naval data. The marine corps numbers 3,000, per personnel data.

The tank fleet is roughly 575 Abrams-type main battle tanks in service, per press analysis. Five hundred is a fair midpoint among competing counts.

The dedicated air-defense branch exists, but it fields about 16,000 troops rather than 10,000, per the branch strength table. The video understates its own evidence here.

A fifth service for strategic missiles genuinely exists, per the branch record. But its DF-3 and DF-21 missiles are conventionally modified under a non-nuclear pledge, per the delivery-systems profile. The word nuclear is doing false work.

Deaths, Threats and Joint Development

An ITV investigation attributed about 21,000 foreign-worker deaths to Vision 2030 projects, per Newsweek. Riyadh's safety agency called the report misinformation, per the official rebuttal. Attribution, not body count, is the dispute.

The threat picture holds throughout. Iran did not close Hormuz during the June 2025 war, per shipping-risk analysis. Islamic State fighters are reactivating, per Reuters, and Houthi shipping attacks have renewed, per energy-shipping data.

Joint development is genuinely accelerating. The French naval pact of 2019 stands in Reuters reporting. The Boeing venture of 2022 stands in the owner's announcement.

A Pipeline With One Dominant Tap

Saudi procurement runs as a pipeline with one dominant tap. American systems anchor the force, with diversification deals layered on top, per the same SIPRI import data.

The mechanism has three stages. First, buy finished foreign systems. Second, move final assembly onto Saudi soil through joint ventures. Third, localize subcomponents over time.

Stage two is where Riyadh currently sits. Assembly lines create jobs and facilities without transferring design authority. Establishment counts grow while blueprints stay foreign.

This explains the paradox of fast localization numbers and slow capability. Spending scale is enormous, over 83 billion dollars in 2025, per the global expenditure factsheet. Money buys production lines quickly. It cannot buy the trial-and-error history behind a fighter jet.

Supplier diversification serves a second purpose beyond bargaining. Each new partner opens a separate channel of technical knowledge for Saudi engineers to study. Korean air defense, Turkish drones and Spanish ship systems each teach different lessons.

The missing stage is indigenous design authority. Until Saudi teams specify requirements, test prototypes and own failures, the pipeline produces hardware without producing mastery. That gap is the central mechanism.

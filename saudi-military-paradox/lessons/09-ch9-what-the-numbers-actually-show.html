What the Numbers Actually Show

Chapter 1 opened this course with a gap: Saudi Arabia spends among the most on defence of any country, yet its forces rank in the middle tier. A WarFronts video asking why the kingdom's military performs so poorly despite its wealth then put 49 checkable claims on the table — and the full check of those claims is what this closing chapter synthesises. The through-line survived; the numbers moved; three claims fell over; and several points downstream chapters had never taught are worth keeping.

The Through-Line Survived

The force-size picture from chapters 1 and 2 stands: about 260,000 active-duty troops — 257,000 active plus 24,500 reserve in IISS-derived tables against 247,000 in Global Firepower's count — per the country expenditure table. Spending moved since filming, from $80.3 billion, seventh worldwide at 7.2 percent of GDP, per Arab News, to $83.2 billion, eighth at 6.5 percent, per the SIPRI factsheet — still the peer bracket of Germany ($88.5 billion), India ($86.1 billion), Britain (about $81.8 billion), and France (about $65–75 billion), per the SIPRI press release. The regional ratios from chapter 2 need only trims: Turkey at 481,000 active and 380,000 reserve, per the Turkish forces record; Egypt matching cleanly at 438,500, 479,000, and $4.82 billion, per the Egyptian forces record; Israel's 169,500 and Iraq's 193,000, per the Israeli and Iraqi records, in the same order of magnitude, with Saudi's 247,000-257,000 running 30 to 50 percent above both.

The hardware from chapters 2 and 5 stands with the same rounding the video applies everywhere: about 575 Abrams, per the National Interest; Apache orders of 58 split 36 for the land forces, 12 for the Guard, and 10 for the Royal Guard, per Janes via Miragec14, the round hundred only counting light types; 11 frigates with four still building and 3,000 marines, per the navy inventory and GlobalSecurity; the Saudi-standard F-15 subset of 84 new builds plus 70 upgrades, per the US Air Force, a slice of the 232-aircraft F-15 fleet chapter 5 counted; the separate European fleet runs 153 strong with 81 Tornados and 72 Typhoons, per the fleet count; and a real fifth-branch missile force flying conventional-modified DF-3 and DF-21 missiles under a non-nuclear pledge, per its service record and the NTI delivery-systems record — so "nuclear" mislabels it.

The industrial story of chapters 3 to 5 stands too: Al Fahd carriers built domestically since 1998, per Army Technology, against otherwise imported majors; the Netherlands comparison, eighth against nineteenth, per the expenditure tables, with Damen, Thales Nederland, and Fokker the difference; two percent domestic rising through 19.35 percent to 24.89 percent toward the 50-percent target, per the PIF's 2017 release, trade.gov via GAMI, GAMI, and the Authority's target page; 5 facilities grown to 311 licensed establishments, per GAMI's licensing record; SAMI's 2017 founding and joint-venture route, per the same PIF release; Cordesman's finding of decisions taken with "limited regard to cost-effectiveness," per the 2018 CSIS study; the Mariani stepstone line on Eurofighter assembly, per Defense News; al-Ohali's exports-"not our priority at the moment," per the 2019 Defense News interview.

The deal flow and backdrop from chapter 5 stand as well: the Korean KM-SAM and German IRIS-T buys, per Defense News and Reuters; the HAZEM, Baykar, DAPA, Naval Group, and Boeing ventures, per Navantia, Reuters' 2023 reporting, Reuters, Reuters, and the PIF release; the contested ballistic-missile-cooperation reports, per CNN and NBC; the attributed 21,000 worker deaths and the Saudi denial, per Newsweek and New Civil Engineer; Hormuz left open in the June 2025 war, per Risk Intelligence; the live ISIS and Houthi threats, per Reuters, June 2025 and S and P Global; the Guard's coup-watch purpose under its own ministry, per the Guard record; and the trainer record from Schwarzkopf's "worst in the coalition" through later accounts, per Time, the American Diplomacy collection, and the Washington Examiner — though the video's "most" overreaches what any survey shows.

Three Claims Fell Over

Four wrongs sit on the verdict's ledger; two are Guard size figures of one family. The Guard is not larger than the rest of the military combined: 125,000 standing Guardsmen against about 127,000 Ministry of Defense troops, per IISS via Defense News, and the Guard only looks bigger if 28,000 part-time Fouj levies are counted in. Across estimates the share runs 100,000 to 153,000 of 227,000 to 257,000 — 30 to 49 percent, per GlobalSecurity — never over half.

China does not supply four times what it once did; its role is niche. Saudi imports for 2020–24 ran America 74 percent, Spain 10 percent, France 6.2 percent, with China outside the top three, per the SIPRI transfers factsheet — China, South Korea, and Germany each hold under 6.2 percent, per the SIPRI top-three table. The real Chinese business is missiles and drones: $4 billion of deals in 2022, per al-Monitor, including Wing Loong II and CH-4 types, per the FMSO record, archived — growth from a tiny base, not four times the volume.

The rest of the falls are rounding and framing. The F-15 fleet is 232 airframes, not over 300 — 80 C and D models plus 152 S and SA models in Cirium data, per Aerospace Global News. The May 2025 American package was $142 billion, not about $150 billion, with the F-35 discussed but excluded, per the White House fact sheet and Business Insider. Iran fields 570,000 active, not 600,000-plus, spending about one-eleventh of Riyadh's, per the Iranian forces record and SIPRI; the Turkey spend ratio reached 36 percent in 2025, so "less than a third" is stale. The air-defence branch holds about 16,000 personnel, not 10,000, per the air-defence forces record. And the force split is not "for no clear reason" — the video explains the reason itself two minutes later, and coup-proofing scholarship agrees parallel militaries are deliberate regime-security design, per the Belfer study with its RAND companion and the peer-reviewed rentier-militaries study.

What the Check Added

Artillery had never been counted in this course: 320 self-propelled guns and 257 multiple-launch rocket systems, per the army inventory, with a DSCA notification for 177 M109A6 Paladins behind the howitzer figure. Support aviation is now counted too: five E-3 AWACS plus KE-3 tankers under Peace Sentinel, per Defense Industry Daily, with Saab 2000 airborne early warning aircraft and a C-130 airlift fleet alongside.

The trainer record gained an origin story: American advisers called the Saudis "poorly trained, at best" as far back as 1957, per War Is Boring — three decades before Schwarzkopf. Corruption gained incidents: the Yemen commander Prince Fahd sacked in 2020 over corruption probes, per Al Jazeera, and the 2017 Ritz-Carlton purge of some 400 elites, per the Guardian. The line about troops being "encouraged not to think" has no primary source behind it.

Yemen gained its hired manpower: up to 14,000 Sudanese fighters, including Darfur children, per the New York Times investigation via Al Jazeera, with Colombian mercenaries reported but attribution contested at coalition level, per Middle East Eye. The campaign itself "largely failed to achieve its stated objectives," its six-week plan becoming "prolonged and costly," per the Arab Center DC assessment; the 2019 Abqaiq–Khurais attack halved oil output, per the attack record. Value for money reads as "wasteful, poorly planned, and involved some element of corruption" with "uncertain results," per Cordesman's CSIS study — while the UAE-against-Saudi contrast stays opinion, not measurement.

The Gap, Repriced

The spine matches the scholarship: the world's seventh-to-eighth largest spender with a roughly 30-percent import-dependent force, a Guard answerable to a separate ministry, documented waste, a Yemen war that missed its objectives, and a deliberately coup-proofed structure — the RAND, Belfer, and peer-reviewed rentier-militaries literature all describe weak-but-regime-safe forces as the explicit trade Gulf rulers made. Independent ranking backs the middle of the story: fifth in the Middle East and 25th globally, per Global Firepower 2026, despite the region's top spending — big money, mid-tier outcome. "Awful" is rhetorical overstatement: IISS notes rapid naval modernisation and one of the region's three largest air forces. And the neighbourhood stays fragmented: Gulf missile defence remains un-integrated because states "do not trust each other enough to share sensitive military information," per the Georgetown assessment.

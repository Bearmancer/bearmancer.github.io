Glossary

As of chapter 6, this glossary covers every person, institution, and program the course names. Lessons use these spellings throughout, and where a record check changed the wording, the entry cites the record inline.

People

Mohammed bin Salman. The Crown Prince of Saudi Arabia and the one named individual in the course. Chapter 1 presents him as the figure directing defense policy and economic diversification, the two forces whose mismatch the course examines.

Institutions

General Authority for Military Industries. The state regulator charged with localizing defense spending toward the goal of over half of outlays by 2030. It reported localization of 24.89 percent by the end of 2024, across 311 licensed establishments in the military-industries sector.

Saudi Arabian Military Industries. The state-owned defense company founded in May 2017 with four initial business units covering air systems, land systems, weapons and missiles, and defense electronics. The course treats it as maintenance-heavy and joint-venture dependent rather than a developer of complex indigenous platforms, a reading the record calls partially correct.

Saudi Arabian National Guard. The separate force under its own ministry, tasked with protecting the ruling family and guarding against coups. The course's larger share figures are overstated: the Guard holds roughly four-tenths of total personnel, not over half.

Royal Saudi Air Defense Forces. The dedicated air-defense branch, numbering about 16,000 personnel, above the course's figure of ten thousand.

Royal Saudi Strategic Missile Force. The kingdom's fifth service branch (established as a separate arm), operating DF-3 and DF-21 missiles that were conventionally modified under a non-nuclear pledge. The branch is real; describing its missiles as nuclear is wrong.

Terms and Programs

Coup-proofing. The practice of fragmenting forces into parallel militaries loyal to the regime rather than building one combat-effective army. Scholarship on the region describes its practice and consequences in the Middle East, including the RAND reprint (Quinlivan on coup-proofing).

Rentier state. A state funded by resource rents, chiefly oil, rather than taxation, which loosens the link between spending and accountability. The peer-reviewed account of Gulf militaries argues rulers traded combat power for regime safety, accepting forces that are more hapless but coup-resistant.

Localization and the Vision 2030 target. Localization is the domestic share of Saudi military spending, targeted at half of outlays by 2030. Trade reporting put the share at 19.35 percent at the end of 2023 on the road to that goal.

GCAP. The Global Combat Air Programme, the sixth-generation fighter effort led by Britain, Italy, and Japan. Saudi Arabia sought entry and was rebuffed; in February 2025 a Leonardo executive floated Eurofighter assembly as a stepstone to membership.

KM-SAM. The South Korean air-defense system at the center of a $3.2 billion Saudi order for ten Block II batteries signed in February 2024.

IRIS-T. The German air-defense missile whose delivery to the kingdom resumed in January 2024 after the export ban imposed in 2018.

F-15SA. The Saudi-standard Eagle: 84 newly built aircraft plus upgrades to the existing fleet (US Air Force announcement). The course's figure of over 300 Saudi F-15s is inflated; fleet data puts the total at 232.

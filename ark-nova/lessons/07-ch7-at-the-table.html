At the Table: Setup, Player Counts, and Solo

Setup in Two Passes

Setup runs in two passes. First the shared table: board in the middle, Break token on its track, nine Bonus tiles shuffled with four placed face up on the blank yellow spaces and five returned to the box, the Zoo deck shuffled with six face-down cards dealt as the display, organizers as the supply, then the Association board with one partner zoo per continent, one university of each kind, the base project deck beside it, the Final Scoring deck beside that, and a random start player. Then each player builds a personal zoo: a Map A map, five action cards side one up with Animals under the leftmost slot, appeal, conservation and reputation counters on their first track spaces, seven map tokens on the left edge, four association workers with one active and three waiting, twenty-five money, two hidden Final Scoring cards, and eight drawn Zoo cards of which four are kept. The display flips face up only after every hand is chosen. The Ark Nova rulebook (Capstone Games, English) gives that two-pass shape, and a local visual read of the same pages agrees on the icon-only values.

What Scales with Player Count

Most numbers never move with player count. Display six, money twenty-five, map tokens seven, workers four as one plus three, Final Scoring cards two drawn, starting hand eight drawn and four kept, Bonus tiles four placed and five returned. What does move: face-up base projects below the board are four in a four-player game and three otherwise; appeal starts by turn order at spaces zero, one, two and three. The rulebook setup steps give that table.

The Break token rule is short: "Take the Break token and place it on the start space of the Break track that corresponds to your player count. If there are three players, place the Break token on the space marked 3." Only the three-player space is printed as a number. The two-player and four-player start spaces are icon-only board markings that the visual read could not resolve, so no positions are given here for those two, though the matching-count principle stands.

Two-player games block parts of the shared board with six off-color tokens: "on the face-up Base Conservation Project cards: the left level on the left card, the middle level on the middle card, and the right level on the right card" plus "the three spaces in the left column of the donation area," and "the covered levels and spaces are considered blocked and cannot be used by either player."

New projects played above the Association board obey a cap stated only as a bare list: "Depending on your player count, there can only be up to 2, 3 or 4 cards above the Association board," with the rightmost card discarded past the limit and its tokens returned to owner supplies. The printed pairings are partial: the worked example ties three players to three projects, and the solo page caps solo at two. The four-player-to-four pairing follows from the list order but is never printed.

Association Work and Donations

Association work at strength needs one or two workers per task with total value within the action strength, and a fresh project card may come straight from the display if it sits in reputation range, for money equal to the number on its folder, with no refill until the turn ends.

Donations ride along with that action: exactly one per Association action, only after at least one task, no worker needed, paying the smallest visible donation amount and covering it while gaining one conservation point, with every later donation past the last open space costing twelve and placing no token. The grid reads zero, two, five, five, seven, seven, eight, ten and twelve beside an infinity mark, each money cell carrying a single shield. The rulebook page states the one-per-action rule and the twelve-money last space, and its example has a five-money donation making the next one cost seven.

Solo Play

Solo play rewrites three things and nothing else. Setup lays out three base projects with no blocking tokens, drops the Break token for seven off-color tokens stacked in the solo tile's left column, and starts appeal at twenty. Interactive cards carry a blue stripe whose solo text replaces the multiplayer effect. At most two project cards may sit above the board. Break advances simply do nothing, though break money still pays and no crossing token is earned for triggering a break. Each turn-end slides the topmost left-column token right; emptying the column reveals the break icon and triggers a break that first parks the topmost token on the smallest open donation space, or beside twelve when full, then refills the left column one row shorter and runs every break step except step six. After six rounds, with two tokens left and the bottom one moved right, final scoring replaces the break. "If you have at least 0 Victory Points after the final scoring (meaning both your Appeal counter and Conservation counter have reached the same scoring area or passed each other), you have won the solo game."

Difficulty scales the start: twenty appeal for beginners, ten or zero for veterans. The three-game challenge chains games without shuffling: strip every card of the finished game, remove the two draft maps and the four Bonus tiles, reuse three random removed Bonus tiles for the third game, keep Assertion and Dominance working across the gap, record each score and pass if the three-game sum reaches zero.

From here the course turns practical: chapter 8 reads the display and the hand you keep, and chapter 9 compresses the Marine Worlds expansion into five minutes.

Setup as a Pipeline

Setup is a fixed pipeline with three player-count valves: project row width, two-player blocking, and the Break start space.

  • Everything personal is constant, so learn it once and reuse it at every count.
  • The blocked levels and donation spaces shrink two-player access symmetrically, which both players feel equally.
  • The above-board project cap throttles how many open goals the table holds before old ones spill to the discard.
  • Donations convert spare money into conservation at a rising price, with twelve as the permanent last fare.

Solo replaces the shared clock with a private one. The tile counts your turns, taxes the donation grid each break, and shortens itself every round, so the six-round limit is enforced by components rather than memory.

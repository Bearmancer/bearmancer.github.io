Training and Equipment

Recruits describe reaching the front after five days. The law requires four months of training — whatever the true average, the gap between statute and practice is the point.

"This training was a joke. We fired a few shots from our rusty rifles and spent the rest of the time just wandering around."

That recruit's account is one voice among many. Storm-Z assault units run ten-to-fifteen-day courses, so shortened preparation is the norm for the men thrown forward fastest.

Assignments themselves are bought and sold: the extortion economy around postings runs through Meduza's pay-and-death reporting on money moving around service. Soldiers were crowdfunding their own boots and scopes back in 2022 — an older picture that may not match today.

An officer recalls two years to train a Soviet-era tank crew. Western intelligence projections give Russia about a decade to rebuild, via the ten-year rebuild headline — so five-to-ten years is a range of estimates, not a measured timeline.

Three Training Lengths

Three training lengths, never averaged:

  • Five days: one recruit's account, the sharpest number.
  • Ten to fifteen days: short courses for assault-role troops.
  • Four months: the legal baseline both fall short of.

Equipment losses compound the training gap — untaught crews break what little kit arrives — under the same decade-long projection.

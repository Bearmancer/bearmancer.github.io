The company & the two businesses

  • Amway / Amway Corporation — the soap-and-products company, founded 1959 (the video's chapter 2 says "1949"; that date belongs to Ja-Ri — see lesson 0001). In the entry stories it looks minor: "just the corporation that manufactures the soap".
  • Ja-Ri Corporation — what DeVos and Van Andel actually formed in 1949; their Nutrilite distributorship vehicle; merged into Amway Corporation in 1964.
  • The American Way Association — incorporated April 1959; ancestor of the name "Amway" (short for "American Way").
  • Nutrilite — the organization DeVos and Van Andel sold for before Amway; the video calls it "generally regarded as the first multi-level marketing company" (checked in lesson 0001: the multi-level system dates to 1945, Mytinger & Casselberry's national distributorship).
  • Richard DeVos & Jay Van Andel — Amway's co-founders; conservative Calvinists of the Christian Reformed Church. Chapter 4 adds a footnote: DeVos reportedly sponsored Fred Hansen, "one of the original Amway distributors", in the '50s.
  • The two pyramids (tools vs Amway) — the video's central structural claim (ch3): the Amway MLM business and the tools business are two overlapping pyramids — separate ownership, separate leadership, historically acrimonious, symbiotic, and unable to exist without each other. The larger, semi-hidden one is the tools cult: "where most of the money is and a lot of the abuses occur". Amway's FTC-blessed legality covers only the smaller pyramid; the tools business's legality is, in the video's phrase, untested.
  • Tools / BSMs (business support materials) — the motivational products sold to distributors: tapes, books, live seminars and rallies; later a voicemail system called Amvox. Circa 1980: ~$5 a tape (~$17 today), about 60¢ to make; sold through standing orders and at rally tables (up to $10,000 cash a night). The video's whole subject — "the larger pyramid".
  • Standing order — the arrangement by which a distributor agrees to buy a tape (usually weekly) regardless of what it is, until cancelled — "which it would almost never be".
  • The 1979 FTC decision — the ruling Amway has trumpeted as its seal of legality. Applies solely to the smaller pyramid; the tools business was neither declared legal nor illegal by it.

Roles, ranks & structure

  • Distributor / IBO ("independent business owner") — anyone who joins. Amway's own term is IBO; the video's point: the jargon turns a buyer into a "business owner".
  • Line of sponsorship — the chain of recruiters a distributor joins; upline = the recruiter above you; downline = those you recruit and their recruits. Upline–downline chains "are very similar to family genealogies, another reason why Amway organizations resemble the structure of the mafia" (ch4).
  • MLM (multi-level marketing) — income primarily from recruiting further sellers rather than retail customers; Amway as archetype.
  • Going direct / Direct Distributor — ordering enough product to buy directly from Amway instead of through your upline's house; in the 1982 Wisconsin record "Direct Distributors" are the top tier (~200 of ~20,000 distributorships) whose tax returns produced the $918 average net loss (checked in lesson 0001).
  • Pin levels — the jewel hierarchy: pearl, ruby, emerald, and the brass ring, diamond (higher levels were later invented as kingpins' businesses grew). "Going diamond" is the movement's euphemism for financial success — "as if it's a foregone conclusion".
  • Diamonds — the Amway stars fêted at rallies alongside the tool kingpins.
  • Kingpins — the men at the top of the tools business; named so far: Bill Britt, Dexter Yager, Doug Wead, and the (unnamed) husband of Betty Joe Renfro. Britt, Yager and Wead are the ones with speaking parts.

The practice & vocabulary

  • "Showing the plan" / "drawing circles" — the classic recruitment meeting: butcher paper, markers, circles standing for recruits and their recruits; Amway often named only at the end.
  • Rallies & seminars — the live arm of the tools: pageantry and "populist hysteria", stars fêted "like conquering Roman emperors or rock stars"; tape tables at the entrances; tapes often recorded straight off the stage — "a snake eating its own tail". The 1980 Charlotte rally ended with distributors in the streets at 3 a.m. chanting "Five and six nights a week" — the nights they intend to spend drawing circles.
  • The Wisconsin study (as told in chapter 2) — "in 1980, the Wisconsin Attorney General crunched the numbers of the top 1% of Amway distributors in that state"; nearly all lost money net of operating costs; average loss put at $900 (checked in lesson 0001: tax years 1979–80, case filed July 1982, the figure is $918).

The people (as of chapter 4)

  • Dexter Yager — the tape business's distribution half. Born 1939, Rome, New York; poor family; his own hagiography stresses a born-salesman childhood (sixth-grade soda venture) that the video doubts; a car salesman and "beer salesman with an outfit called the West End Brewery"; early heavy drinker. Joins Amway 1964 — makes no real money (job-hunting by ~1967); moves to Charlotte, NC in 1969 with Birdie and the children. Sponsors Renard (1966); declines the $50,000 tape-plant pitch; distributes Britt's tapes through his downline; gets rich "within a few short years". (Video captions sometimes render the name "Yeager"; canonical here: Yager.)
  • Birdie Yager — Dexter's wife; four children at the Charlotte move, eventually seven.
  • Tony Renard — sponsored into Amway by Yager, Fall 1966; buys a second-hand tape recorder from him; begins duplicating motivational tapes; by the late '60s tapes are moving through Yager's downline — "at first, they're not a profit center".
  • Dominic Conigliaro — Amway distributor sponsored by Yager; in 1970 he sponsors Bill Britt.
  • Bill Britt — the tape business's manufacturing half. "Virtually a black box" biography: U.S. Army service in the Korean War; minor official in Charlotte-area municipal governments. Joins Amway 1970 (sponsor: Conigliaro). At the Fontainebleau coffee-shop meeting he answers the nameless man's $50,000 tape-duplicating pitch — which Yager declined — with "I'll do it."
  • Fred Hansen — "one of the original Amway distributors", reportedly sponsored by Rich DeVos in the '50s; possibly the man who lent Yager the Nightingale record. The video keeps this uncertain.
  • ? The nameless young man — the person Wead's account says pitched the tape-plant investment at the Fontainebleau Hotel coffee shop, "for a $50,000 investment". Identity unknown; Wead himself is considered and set aside (too young, hadn't met Yager yet by most accounts).
  • Doug Wead — a tools kingpin and, in this chapter, the key source: the Fontainebleau founding account is his. Earlier (ch3) quoted on "the tape of the day"; the video promises a fuller profile later. Open thread.
  • Betty Joe Renfro — wife of a tools kingpin; told Wead at a rally: "Don't you ever talk about the tape of the week to our group… We have the tape of the day, or the tape of the hour." Her husband is not yet named.

The lineage & parallels

  • The Strangest Secret (1956) — Earl Nightingale's spoken-word record; "the earliest motivational tape" and the model for the whole duplication practice; reaches Yager mid-'60s (possibly via Hansen).
  • Napoleon Hill — author of Think and Grow Rich (1937), later at the top of the classic Amway reading list; Nightingale's inspiration; in the video's telling "a con man with a long string of failed businesses".
  • Dale Carnegie — How to Win Friends and Influence People; part of the same 1930s–50s self-help wave the tools recycled; the video notes it "inspired, among others, Charles Manson".
  • Norman Vincent Peale — conservative evangelical preacher; his New York congregation included the Trump family; another node of the same wave.
  • Holiday Magic and Koscot Interplanetary — two early multi-level marketing operations also developing motivational divisions in the late '60s; parallels to the Amway tools, not yet linked by any personal line.
  • The Fontainebleau meeting (~1970/71) — the founding scene of the tools business, as Wead's account tells it: Nameless pitches; Yager declines; Britt accepts. The video flags that the account's source status must be weighed (see lesson 0002's fact-check).

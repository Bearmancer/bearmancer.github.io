The French Sovereign Debt Crisis

In the Eurozone, German Bunds serve as the continent's risk-free benchmark. Every other sovereign borrows at a spread over them—a measure of the extra yield investors demand for credit risk. French OATs (Obligations Assimilables du Trésor) historically traded just 20–40 basis points wide. That stability broke down as markets questioned France's ability to rein in its finances.

France's debt-to-GDP ratio has climbed to 120%, with persistent deficits above 5.5% of GDP—well over the EU's 3% Maastricht limit. Public spending exceeds 57% of GDP, the highest in Europe. With little fiscal slack, bond market tolerance ran out. The French–German 10-year spread widened to 175 basis points—its widest since the 2011 sovereign debt crisis. French borrowing costs rose above 5% and briefly exceeded Italian BTPs.

Snap elections left the National Assembly split among three hostile blocs with no majority. Meaningful fiscal consolidation—spending cuts, pension reform, or tax hikes—became politically impossible. Any prime minister attempting reform faced immediate no-confidence votes, locking France into gridlock at the moment markets demanded action.

As a euro member, France cannot depreciate its currency or unilaterally monetize debt. The ECB can intervene in extremis, but support isn't unconditional—tools like the Transmission Protection Instrument require fiscal discipline a divided parliament cannot deliver. Investors see no credible backstop if gridlock persists.

Bond vigilantes have returned. Markets no longer grant G7 sovereigns automatic exemption from unsustainable deficits. A sharp repricing of a core Eurozone economy signals no guarantee of cheap funding, no assumed bailout, and no return to zero-rate complacency—for governments and capital-intensive borrowers alike.

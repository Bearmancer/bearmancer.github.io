The French Sovereign Debt Crisis

In Europe, countries that share the euro currency do not print their own money. When European governments need to borrow, they sell loan certificates to private investors. Germany has strict legal limits on debt and a very disciplined national budget, so investors view lending money to Germany as the safest investment in Europe. Because Germany carries the lowest risk of default, its interest rate serves as Europe's baseline. Historically, investors viewed France almost as safely as Germany. When France borrowed money, it paid only a fraction of a percentage point more than Germany. That long period of trust has broken down.

France has spent more than it collects in taxes for decades. Government spending in France now accounts for 57% of its total economic output, the highest share of any nation in Europe. Total French government debt has climbed to 120% of its yearly economy, and its annual budget deficit reached 5.5%, almost double the safety limit set by European agreements. For years, investors ignored these numbers because central banks kept interest rates near zero. When global interest rates rose, the cost of paying interest on that massive debt pile exploded, and lenders began to worry whether France could ever afford its bills.

To fix a budget deficit, a government must either cut public spending or raise taxes. France cannot do either. Recent elections divided the French parliament into three hostile political groups, with no single faction holding a majority. Whenever a prime minister proposes spending cuts or tax hikes, opposing politicians unite to remove them from office. Unlike the United States or Britain, France cannot order its central bank to print new currency to pay off its loans, because the euro is managed collectively by the European Central Bank. The central bank refuses to bail out countries that fail to control their own budgets, leaving France trapped with no political way to fix its finances.

When investors realized France was politically paralyzed, they rebelled. Lenders refused to buy French government loans unless France paid a steep penalty. The extra interest rate France had to pay above Germany jumped from a normal gap of 0.3 percentage points to 1.75 percentage points. Total French borrowing costs climbed above 5%, briefly rising higher than borrowing costs for Italy, a country long considered much riskier. When private lenders refuse to fund a government without punishing interest rates, they force financial discipline on politicians who refuse to budget responsibly.

Steve Eisman highlighted the French debt crisis because it demonstrates that financial markets have re-established strict limits on debt. For fifteen years, businesses and governments assumed money would always remain cheap and that central banks would always intervene to rescue borrowers. The repricing of France proves that lenders no longer give automatic passes to anyone, even major world powers. If one of Europe's largest economies is forced to pay high interest rates because of its debts, private tech companies trying to borrow billions of dollars for speculative AI data centers cannot expect cheap money either.

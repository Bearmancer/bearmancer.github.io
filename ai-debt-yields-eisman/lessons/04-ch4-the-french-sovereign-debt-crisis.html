The French Sovereign Debt Crisis

In the Eurozone, German Bunds serve as the continent's risk-free benchmark. Every other sovereign borrows at a spread over them, a simple measure of how much extra yield investors demand to take on that government's credit risk. For decades, French OATs (Obligations Assimilables du Trésor) traded in lockstep with Bunds, typically just 20 to 40 basis points wide. That longstanding stability broke down as markets began to question whether France could bring its finances under control.

France's debt-to-GDP ratio has climbed to 120%, while persistent budget deficits of over 5.5% of GDP have kept it well above the EU's Maastricht limit for years. Public spending now exceeds 57% of GDP, the highest ratio in Europe. With so little fiscal slack and deficits compounding year after year, the bond market's tolerance for inaction ran out. The spread between French 10-year OATs and German 10-year Bunds widened to 175 basis points—its widest level since the 2011 European sovereign debt crisis. At its peak, French borrowing costs rose above 5% and, in an extraordinary turn, briefly exceeded those of Italian BTPs, long seen as the Eurozone's canonical high-risk sovereign.

Several forces converged to trigger that blowout. Snap legislative elections left France's National Assembly fractured into three mutually hostile blocs with no group holding an absolute majority. With no path to a stable coalition, passing meaningful fiscal consolidation—whether through spending cuts, pension reform, or tax increases—became politically impossible. Any prime minister who dared to try faced an immediate vote of no confidence, locking the country into a state of fiscal gridlock at precisely the moment markets were demanding action.

Compounding that political paralysis is the structural constraint of the euro. France has no currency of its own to depreciate and no ability to unilaterally monetize its debt. The European Central Bank does have tools to stabilize markets in extremis, but access to support is not unconditional. Instruments like the Transmission Protection Instrument come with expectations of fiscal discipline—expectations that a divided parliament cannot meet. For investors, there is simply no credible backstop if political gridlock prevents a fiscal adjustment.

The return of bond vigilantes is the broader point. Markets have re-established a hard price for unsustainable deficits, and they are no longer willing to grant G7 sovereigns an automatic exemption from it. When a core Eurozone economy can be repriced this sharply on the basis of chronic deficits and political paralysis, the message is unambiguous: there is no guarantee of cheap funding, no assumed bailout, and no return to zero-rate complacency. For heavily indebted borrowers everywhere—from governments down to capital-intensive AI infrastructure projects—that repricing is the new reality.

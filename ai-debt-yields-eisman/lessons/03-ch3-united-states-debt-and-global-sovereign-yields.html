United States Debt and Global Sovereign Yields

A government bond is an IOU. When the United States government spends more money than it collects in taxes, it borrows the difference from investors. An investor hands the government cash today. In return, the government promises to pay fixed annual interest, historically called a coupon, and return the original sum at a set future date. The actual percentage return the investor earns each year on their investment is the yield. Because the American government can raise taxes and print currency, investors treat its IOUs as the safest place on earth to store cash. Default is considered impossible.

Because American government debt is completely safe, its interest rate sets the floor for the entire economy. No investor will lend money to a private business at 3% or 4% if they can get a guaranteed 5% from Washington. Every corporate loan must pay the government baseline plus an extra fee to cover the risk of the company going bankrupt. Large, cash-rich corporations might pay two percentage points above the baseline. High-risk technology companies building artificial intelligence data centers must pay ten percentage points above it. When government borrowing rates rise, borrowing costs for every company in the world climb with them.

The United States owes more than $35 trillion in total debt, which is 120% of its entire yearly economic output. The government also runs an extra deficit between $1.5 trillion and $2.0 trillion every year. During the 2010s, Washington borrowed trillions of dollars at tiny interest rates near 1%. Those loans were temporary, and they are now reaching their end dates. The government does not have cash to pay them off, so it pays off each expiring loan by taking out a new loan at current rates between 4% and 5%. This flood of new government IOUs soaks up vast amounts of investor cash every month, keeping global borrowing costs locked at high levels.

Any other country with a debt burden equal to 120% of its economy would trigger a financial panic and a collapsing currency. The United States avoids this because the US dollar is the global reserve currency. International trade in oil, raw materials, and manufactured goods is priced in dollars. Foreign central banks and global businesses must keep huge reserves of dollars and US government IOUs on hand just to settle daily commerce. This forced global demand guarantees that the American government always finds willing buyers for its debt, even as its annual deficits widen.

The dollar's special status protects Washington, but it offers no protection to private technology companies. Because global lenders can earn a safe 5% return by holding United States debt, they refuse to take risks on private borrowers for cheap money. Specialized AI cloud providers who borrow tens of billions to buy Nvidia processors are forced to pay 14% to 16% interest on their loans. The United States government's massive borrowing needs keep baseline rates high, ending the era of cheap speculative capital that launched the AI buildout.

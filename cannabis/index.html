Cannabis

Cannabis is the only drug in this ledger with decades-long population cohorts. Most long-term data concern smoked cannabis; route is often unreported in the cognition and mental-health studies. Matching-line counts in claims.yaml (my own rg -i -c): cannabis|marijuana|\bTHC\b → 1,910 lines.

Inhaled

Airways and lung function — the best long-term evidence in the whole ledger.

  • The Dunedin birth cohort (n=1,037, followed from birth) at age 32: cumulative cannabis use was associated with higher FVC (+12.0 mL per joint-year after tobacco adjustment, c2956), higher total lung capacity, FRC and residual volume (hyperinflation), and higher large-airway resistance, but not with lower FEV1, FEV1/FVC or impaired gas transfer once tobacco was accounted for s17. Findings were similar among never-tobacco smokers s17.
  • The same cohort re-assessed at age 45: per joint-year, FEV1 −2.7 mL (non-significant), FVC +6.6 mL, FEV1/FVC −0.13 percentage points (p=0.004), TLC +18.1 mL, and — a first report — reduced gas transfer (DlCO −0.051, p=0.045; DlCO/Va −0.016, p<0.001) s30 (c4004–c4022, c4090). The pattern is not the tobacco/COPD pattern: no accelerated FEV1 decline across adulthood; emphysema was uncommon unless participants also smoked tobacco s30.
  • CARDIA (>5,000 young adults, 20 years of repeated measures): the exposure–response is non-linear. At low exposure FEV1 rose (+13 mL/joint-year); above ~10 joint-years the FEV1 slope turned slightly negative (−2.2 mL/joint-year, borderline); above 20 episodes/month FEV1 declined significantly (−3.2 mL per episode/month, p=.017), while FVC stayed elevated (+76 mL at 20 joint-years) s42 (c6827–c6904). Up to ~7 joint-years there was no evidence of harm; the sample had too few very heavy users to confirm a detrimental effect at the top end s42 (c6910–c6911).
  • A structured review of 19 spirometry studies: chronic cannabis smoking reliably produces bronchitic symptoms (cough, sputum, wheeze) and large-airway injury; six studies found small FEV1/FVC reductions (0.5–1.9%); recent larger studies show raised FVC rather than obstruction; chronic smokers "do not appear to develop airflow obstruction and COPD" s43 (c7458, c7637). One pack-year of tobacco was estimated equivalent to 4.1–7.9 joint-years for airflow obstruction (Aldington, via s43).

Cancer and bullous disease. Lung-cancer evidence is inconclusive: a pooled analysis of 6 case–control studies (2,159 cases) found little or no association (pooled OR 0.96); a 64,855-person retrospective cohort found no increased risk after 8.6 years; but a 40-year Swedish conscript cohort (49,321 men) found ~2-fold risk in those who smoked cannabis >50 times, and one small case–control study found a per-joint-year increase after tobacco adjustment s43 (c7604–c7616). Macroscopic emphysema was not increased in cannabis-only smokers (1.3% vs 16% in tobacco±cannabis) s43. Seventeen case series/reports describe bullous lung disease (56 heavy smokers, often with pneumothorax); epidemiological confirmation is lacking s43s30 (c7589–c7594, c4112).

Reversibility. Bronchitis symptoms improve after quitting and fall to never-user levels (Hancox quitters; Tashkin cessation follow-up averaged 9.8 years) s43 (c7571–c7578). In Dunedin, 158 participants who cut down or quit showed no statistically significant spirometric change either way, and whether the lung-function effects are reversible remains unknown s30 (c4078–c4082, c3952).

Vaping. Vaporizers are suggested to reduce pulmonary complications, but there were no peer-reviewed studies of different cannabis smoking methods and lung health, and Dunedin collected no vaping data s43s30 (c7416–c7417, c4107). Long-term vaping evidence: none in this ledger.

Acute PK context. Inhaled cannabis acts within 10 minutes, peaks at 30–90 minutes, clears within ~4 hours s32.

Eaten

Pharmacokinetics (controlled dosing). Oral THC reaches significant blood levels only after ≥30 minutes, peaks at ~3 hours, and clears at ~12 hours s32 (c5485–c5487). In a brownie study (10/25/50 mg THC), blood Cmax was 1/3.5/3.3 ng/mL; the active metabolite 11-OH-THC reached similar Cmax/Tmax to THC; blood detection windows ran 0–22 h; blood levels correlated with subjective effects but not orderly with cognitive impairment s40 (c6079–c6094). Oral cannabinoid levels are low compared with inhalation s40. Oral THC bronchodilation can last up to 6 h s43.

Population-level harm signals (Colorado, 2014–2016). Edibles were 0.32% of THC sold by weight but accounted for 10.7% of 2,567 cannabis-attributable ED visits — about 33× more than expected if routes were equally toxic s32 (c5451, c5512–c5514). Edibles are a major source of unintentional pediatric exposure s32. The only Colorado deaths definitively associated with cannabis involved edible products s32 (c5494) — yet none of the 2,567 ED visits died (c5413). Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome was less frequent in edible than inhalable visits (8.4% vs 18.0%) s32.

Long-term edible-specific evidence: none. The 39 Eaten-route claims in the digest (read in full) are PK, sales and ED-visit data; no long-term cohort of edible-only users exists in the ledger. The delayed onset/long duration above is the basis for the acute overdose risk, not a documented chronic effect.

Cross-route long-term findings (route usually unreported).

  • Cognition. A 69-study meta-analysis (2,152 users vs 6,575 controls, age ≤26) found a small overall deficit, d=−0.25; abstinence >72 hours diminished deficits; effect sizes did not vary by age of onset s16 (c353–c370). In the large PNC community sample, frequent adolescent users showed small executive-control deficits at ages 14–17, occasional users showed relative strengths, and earlier initiation predicted poorer executive control in occasional users; the design is cross-sectional s21 (c788–c800, c829). The IQ-decline question is represented only indirectly: longitudinal studies link continued use to IQ decline, but two recent longitudinal studies found no IQ association after adjusting for confounders and familial factors s21 (c715, c718). The primary Dunedin IQ papers (s1, s5) have 0 evidence-linked claims in the ledger, so neither side of that debate can be cited directly here.
  • Psychosis. Sourced coverage is thin: of 13 cannabis-section lines mentioning psychosis/schizophrenia, 8 are unsourced claims, 1 is a study-limitation statement s21, and 4 come from the single Colorado ED study — 297 visits for acute psychiatric symptoms including acute psychosis, 100 for chronic conditions including schizophrenia/psychosis, and the statements that THC can cause or exacerbate psychosis s32 (c5421, c5423, c5495–c5496). The often-quoted cohort/conscription evidence is not present as cited claims.
  • Dependence and treatment. There are no FDA-approved treatments for cannabis use disorder s6 (c2712); a 15-person proof-of-concept RCT of ketamine for CUD is registered s6 (c2806). The 9%/17%/25–50% addiction figures in the ledger appear only as unsourced claims and are not cited here.
  • Other. Cannabis is recognized as a risk factor for adverse cardiovascular events s32 (c5497); cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome produced 440 visits from 301 patients (up to 17 visits/patient/year) s32 (c5418).

Psilocybin

Coverage: 720 matching lines (psilocybin|psilocin). The ledger contains the longest controlled follow-ups of any repurposed drug here except esketamine, plus one 4.5-year observational follow-up.

  • Treatment-resistant depression. COMP005, the first Phase 3 trial of COMP360 (synthetic psilocybin), dosed 258 TRD participants at 32 US sites; a single 25 mg dose produced a −3.6-point MADRS difference vs placebo at week 6 (95% CI −5.7 to −1.5, p<0.001) s18 (c1271–c1274). Part B remains blinded through week 26, with open-label treatment weeks 26–52; the DSMB found no new or unexpected safety findings and no clinically meaningful suicidality imbalance across COMP005/COMP006 s18 (c1281–c1287). COMP006 (568 planned) is ongoing s18. Earlier: a randomized waitlist study reported d=2.3 at 1 month, 71% response, 54% remission after two doses s3 (c1766–c1768); 24 completers of two-dose therapy were followed to 12 months with stable attributed well-being and no depression exacerbation s3 (c1784, c1875, c1886). Against escitalopram, the 6-week primary outcome (QIDS) was not significantly different; secondary measures favored psilocybin but were uncorrected for multiplicity s3 (c1770–c1772).
  • Cancer-related distress. High-dose psilocybin (22–30 mg/70 kg) vs very low dose produced large decreases in depressed mood and anxiety, increased quality of life/meaning/optimism, decreased death anxiety; mystical-type experience mediated outcomes s19 (c2659–c2667). A follow-up study documented decreases in depressive symptoms up to 4.5 years after treatment in cancer-related distress — the longest psilocybin follow-up in the ledger, small and uncontrolled s3 (c1902).
  • Alcohol use disorder. The first controlled AUD trial (n=93 treated; 48 psilocybin, 45 diphenhydramine, both with psychotherapy): percentage of heavy drinking days during 32 double-blind weeks was 9.7% vs 23.6% (difference 13.86 points, g=0.52, p=.01); abstinence at weeks 33–36 was 47.9% vs 24.4% s41 (c6624, c6676, c6684). No serious adverse events in the psilocybin arm; headache 43.8% vs 4.4%; one 15-minute episode of passive suicidal ideation during a session, resolved s41 (c6477, c6700, c6704). The authors state the study provides no information beyond 32 weeks s41 (c6725).
  • Pipeline with longer horizons. PsiloMDDAUD (MDD+AUD, n=90, Phase 2) measures outcomes to 12 months after the second session s11; PsilOCD (n=19, 10 mg ×2) completed 2024 s11; a Parkinson's-depression pilot (n=12) reported 0 serious adverse events with follow-up to 3 months s11 (c7012); a smoking-cessation RCT vs nicotine patch uses 30 mg/70 kg s11.
  • Long-term safety signals. No reported psychedelic use during the 12-month MDD follow-up s3 (c1897); antidepressant use during follow-up confounds some results s3; psilocybin has relatively low abuse potential s3 (c1918); healthy-volunteer studies report positive effects more than a year after a single dose s38 (c5331). Blinding is a known weakness across the program s41 (c6719).

Bottom line: robust short-term efficacy signals; durability beyond ~6–12 months rests on small, mostly uncontrolled follow-ups (longest 4.5 years). No Phase 3 long-term results are in the ledger yet.

MDMA

Coverage: 1,169 matching lines (\bMDMA\b|ecstasy|midomafetamine). The ledger documents both the strongest long-term follow-up data and the regulatory rejection built on their weaknesses.

  • Phase 3 PTSD trials. MAPP1 (n=91, severe PTSD) and MAPP2 (n=104, moderate–severe): three MDMA-assisted psychotherapy sessions (total 402 mg) over 18 weeks; CAPS-5 placebo-subtracted differences −11.86 (p<0.0001) and −8.91 (p=0.0004); Sheehan Disability Scale also significant s14 (c3389–c3390, c3413–c3414).
  • Long-term follow-up (MPLONG). 142 phase-3 participants enrolled in a single-visit observational follow-up 6 months to >2 years after the parent study (median 23.4 months for MAPP1, 8.5 for MAPP2); CAPS-5 gains were maintained or slightly larger at follow-up (MAPP1 MDMA arm: −30.31 from baseline vs −24.80 at week 18) s14 (c3465, c3475–c3476). But: ~25% dropout, no prespecified type-I-error control, most participants received interim psychotherapy (82.4% of MDMA-arm, 71.7% of placebo-arm), 13 MDMA-arm participants used illicit MDMA between studies, and 94% of MDMA recipients correctly guessed their arm s14 (c3415, c3322, c3437–c3445, c3515).
  • The longest follow-up. Mithoefer's first-trial LTFU: all 19 MDMA-treated subjects assessed 17–74 months (mean 45.4) after the final session; CAPS gains maintained; no subject developed a substance-abuse problem with any illicit drug afterwards; the original trial found no cognitive morbidity s29 (c7996, c8064, c8115, c8129).
  • Retrieved with caution: the pooled phase-2 LTFU analysis is retracted (2024) s31 (c8652). Before retraction it reported ≥12-month follow-up across six trials, 9.6% post-exit ecstasy/MDMA use (6 of 8 were prior users), and no abuse signals s31 (c8739, c8833–c8835, c8853). Its numbers cannot be relied on.
  • Regulatory outcome. June 2024 FDA advisory committee: effectiveness 2 yes / 9 no; benefit–risk 1 yes / 10 no s20 (c6811–c6812). The August 2024 Complete Response Letter concluded durability was not demonstrated beyond the 18-week assessments, faulted MPLONG's single variable-timing visit and interim interventions, noted ~40% of enrollees had prior MDMA use, found adverse-event collection inconsistent with the protocol (positive effects excluded), and required a new trial plus dedicated cardiac (Holter) and laboratory assessment s28 (c7293–c7310, c7327–c7332).
  • Safety, acute and chronic. In pooled phase 3: systolic BP ≥180 mmHg in 6.1% of MDMA vs 0% placebo; BP ≥140/90 in 67.7% vs 22.1%; all suicidal-ideation/behavior SAEs occurred on placebo; no hepatocellular-injury adverse events in trials, though severe liver injury is documented in illicit use s14 (c4542, c5868, c5874, c4584, c2415). Cardiac safety is "not well characterized" and the QT assessment incomplete s14 (c2406–c2407); FDA judged the hERG assessment inadequate s28. Human abuse-potential studies show equal or greater abuse potential than amphetamine/methamphetamine/methylphenidate s14 (c4709). Illicit-use epidemiology: 693 MDMA-involved overdose deaths 2010–2017, only 7.3% single-substance; poison-center cases less fatal than methamphetamine (0.7% vs 8.2%); acute syndromes include hyperthermia, hyponatremia, rhabdomyolysis, renal failure and delayed hepatic injury s14 (c4756, c4769, c4751, c4787, c4810).
  • Recreational/ecstasy cognition. Findings are contradictory; the best-controlled studies report largely negative findings s29 (c8135–c8136). A valvulopathy concern exists at preclinical level: MDMA induces fenfluramine-like proliferative actions on human cardiac valvular interstitial cells in vitro (cited in s29, c8390); no long-term human valve data are in the ledger.

Bottom line: the longest mean follow-up (45 months) is a 19-person uncontrolled series; the large observational follow-up was judged insufficient by FDA; chronic cardiac, hepatic and cognitive safety in therapeutic use remains uncharacterized.

Ketamine

Coverage: 565 matching lines (ketamine|esketamine). Two distinct long-term pictures: supervised intermittent esketamine (large, multi-year) vs daily recreational ketamine (case-series harm).

  • Esketamine (Spravato), TRD. EU-authorized with an SSRI/SNRI; relapse-prevention trial: 27% relapse on esketamine vs 45% on placebo; a ~1-year study showed maintained benefit s37 (c4487–c4490). SUSTAIN-3: 1,148 TRD adults treated up to 6.5 years, 3,777 cumulative patient-years (4,247 including parent studies; 9.7% treated ≥72 months); no new safety signals with intermittent dosing, including cognitive, hepatic and renal safety; no new concerns for blood pressure, renal disorders, lower urinary tract symptoms or suicidality; 9 deaths (0.8%), none considered treatment-related (one completed suicide on day 26, 4 days after last dose); 6.4% discontinued for adverse events; 97% of dosing-day adverse events resolved the same day; no evidence of abuse, misuse or withdrawal within the supervised delivery model s39 (c4817, c4794, c4954–c4955, c4812, c5100, c5006, c4944, c5007, c4970, c5103). Real-world surveillance shows rising misuse of ketamine but not of esketamine s39 (c5104). ESCAPE-TRD: 37.0% functional remission at 32 weeks s39.
  • Substance use disorders. A review of human studies: heroin — 50% abstinence at 1 year with multiple ketamine sessions vs 22.2% single-session s6 (c2761); alcohol — 65.8% vs 24% abstinence at 1 year (ketamine-assisted psychotherapy vs follow-up-as-usual), 40.7% at 2 years (no 2-year control) s6 (c2776–c2777); cocaine — craving reduced ~60% acutely but losing significance over follow-up s6 (c2749, c2815); significant between-group effects up to two years after a single infusion are reported s6 (c2693). No human studies for tobacco or stimulants other than cocaine s6 (c2731). Ketamine requires repeated administration to maintain antidepressant efficacy and has nontrivial abuse potential s3 (c1916–c1917).
  • Recreational ketamine — ketamine-induced cystitis (KIC). Urinary symptoms occur in >25% of recreational users, dose- and frequency-related; regular use raises cystitis risk 3–4-fold (20% of frequent vs 6.7% of infrequent users) s36 (c3866–c3867, c3835). Damage progresses from urothelial denudation to bladder fibrosis, contracted bladder, hydronephrosis and chronic kidney failure s36 (c3846, c3873). Cessation usually improves symptoms, but they can persist or worsen: one case report describes symptoms persisting 9 years after cessation; another required neobladder reconstruction; a teen case combined cholangiopathy with ulcerative cystitis s36 (c3868, c3898–c3899, c3897). Malignancy risk is not well defined s36 (c3903). Dependence is chiefly psychological with cravings on cessation s36 (c3906–c3907).

Bottom line: the only multi-year (to 6.5 y) safety database in the ledger belongs to supervised esketamine; recreational daily use carries documented, sometimes irreversible urinary-tract harm. The two exposure patterns must not be conflated.

LSD

Coverage: 433 matching lines (\bLSD\b|lyserg). Long-term evidence is historical (1960s alcoholism RCTs) plus one small modern 12-month follow-up; new Phase 3 trials have no long-term data yet.

  • Alcoholism (historical RCTs). A meta-analysis of six trials (1966–1971) found remission nearly twice as often with LSD (OR 1.96, 95% CI 1.36–2.84, p<.001) s41s38 (c6487, c5332); a single dose compared favorably with daily naltrexone/acamprosate/disulfiram s38 (c5334). Individual trials: Tomsovic & Edwards — higher abstinence at 3 months maintained at 1 year; Savage & McCabe — higher total abstinence at 12 months; Pahnke — 6-month improvements; Hollister — significant at 2 months but not at 6 months s38 (c5298–c5308). Three studies showed LSD/control homogenization at long-term follow-up s38 (c5328). Most historical studies predate modern standards s38 (c5147).
  • Anxiety in life-threatening illness (modern). Gasser 2014: 12 patients, two 200 µg LSD-assisted sessions (vs 20 µg active placebo), 12-month follow-up; no drug-related severe adverse events in 22 sessions; no prolonged anxiety, lasting psychotic or perceptual disorders (flashbacks); quality of life rose (EORTC-QLQ 37.4 → 50.0); anxiety reductions trended maintained at 12 months but without significant between-group difference s34s38 (c3745, c3791, c3813, c3779, c5314). All participants correctly guessed their dose — blinding failed s34 (c3788).
  • Current pipeline. MM120 ODT (lysergide D-tartrate) Phase 3 trials in GAD (Voyage, Panorama) and MDD (Emerge) are actively enrolling, 100 µg, 12-week double-blind periods s23 (c1988–c2011). No long-term outcomes exist yet.
  • Chronic-use safety. LSD produces no physical dependence/withdrawal syndrome; tolerance develops with frequent use; classical hallucinogens show very low physiological toxicity "without evidence of organic damage or neuropsychological deficits" s38 (c5181–c5184). Historical use in schizophrenia patients produced worse outcomes s38 (c5192–c5193). Healthy-volunteer studies report positive effects >1 year after a single dose s38 (c5331).

Bottom line: suggestive durable effects from small, old, poorly-blinded studies; no modern long-term controlled data; no signal of organic neurotoxicity in the reviewed literature.

Ibogaine

Coverage: 289 matching lines (ibogaine|noribogaine). The ledger's ibogaine material is dominated by cardiac safety and mortality, not long-term efficacy.

  • Cardiac risk and mortality. Ibogaine blocks hERG channels, prolongs QTc and can cause torsades de pointes; noribogaine (metabolite, half-life 28–49 h) also prolongs QTc s4 (c1946–c1948, c2185–c2194). A 2026 Research Square preprint (not peer-reviewed) reports 19,071 patients treated under contemporary safety guidelines across international clinics (1996–2026) with 6 deaths within 72 h; its updated systematic inventory totals 48 reported ibogaine-associated deaths (1990–2026, 15 countries), up from 19 (1990–2008) and 33 (to 2018) s2 (c1181–c1183, c1200, c1131–c1132). Many earlier fatalities involved preventable factors (excessive dosing, QT-prolonging co-medications, CYP2D6 inhibitors, electrolyte abnormalities); two were accidents during disorientation s2 (c1138, c1226–c1228).
  • Efficacy evidence is observational. Case series report diminished opioid withdrawal, abstinence for varied periods and reduced craving s4 (c1943). A prospective veteran study (Special Operations Forces, TBI+PTSD) of magnesium–ibogaine reported large, sustained symptom improvements with no serious cardiac events — cited within the preprint, uncontrolled s2 (c1118–c1120). The registered "Trifecta" study combines ibogaine–magnesium, 5-MeO-DMT and MeRT in veterans, observational s11 (c7960). Most clinics in the mortality cohort also administered adjunctive 5-MeO-DMT s2 (c1252).
  • Controlled safety data are tiny. One study treated 3 opioid-dependent patients with 10 mg/kg oral ibogaine HCl under intensive monitoring: transient ataxia fully resolved within 24 h, vomiting the only adverse event, withdrawal severity low; "there are no reports of long-lasting neurologic effects of ibogaine treatment"; a human autopsy after multiple doses showed no cerebellar damage (rodent Purkinje-cell death occurs at much higher doses) s4 (c1980, c2159–c2162, c2199–c2200). Anecdotal reports include seizures, mania, HPPD and one suicide under underground supervision s4 (c1954–c1957).
  • Regulatory motion. FDA issued a request for information on early-phase ibogaine trial design, allowed a noribogaine IND for alcohol use disorder, and NIDA funds ibogaine research; Texas allocated $50M for FDA-supervised trials (2025), matched federally s35s2 (c3520–c3536, c1122–c1123).

Bottom line: no controlled long-term outcome data in the ledger (the 289 matching lines are overwhelmingly safety/registry material); the risk–benefit case rests on observational series and a large but unreviewed mortality cohort.

DMT (including ayahuasca)

Coverage: 1,077 matching lines (\bDMT\b|ayahuasca|dimethyltryptamine) — mostly trial-registry metadata. Human long-term evidence is cross-sectional ayahuasca studies; DMT trials are acute/single-dose.

  • Long-term ritual ayahuasca users. A Spanish study compared 40 ayahuasca-naïve adults before their first use (with 1- and 6-month follow-ups) against 23 long-term users (>30 lifetime uses; mean 70 ceremonies, range 50–100) s7 (c16–c19, c119, c198). After initiation, >80% of those meeting psychiatric diagnostic criteria showed clinical improvements; long-term users had lower depression scores (HAM-D d=0.49, p=0.03), higher self-transcendence and quality of life s7 (c21–c26, c123). Ayahuasca use "has not been associated with increased psychopathology" or "impairments in neuropsychological functioning" s7 (c39–c40); one MRI study found posterior-cingulate cortical thickness inversely correlated with lifetime use s7 (c52). In a prior sample of 380 long-term users, half reduced or eliminated prescription drugs after beginning regular use s7 (c137). Caveats: cross-sectional, self-selected, religious-context users; case reports of ayahuasca-induced psychotic episodes exist s7 (c69); one naïve participant developed GAD (attributed by the subject to a breakup) s7 (c140).
  • Controlled depression trial. The first RCT of ayahuasca in treatment-resistant depression (NCT02914769; 29 completers, single dose): day-7 MADRS 11.58 vs 26.76 placebo; HAM-D response 8/14 vs 3/15; remission 6/14 vs 2/15; no serious adverse events; nausea 10/14 vs 4/15 s8 (c306–c333). Earlier open-label studies (n=6, n=17) showed effects within 2 hours lasting 21 days s8 (c244–c247). Follow-up in the RCT stops at day 7 — no long-term controlled data.
  • DMT pharmaceutical pipeline (all early-phase). Vaporized 5-MeO-DMT (GH001) Phase 1 and a Phase 1/2 TRD trial (Reckweg 2023) s11 (c5566); IV 5-MeO-DMT (GH002) Phase 1 s11; IV DMT fumarate (SPL026) for MDD s11; a DMT dose-response study (NCT05384678) s11; continuous IV infusion extending the DMT state to ~1 hour for fMRI s11 (c8440); vaporized DMT TRD pilots reporting "rapid and sustained" antidepressant effects (Falchi-Carvalho 2025) s11 (c8411); an oral ayahuasca decoction (SM-001) dose-ranging study (0.5/1/2 mL/kg, 12 participants) s11; AtaiBeckley's oral transmucosal DMT film (VLS-01) and intranasal mebufotenin/5-MeO-DMT (BPL-003) s24 (c2608, c2618). Registered ayahuasca-vs-esketamine RCTs in MDD and PTSD exist s9. Parenteral DMT acts <20 minutes s11 (c7152).
  • Long-term DMT (non-ayahuasca) human data: none in the ledger. The 1,077 matching lines are dominated by registry design/eligibility claims; no longitudinal cohort of DMT users appears.

Bottom line: the best long-term human signal is cross-sectional ayahuasca research (better mental-health scores, no neuropsychological harm detected); everything pharmaceutical is acute-phase with short follow-up.

Mescaline

Coverage: 91 matching lines (mescaline); the digest's mescaline group holds 41 claims, read in full. No long-term human outcome study of mescaline exists in the ledger.

  • Modern acute-effects studies (Basel). MDR (NCT04849013, completed 2023-03-27): 16 healthy participants, 6-period crossover, mescaline 100/200/400/800 mg with a ketanserin (5-HT2A blocker) condition; acute psychometrics, physiology and QTc ECGs — a mechanism study, not a treatment trial s10 (c3123–c3149). The registry itself stated that "modern data on the acute effects of mescaline on humans is lacking" s10 (c3129). LPM (NCT04227756, completed 2022-09-02, n=32) compared LSD 100 µg, psilocybin 20 mg and mescaline 300–500 mg within subjects (Ley et al. 2023) s10 (c3064–c3097). Oral mescaline pharmacokinetics published 2025 (Mueller et al.) s10 (c3098).
  • Historical safety. One mescaline-associated fatality case report (Reynolds & Jindrich 1985) is cited in the LSD review's reference list s38 (c5996).
  • Data-quality note. The mescaline registry search also returned a lupus-nephritis trial whose interventions are MPA-type immunosuppressants mislabeled in the registry as "mescaline sodium enteric-coated tablets" / "morte-mescaline (mortifamate)" s10 (c3100, c3118). Those records are not psychedelic-mescaline evidence and are excluded here.

Bottom line: mescaline is the thinnest-evidenced drug in the set — acute pharmacology only; no therapeutic long-term data to verify or cite.

Cross-cutting observations

  1. Evidence asymmetry. Cannabis has 20–27-year population cohorts (lung function, cognition); the repurposed drugs mostly have single- or few-dose trials with weeks-to-months follow-up. The longest human exposures documented: esketamine to 6.5 years (n=1,148, supervised) s39; MDMA mean 45 months (n=19, uncontrolled) s29; psilocybin 4.5 years (small, cancer-distress) s3; ayahuasca decades of ritual use (cross-sectional) s7.
  2. Blinding and expectancy are the field's central methodological problem — 94% treatment-guess accuracy in MAPP2 s14, all-correct guessing in the LSD pilot s34, and explicit statements that double-blinding is virtually impossible with these drugs s38.
  3. Regulatory status (as recorded in the ledger): esketamine approved (EU/US) with multi-year safety data s37s39; psilocybin Phase 3-positive, breakthrough designation s18; MDMA rejected via CRL with a new trial required s28s20; ibogaine/DMT/mescaline in early-phase or RFI stage s35s11s10.
  4. Everything above is unverified extraction. 9,110 open claims, 0 verified; 914 claims lack evidence links; 11 registered sources have no claims; 4 sources failed to fetch; the pooled MDMA LTFU paper is retracted. Treat every number as a lead to check, not a finding.
